Unit 3 · The ground is not finished · ~40 minutes

Plate tectonics

Plates move. Ridges, trenches, and slide-past faults are process, not scenery. Centimeters per year times deep time is an ocean.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Name the three plate-boundary types and one thing each typically does.
  2. Connect a pattern of earthquakes or volcanoes to a boundary without treating the map as a finished picture.
  3. Explain “the ground is not finished” as an evidence claim (rate, belts, living measurements), not a slogan.

Lesson

The ground is not a finished stage set. The outer shell of the Earth — the lithosphere — is broken into plates that move a few centimeters a year. That rate sounds like nothing until you give it deep time. Centimeters per year times millions of years is an ocean, a mountain belt, a rift. Plate tectonics is the 101 name for that process. It is not a slogan. It is a claim with several independent checks: earthquake belts, volcano chains, seafloor age and magnetic stripes, the fit of continents, GPS velocities today.

Alfred Wegener argued that continents had moved. The fit and the fossils were real clues. He did not have a workable mechanism, and many geologists rejected the package. Mid-twentieth-century seafloor data — young crust at ridges, older crust farther away, symmetric magnetic stripes — supplied the mechanism: seafloor spreading, and later the wider plate model. That history is an evidence lesson, not a hero story. A good idea with a missing mechanism is incomplete. New measurements can reopen it. Show the step.

Three boundary types will carry a first-year load.

Divergent. Plates move apart. At mid-ocean ridges, melt rises and new oceanic crust forms. On land, a rift can thin the crust and eventually become an ocean. Earthquakes here are usually shallower. Volcanism is common. The claim is not “the Atlantic is a pretty shape.” The claim is “the seafloor is younger near the ridge and older toward the continents,” which maps have shown.

Convergent. Plates move together. If one plate is oceanic, it can sink — subduction — and you get a trench, a volcanic arc, and a deep earthquake zone. If two buoyant continents meet, subduction is harder and you get a thickened crust and high mountains (the Himalaya as the textbook case). Volcanoes and mountains are not decorations at the edge of a map. They are what happens when plates interact. “The ground is not finished” means those processes are still running.

Transform. Plates slide past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed in the simple cartoon. Earthquakes are the main news. The San Andreas system is the usual example; you do not need a California field trip to use it. A transform claim is about relative motion and strain, not about a state personality.

Hotspots — volcanism that can punch through a plate and leave a chain of ages — sit beside the three-boundary cartoon. They are useful. They are also easy to overclaim. This sample will not make you a plume specialist. If a chain of islands gets older in one direction, that is a pattern a classmate could check. The deep cause is a research argument.

What first-years get wrong: treating earthquakes as random punishment; treating volcanoes as entertainment; treating continents as permanently parked. Earthquakes cluster where strain is stored and released, especially at plate edges (and at some other faults). Intraplate quakes happen; they do not erase the belt pattern. A building code is a policy response to a process claim. This unit grades the process claim.

What can you check without inventing a lab? A public earthquake map (USGS) for a week of events. A world map of trenches and ridges. A sentence about rate: centimeters per year, not “drifting wildly.” A refusal: “I have not measured this plate; I am using the published velocity.” That last sentence is not weakness. It is the incomplete-record habit from Unit 1 applied to a living planet. Practice will ask you to name a boundary, reject a finished-scenery sentence, and write why the ground is not finished as an evidence claim, not a poster.

Finished-scenery talk
The mountains are just there. The map is done.
Process claim
These mountains sit at a convergent boundary; uplift and erosion are both still running.

Practice

Write a plate sentence

Use a real, public pattern: mid-Atlantic ridge, Himalaya, San Andreas, or a USGS earthquake map you can point to. One or two sentences: boundary type (or “I cannot tell from what I have”), plus one check. No invented field trip.

Homework

A page you could hand in. Not a field trip.

Open a public earthquake map (USGS is fine) or a world map of ridges and trenches. Do not invent a campus seismometer. Describe one pattern you can actually see: a belt, a gap, a ridge, a trench. Name a likely boundary type or say you cannot tell. Write what measurement would change your mind. 150–220 words.
