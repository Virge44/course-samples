Unit 5 · Not a forecast app · ~35 minutes

Atmosphere and weather

Weather is the atmosphere’s state now. Climate is the pattern over decades. An icon is not a mechanism.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Separate weather (state now or soon) from climate (pattern over decades).
  2. Name what a weather claim needs: a time window plus temperature, pressure, moisture, wind, or precipitation.
  3. Read a simple situation without treating a forecast-app icon as the explanation.

Lesson

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a time and a place: temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, cloud, precipitation. Climate is the pattern of that weather over a long stretch — decades, not a weekend. If you mix the two, you will treat a cold morning as an argument about the century, or a forecast icon as an explanation. This unit is not a forecast app. An app can be useful. It does not tell you why a front lifts air, or why a clear night can be cold. The habit is the same as Unit 1: a weather claim names variables and a time window a classmate could check.

The atmosphere is a thin skin. Most of the mass that makes weather sits in the troposphere, the lowest layer — on the order of 8 to 18 kilometers, thinner over the poles, thicker in the tropics. Above that, other layers matter for ozone and for some research. For 101 weather, you live in the troposphere. Air has weight. That weight is pressure. Pressure is not a mood, and it is not “the air getting heavy because rain is sad.” It is a measurable force on a unit area. Highs and lows on a map are patterns of that measurement.

Air moves from higher toward lower pressure, then the rotating Earth turns the path. You do not need the full Coriolis derivation in this sample. You need the consequence: winds around highs and lows have a pattern; they do not blow in cartoon straight lines from H to L. Rising air cools; sinking air warms. Rising, cooling air can reach saturation and make cloud and rain. Sinking air often means clearer weather. That is energy and moisture, not an icon of a sun wearing sunglasses.

A front is a boundary between air masses with different temperature and moisture. A cold front can lift warm air quickly; showers and a wind shift are common textbook companions. A warm front lifts more gently; a longer shield of cloud is the usual picture. Real events are messier than the diagram. The diagram is still better than “it feels like rain.” Convection — warm air rising in a plume — builds thunderstorms when the air is unstable and moist. Lightning is a discharge, not a personality. Hail and tornadoes are specific, violent cases. This sample will not make you a storm chaser and will not invent a campus weather station.

Humidity is water vapor, a gas. Relative humidity is how close the air is to saturation at that temperature. Cold air holds less vapor before it saturates; that is why a humid night and a dry winter day are different sentences. Dew and frost are local saturation on a surface. Fog is cloud on the ground. None of these require a spiritual reading. They require a temperature, a moisture, and a check.

What first-years get wrong: treating the phone icon as the mechanism; treating “the weatherman was wrong” as a refutation of atmospheric science (a forecast is a short-range probability, and models have limits); treating one hot afternoon as climate (that is Unit 6). Another error: inventing a field trip to a TV studio or a partner NWS office. Public pages exist. A dated observation from a nearby ASOS or a printed newspaper high and low is enough.

Keep my frame. This is gen-ed instructional design, not a meteorology appointment. Conservative facts: weather versus climate, pressure and moisture, rising air and clouds, fronts as boundaries. Practice will ask you to mark a weather claim, reject an app-as-explanation sentence, and write a short situation with a time window and at least one variable a roommate could look up.

App talk
The icon said sun, so nothing is happening in the air.
Weather claim
Tonight’s forecast low is 38°F under clear sky; radiational cooling can drop the temperature after sunset.

Practice

Write a weather claim

One or two sentences: a time window, a place you can name, and at least one variable (temperature, wind, rain, pressure, cloud). Use a real day this week or a public observation. No invented station.

Homework

A page you could hand in. Not a field trip.

For one real day this week, write 150–220 words: the weather claim (time, place, two variables), what an app icon would have shown, and what the icon does not explain (a front, cooling at night, a wind shift). Use a public observation if you can. No invented campus weather station. No partner TV studio.
