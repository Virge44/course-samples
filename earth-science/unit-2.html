Unit 2 · Not decoration · ~40 minutes

Minerals and rocks as evidence

A mineral has a recipe and a structure. A rock is an aggregate with a formation story. Pretty is not a claim.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Tell a mineral from a rock in one sentence.
  2. Match a short description to igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic and name the condition it records.
  3. Write a formation claim a classmate could check against a hand sample, a photo, or a published map — no invented lab.

Lesson

A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a definite chemical recipe and an ordered crystal structure. Ice in a glacier counts. Glass in a window does not — it has no ordered structure. A rock is not a single mineral with a last name. A rock is an aggregate: minerals (sometimes glass or fossil bits) locked together. Granite is typically quartz, feldspar, and mica. Basalt is a darker, finer package from a melt that cooled faster. Sandstone is mostly sand-sized grains, often quartz, glued by a cement. If you cannot say mineral versus rock in one sentence, you will treat every specimen as a souvenir.

This unit asks you to treat a rock as evidence, not decoration. Evidence of what? Of conditions. Melting, cooling, weathering, transport, burial, heat, and pressure leave different signatures. You do not need a museum. You need three formation stories and the honesty to say when a hand sample cannot finish the story.

Igneous rocks come from melt. If the melt cools slowly underground, crystals have time to grow and you can see them (granite is the usual first-year example). If the melt erupts and cools quickly, crystals stay small or the rock is glassy (basalt, rhyolite, obsidian). Grain size here is a cooling-rate clue, not a beauty score. A vesicular basalt — holes from gas — is a sentence about a lava that lost bubbles near the surface. It is not “a rock with holes in it, how interesting.”

Sedimentary rocks are made of pieces or of material that precipitated. Clastic rocks (conglomerate, sandstone, shale) are grains sorted by water, wind, or ice, then buried and cemented. Grain size and sorting are transport clues: a river can carry sand; a quiet lake or deep water can settle mud. Chemical and biochemical rocks (many limestones, rock salt) record water chemistry and, often, life. A fossil in a limestone is not a mascot. It is a time-and-environment clue, and it is still incomplete: most organisms never fossilize.

Metamorphic rocks are older rocks changed by heat and pressure without fully melting. Slate can come from shale. Marble can come from limestone. Gneiss can show banding from high-grade conditions. Foliation — minerals lined up — is a stress clue. If the rock melted completely, you are back in igneous territory. The boundary is taught as clean in 101; nature is messier. A careful claim says “this looks like a metamorphic fabric in hand sample,” not “I have the full pressure-temperature path.”

The rock cycle is the cheap diagram that tries to hold these stories together: any rock can, given time and the right conditions, become another. The diagram is a map of processes, not a promise that your campus stone has been through every loop. Most first-year mistakes are category errors. Students call a mineral a rock, call a rock “volcanic” because it is dark, or assume every layered rock is sedimentary (some igneous rocks layer too). Dark is not a rock type. Layered is not a complete story. You owe a formation sentence.

What can a classmate check without a lab? Color and grain size, whether grains are interlocking crystals or worn fragments, whether you can see fossils or banding, whether a published geologic map of the area names a unit. Mohs hardness and acid on limestone are useful if you have the tools; this sample will not invent a lab kit or a campus rock garden. If you cannot get to an outcrop, a building stone with a quarry name, or a map, say so. Missing access is a gap in your record, not a reason to invent a field trip.

Keep the author’s frame straight. This is instructional design for a gen-ed, written by someone who taught claim-and-because, not mineralogy. The facts are conservative: three rock families, mineral versus rock, rocks as conditions. Practice will ask you to classify a short description, reject a souvenir sentence, and write a formation claim a roommate could test against a picture or a map.

Mineral or rock?

Practice

Match the formation story

A hand sample is dark, fine-grained, with small holes. No fossils. What is the most careful 101 reading?

Practice

Write a formation claim

Pick one: granite in a kitchen counter (if you can see the crystals), a sandstone step, or a limestone with a visible shell. Write one sentence that names the rock family and the condition it records. Do not write “it looks nice.”

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Find one rock you can actually see this week: a building stone, a gravestone, a gravel grain, a countertop, a river cobble. Do not invent a campus quarry or a class field trip. Name it as mineral or rock. If it is a rock, give the family (or your best 101 guess) and the condition it records. Then write one sentence about what you cannot tell without a thin section or a map. 150–220 words.
