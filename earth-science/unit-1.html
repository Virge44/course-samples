Unit 1 · Deep time, incomplete record · ~35 minutes

What an Earth claim is

A sentence about the planet that a classmate could check. Deep time changes always. The record is incomplete on purpose — geology’s purpose, not a scandal.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Split a short sentence into an Earth claim (checkable) and a vibe or a meaning-claim.
  2. Say why deep time changes what always, never, and recently can mean.
  3. Name one way the rock record is incomplete (erosion, non-deposition, unconformity) and what that does to a claim.

Lesson

An Earth claim is a sentence about the planet that could, in principle, be checked. “This river flooded last year” can be checked against a stream gauge, a dated photograph, a newspaper, or a high-water mark on a bridge. “Nature is amazing” cannot. It is a mood. This course is a first-year gen-ed sample about making the first kind of sentence and refusing the second. It is not a field camp, not a lab sequence, and not a credential in geology.

Earth science has a second problem that campus talk usually does not. The thing you want to check is often already gone. Most of Earth’s history is not sitting in a drawer. Rock is eroded, melted, subducted, or never deposited. Soft tissue almost never fossilizes. A lake that lasted ten thousand years can leave a thin pair of muds. A mountain range can be worn to a plain. The record is incomplete by nature, not by a conspiracy of missing pages. A careful claim names what is present and what cannot be seen.

Deep time is the name for that scale. The Earth is about 4.54 billion years old. That number is not a vibe. It is a measured age from radiometric dating of meteorites and of the oldest surviving minerals, cross-checked by more than one method. You do not need to run those labs in this sample. You need the consequence: a human lifetime is a rounding error. “Always” on a hillside is not a geologic word. A stream terrace that looks permanent to a commuter can be a few thousand years old. A granite that looks fresh in a roadcut can be hundreds of millions of years old. If your claim uses always, never, or recently, you owe a scale.

Three ordinary tests help.

First: can you say what would count as a check? A grain size, a fossil, a mapped contact, a dated layer, a gauge height, a satellite image with a date. If the only check is “it feels true,” you do not have an Earth claim yet. “This hill is made of sandstone” can be checked by looking at the grains — if you can get to an outcrop, a hand sample, or a published map. “This hill has a spiritual energy” may matter to someone. It is not an Earth claim in the sense this course uses.

Second: have you admitted the gap? Superposition — in an undeformed stack of sedimentary layers, the lower bed is older — is a strong rule. It fails if the stack has been flipped, cut, or interrupted by an unconformity: a buried erosion surface that stands for missing time. Cross-cutting is another rule: a dike that cuts a sandstone is younger than the sandstone. Both rules are tools. They do not make the record complete. An honest claim says “this bed is older than that one in this outcrop if the sequence has not been overturned,” not “we know the whole story of the county.”

Third: are you mixing a process claim with a meaning claim? “This valley was cut by a river” is a process claim. “This valley means we should change the zoning map” is a different kind of sentence. Process claims belong in this course. Policy still needs the process sentence underneath, and it is not the work of Unit 1.

Why start here instead of with pretty rocks? Because students who skip the claim test treat the rest of the course as a catalog. They memorize igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic and cannot say what would change their mind. The public habit in this sample is the same habit a large-enrollment reasoning course uses: say why, show the step, leave something a classmate can check. The content facts here are conservative textbook 101. Leave with a checkable sentence.

You will hear people say the Earth is “finished” — mountains as backdrop, weather as an app, climate as a mood. The next units treat the planet as process. This unit only asks you to stop using always without a clock, and to stop treating a missing layer as a personal insult. Incomplete is the working condition. Practice here is not a quiz you pass once. You will mark a claim that can be checked, name a hole in the record, and write one Earth sentence that survives a roommate who was not on the hill with you.

Earth claim
The lower sandstone bed in this undeformed roadcut is older than the shale above it.
Check
Superposition, plus a look for folding or overturning. A classmate can ask: was the stack flipped?
Not yet a claim
This landscape has always been here. It feels ancient.
Why it fails
Always has no scale. Feeling is not a check. How old is the rock? How old is the hill’s shape?

Practice

Which sentence is an Earth claim?

Pick the sentence a classmate could actually check.

Practice

Name the hole in the record

A roadcut shows sandstone, then a wavy rusty surface, then limestone. No fossils of the animals you expected between them. What is the most careful reading?

Practice

Write one checkable Earth sentence

Do not write “nature is amazing.” Write one sentence about a hill, a river, a rock, or a weather day that a roommate could check — a scale, a map, a date, a gauge, a layer. If you do not have a place yet, use this public-style fact: a USGS topographic map labels a creek and a 40-foot contour on a hill you can name.

Homework

A page you could hand in. Not a field trip.

Find one sentence in ordinary talk this week that sounds like earth science — a hill, a flood, “always been this hot,” a roadcut, a weather complaint. Do not invent a story. Copy or paraphrase the sentence. Mark it: Earth claim, vibe, or meaning-claim. If it is a claim, write the check a classmate could run. If it is not, rewrite it as a checkable Earth sentence, and name one gap in the record you still would not have. 150–220 words. No invented field trip.
