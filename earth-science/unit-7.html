Unit 7 · Where stuff comes from · ~35 minutes

Earth resources and waste

An object has a source and a rate. Waste is still matter. Caring is allowed. A slogan is not a budget.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Trace one everyday object back to a rock, well, forest, or field — no invented mine tour.
  2. Distinguish a renewable rate from “we can use it forever.”
  3. Name where one waste stream goes (landfill, atmosphere, water, reuse) as an Earth claim, not a scold.

Lesson

Everything you are holding came from the Earth. The phone, the desk, the food, the asphalt, the drywall. Ore, petroleum, sand and gravel, timber, soil, groundwater. This unit is about stocks and flows, not a scold and not a shopping ban. A resource claim names a source and a rate. A waste claim names where the leftover matter goes — landfill, tailings, atmosphere, water, a reuse stream. “We should care more” is not yet either claim.

A nonrenewable resource, in 101 language, is one whose replacement rate is geologic. Coal, oil, gas, most metal ores: the Earth still makes them, on timescales that do not help a human budget. A renewable resource replaces on a human or near-human timescale if you do not take it faster than it returns: timber, some groundwater, soil in principle, sunlight as an energy flux. The trap is the word renewable used as “forever, no matter the rate.” A forest cut faster than it grows is a stock being mined. An aquifer pumped faster than it recharges is a stock being mined. Rate is the claim.

Where stuff comes from is often ugly in a quiet way. A copper wire began as a sulfide or oxide ore, blasted, crushed, concentrated, smelted. A concrete sidewalk is crushed rock plus cement; cement is cooked limestone and other materials, energy-heavy. A plastic bottle is a hydrocarbon chain from oil or gas. Phosphorus on a farm came from a mine. Sand for glass and for concrete is a rock product people forget to count. You do not need a mine tour this sample will not invent. You need to pick one object you already own and refuse to treat it as having appeared in a store by magic.

Waste is still matter. It does not vanish when the truck leaves. Landfills bury a mix; they leak if they are poorly built; they make methane as organics decay. Incineration moves much of the mass to the atmosphere and to ash. Mine tailings are crushed rock with leftover chemistry. Combustion of coal and gasoline puts carbon dioxide and other gases into the air — a Unit 6 measurement sitting on a Unit 7 flow. Nutrient runoff from fields is a water claim from Unit 4. Recycling is a real path for some materials (aluminum is the usual good example) and a thin path for others. “I recycled it” is not a complete Earth sentence if you cannot say whether that stream actually remakes the object.

Reserves versus resources: a conservative distinction. A resource is what is there. A reserve is what is economic to extract with current prices and technology. Those numbers move. They are not a conspiracy when they move. They are a budget that depends on price, law, and engineering. Peak-everything slogans and infinite-substitute slogans both skip the budget. This 101 wants the budget.

Energy is a flow sitting on those stocks. A coal plant and a gas plant burn geologic carbon and send waste to the air. A hydroelectric dam is a water-and-gravity machine with a drowned valley behind it. A wind turbine is steel, copper, concrete, and a wind resource; it is not “nothing from the ground.” None of those sentences is a vote. They are source-and-waste sentences. If you skip them, you will treat electricity as a mood that comes from a wall socket.

What first-years get wrong: treating “natural” as automatically kind; treating “chemical” as automatically evil (rocks are chemistry); inventing a partner landfill or a class mine visit; writing a sermon about other people’s consumption while skipping the object in their bag. The author is not a mining engineer. He is asking for the same public habit as the rest of the sample: source, rate, destination, a check. Practice will ask you to trace an object, mark a rate claim, and write where one waste stream goes without a scold.

Rate, not forever

A town calls its groundwater “renewable, so we can pump without a budget.” What is the careful reply?

Practice

Trace one object

Name something you actually have (pen, can, shirt, phone, paper). Two or three short lines: likely Earth source, one process, likely waste path. No invented mine tour.

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Pick one object you actually own. Write 150–220 words: likely Earth source, one process between ground and hand, likely waste path, and one number or rate you do not have (and would need). Do not invent a class visit to a landfill or a mine. Do not scold a stranger. Do not claim you assayed the ore.
