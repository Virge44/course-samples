Unit 4 · The cheap-looking miracle · ~40 minutes

Water: rivers, groundwater

Liquid water does work. Rivers cut and carry. Groundwater lives in pores, not a secret lake. “Important” is not a claim.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Trace a drop through the water cycle as a budget (store, path), not a cartoon.
  2. Distinguish a river’s work (erosion, transport, deposition, floodplain) from “water is important.”
  3. Write one groundwater claim a classmate could check (water table, well, permeability) without inventing a campus well.

Lesson

Liquid water at Earth’s surface is easy to take for granted. Most of the solar system does not offer it as an everyday liquid. That is the cheap-looking miracle: a temperature range, a pressure, and a supply that lets water evaporate, condense, fall, run, soak, and freeze on a living planet. This unit will not sermonize about “importance.” Importance is a mood. A river claim is about work: erosion, transport, deposition. A groundwater claim is about pores, a water table, and a path.

The water cycle in 101 is a budget, not a cartoon cloud with arrows for children. Water evaporates from oceans and land, transpires from plants, condenses into clouds, precipitates, then runs off or infiltrates. Some is stored in ice, lakes, soil, and rock. The stores are unequal. Oceans hold most of the water. Fresh surface water is a thin slice. Groundwater is a larger fresh store than rivers, and it is mostly not an underground lake. It lives in pore spaces and fractures. If you picture a hidden river under every town, you will misread wells and contamination.

A river does work. It picks up sediment, carries it, and drops it when the water slows. Steep channels cut. Broad floodplains receive mud. A meander can migrate. A flood is not a moral failure of the river. It is the river using a part of its valley it does not use every week. People build on floodplains because the land is flat and the soil is good. That is a land-use claim sitting on a process claim. This sample will not invent a local flood tour. It will ask you to keep the process sentence visible: water, slope, sediment, time.

Discharge is the volume of water passing a point in a time. A drainage basin (watershed) is the land that sheds water to a stream. A divide separates basins. These are checkable ideas. You can, with a topographic map, sketch where a raindrop would run. You cannot, from a motivational poster, say where it would run. Grain size on a bar or a point — cobbles versus sand versus mud — is a transport clue, the same habit as Unit 2, now in motion.

Groundwater needs three words used carefully. Porosity is empty space. Permeability is whether that space is connected enough for water to move. A clay can be porous and still tight. An aquifer is a body of rock or sediment that can yield useful water. The water table is the top of the saturated zone; it rises and falls. A well that “never fails” is a claim that needs a dry year. Contamination does not teleport. It follows flow, slowly or quickly depending on the material. A spill on a permeable sand is a different problem than a spill on a tight clay. That is an Earth claim, not a scold.

Ice and coasts belong in a longer course. Here, one conservative sentence each: glaciers are moving ice that carve and deposit; sea level is not a mood, it is a measured height with more than one cause (ice volume, temperature of the water, local land motion). Do not inflate that into a Unit 6 fight. Keep it as a measurement habit.

What first-years get wrong: “water is life” as a complete answer; groundwater as a lake; floods as freak exceptions instead of part of the river’s job; inventing a class canoe trip. This folder will not supply a partner river or a campus well. If you want a place, use a creek you can stand beside, a tap you can name, or a public hydrograph. Practice will ask you to reject the importance slogan, place a contamination path, and write a water sentence with a path or a table in it.

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Pick a real water you can point to: a named creek, a tap, a storm drain, a public stream-gauge page, a puddle path after rain. Trace it in 150–220 words: where it comes from, where it goes, what work it does or what store it sits in. Name one thing you could not see (the water table, the next basin, the flood of record). No invented field trip. No partner river.
