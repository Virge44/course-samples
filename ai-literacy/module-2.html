Module 2 · Ethics · async · ~25 minutes

Integrity Without Panic

Most rooms collapse to ban it or pretend it is not there. You leave with a spectrum you can publish, and one sentence students can actually use.

3 objectives Spectrum · rewrite · case 1 homework

You leave able to

  1. Place common AI uses on an integrity spectrum (prohibited / disclose / required tool / redesign).
  2. Separate authorship, privacy, bias, and labor so every issue is not “cheating.”
  3. Rewrite foggy student-facing policy into language that names what is allowed, what needs disclosure, and what happens next.

Lesson

Publish a spectrum

Ban it, or pretend it is not in the room. Both waste a year. Students already have the tools. A binary rule teaches them to hide the work.

Say which zone a task sits in. Then teach that.

Prohibited
Submit AI text as your own graded essay with no process evidence.
Disclose
AI for brainstorming or outline only, with disclosure and teacher-visible process.
Required tool
Use AI to generate counterarguments you must critique. The critique is graded.
Redesign
Change the task so the product alone is weak evidence — conferences, drafts, oral defense, in-class writing.

You do not have to live in all four zones. You do have to say which zone a task sits in. “AI is cheating” does not tell a tenth grader whether an outline is allowed. “Students shall not employ generative artificial intelligence in any capacity whatsoever” does not tell a first-year what to do when the library chatbot is already in the search bar.

K–12: guardians, developmental fairness, mixed access at home, a school policy that may still be a paragraph. Higher ed: syllabus as contract, an integrity office, adult learners, program norms. Same spectrum. Different paperwork.

Four problems. Do not mash them.

If every hard moment is “cheating,” you will punish the wrong thing and miss the right one.

Authorship
Who did the thinking? Process evidence matters — drafts, notes, a conference, an oral walk-through.
Privacy
Student data, unpublished keys, IEP or health notes. That is Module 1’s red line, still in force.
Bias / harm
Stereotypes, erased groups, a “fun dialect” skit, a fabricated quote from a real activist.
Labor / power
Who benefits from unpaid student AI labor? Who cannot afford the tool you just required? Who is locked to a vendor?

A worksheet that invents a historical quote is not a student-cheating case. It is fabricated authority in materials you assigned. A student who used AI for three thesis options, picked one, wrote the essay, and disclosed is not automatically dishonest. A student who cannot run the required tool at home is an equity case before it is an integrity case.

Rewrite the fog

Students should be able to answer three questions after reading your line: What may I do? What must I say I did? What happens if you are unsure I did the thinking?

Fog sounds official and does none of that. Plain language does. Name allowed use. Name disclosure. Name the ban (usually: the tool may not write the graded work). Name the privacy line. Say that authorship questions start with a conversation — then consequence if the rule was clearly broken.

You are not writing campus counsel’s policy. You are writing the sentence students will actually read. Link the official policy under it if you have one.

Process conference first

Detectors are weak evidence alone. Voice-change overnight is a signal, not a verdict. The first move on a borderline case is private and procedural: compare to prior work, ask them to walk the process, ask for drafts or a short oral defense, then decide redo versus consequence with the published rule in the room.

An automatic zero with no conversation teaches them to hide it. So does public shame. A private conference is the first move: walk the process, then decide.

Homework

Rewrite your syllabus or classroom AI line in plain language this week. Students should know what is allowed, what needs disclosure, what is not allowed, and that authorship questions start with a talk. If your school already has official language, write the student-facing version you will actually say. Paste it into an LMS journal, or keep it in the syllabus draft you will use.

Homework: Rewrite the AI line for one class you teach. Include: allowed / disclose / not allowed / privacy / what happens if authorship is unclear. Do not paste student work into a tool to do this.
