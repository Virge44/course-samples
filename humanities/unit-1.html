Unit 1 · The question type · ~35 minutes

What a humanities question is

Not “what happened.” Not “what is the theme.” What does this made thing make of a living person?

3 objectives Sort + rewrite 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish an event question (“what happened”) and a tourist question (“theme,” “I feel”) from a humanities question.
  2. Name the three parts a working humanities question needs: a made thing, a doing, and a living person who could be changed by seeing that doing.
  3. Rewrite a tourist plaque into a question a classmate could refuse by pointing at the same lines.

Lesson

Three kinds of question

A history question asks what happened. A tourist question asks what to feel, or what the theme is so you can leave. A humanities question asks what a made thing makes of a living person.

If you ask “What happened when Odysseus went to the underworld?” you can get a plot card: he digs a trench, pours blood, the dead come. That is not nothing. You should be able to say it. It is also not the work of this course. The work starts when you stay with a made choice the poem did not have to make.

In the Nekyia — the underworld book of the Odyssey — the first shade who speaks is not a king and not the prophet Odysseus came for. It is Elpenor, a crewman left unburied on Circe’s roof. He died drunk, fell, broke his neck, and the hero sailed. The poem could have opened the underworld with Tiresias, the official reason for the trip. It does not. It makes you meet the man the voyage forgot.

Teaching retelling · Odyssey 11

When the blood was in the trench the dead came crowding. The first to speak was not the prophet. It was Elpenor, whose body we had left behind, unwept, unburied, on Circe’s house, because another labor had already called us to the ship. He said: do not leave me unmourned when you go back to the daylight. Burn me with my armor. Heap a mound on the shore of the gray sea, and plant my oar on top of it, the oar I rowed with while I lived, so that people yet to come will know a man was here. I promised. Only then did the other dead come forward to drink.

Teaching retelling of a beat in Odyssey 11, written for this sample. Not a published translation. Not student work. The Greek poem is ancient; this English is a classroom object.

The question the retelling will not ask for you

A humanities question is not “Who is Elpenor?” That is a flashcard. It is: what does it make of a living person that the first dead voice is the one the living failed? What kind of “we” does a poem build when it will not let the hero’s errand start until the forgotten man is heard? Those questions can be wrong. A classmate can say the poem is doing something else — that Elpenor is comic relief, or a ritual checklist, or a warning about drink. Good. Now you have a course, not a souvenir.

You will be tempted to answer with a theme. “This is about guilt.” “This is about leadership.” “This is about the human condition.” Themes are tourist plaques. They let you leave the object. A humanities answer has to be able to point at a line and say: here, this is doing the making. In the retelling above, the oar on the mound is not decoration. It is a demand that the living world keep a record of a man the voyage treated as leftover labor. If your paper cannot get back to the oar, you have not asked a humanities question. You have asked for a grade.

A public habit, not a museum mood

The habit of the course is small and public. Look hard at a made thing. Say what it is doing — not what it is “about,” not how it makes you feel in a private way, not the Wikipedia plot. Then say why that doing matters to a person who is still alive: a reader, a citizen, someone who has been left on a roof in some other form. If you cannot name the living person, you are still in the gift shop.

This is not art appreciation. Appreciation is a mood. It asks you to like, or to perform liking. Humanities is a public habit. You can dislike a work and still do the work. You can love a work and still be lying about it. The test is whether another person could refuse your account by pointing at the same lines.

It is also not a Western-civ name dump. You will meet Homer in here because the author taught that poem-world for years and because the Elpenor beat is short enough to hold. You will meet Douglass, Dunbar, and Equiano because their sentences are public domain and because they refuse the gift-shop version of “us.” You will not collect names to look educated. A name without a question is a souvenir.

The three false questions

They show up in first-year papers on schedule:

  1. The event question: What happened? Useful as a check. Fatal as a thesis.
  2. The vibe question: How does this make me feel? Your feeling is data, not an argument. The work is doing something whether you felt it or not.
  3. The plaque question: What is the theme? Theme is what you say when you are already walking toward the exit.

A working humanities question has a made thing in it, a doing verb, and a living person who could be changed by seeing that doing. “What does the oar on Elpenor’s mound make of a student whose labor gets used and then forgotten when the group project ships?” That can be wrong. A classmate can say the retelling is not doing that. The disagreement is the point. “What is the Odyssey about?” cannot be wrong, which is why it is useless.

You will practice converting tourist questions into humanities questions. The page can catch “what happened” and “theme” and “I feel” and “human condition.” It cannot catch a pretty sentence that still refuses the object. That part is yours. Stay with the thing. Ask what it makes of us — “us” meaning someone who can be named, not a cloud called humanity.

Practice

Which question is the humanities question?

Same object: the Elpenor retelling. Pick the question that names a doing and could be refused by pointing at the lines.

Practice

Rewrite a tourist plaque

Tourist version: “The Odyssey is a timeless story about the human condition and the importance of home.”

Rewrite it as one humanities question. Name the made thing (a line, an object, a beat — not “the whole epic”). Name a doing. Name a living person who could be changed by seeing that doing. Avoid “theme,” “timeless,” “human condition,” “I feel,” and “what happened.”

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words.

Take two questions you have actually heard — in a class, on a plaque, in a group chat, or in your own first draft. One should be an event question or a tourist plaque. Rewrite both as humanities questions. Then write one paragraph: which of your rewrites could a classmate refuse by pointing at a line, and which one is still a souvenir? Do not use “human condition,” “timeless,” or “it matters.” Name the made thing. Name the doing. Name the living person.
