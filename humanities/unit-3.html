Unit 3 · Enough history to hear · ~35 minutes

Context without drowning

Use just enough history to hear the work. A textbook chapter that replaces the sentence is not context. It is a different assignment.

3 objectives Keep / cut 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Tell a hearing-aid note (context that lets you hear a sentence) from a textbook dump that replaces the work.
  2. Choose one piece of context you would keep for a public-domain sentence, and name what it lets you hear.
  3. Cut a context paragraph that is true and still useless — because it never returns to the line.

Lesson

Context is a hearing aid

Without some history, you will mishear. With too much, you will never get back to the sentence. The job of context in this course is not to prove you studied. It is to let a living person hear what a line is doing.

Read this. Three sentences. 1845. Public domain. Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave, chapter 7:

Public-domain excerpt · Douglass 1845

I would at times feel that learning to read had been a curse rather than a blessing. It had given me a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy. It opened my eyes to the horrible pit, but to no ladder upon which to get out.

Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life (Boston, 1845), ch. 7. Excerpt under 80 words. Do not treat this page as a scholarly edition.

If you know nothing, you can still hear a paradox: reading as curse. You can still do Unit 2 work: stay with “no ladder.” But you will miss a fact the sentence is spending. In the Maryland of Douglass’s youth, teaching an enslaved person to read was not a self-help project. It was a violation of the order that kept him property. Hugh Auld says, in the same chapter, that a slave who reads becomes “unfit” — the word is Auld’s logic, not a compliment. Douglass learns anyway, often from white children in the street, trading bread for letters. The curse he names is not “school is hard.” It is knowledge of a pit with the exit still owned by someone else.

That is enough context to hear the sentence. You do not need a chapter on the Mexican-American War, a biography of Garrison, or a timeline of every state statute on literacy. Those things are true. They will drown the pit and the ladder. A first-year paper that opens with two pages of “the 1840s were a time of…” has not begun. It has postponed looking.

Too little, too much, enough

Too little: “This is a slave narrative, and slave narratives are about freedom.” Genre wallpaper. You have named a shelf. You have not heard “curse rather than a blessing.” Plenty of freedom-talk would have been safer on the 1845 market. Douglass spends the safer word and then retracts it. The retraction is the doing. Wallpaper cannot hear a retraction.

Too much: a paragraph on the cotton gin, the Missouri Compromise, the author’s later career as a diplomat, and a general claim about “the importance of literacy in American history.” A reader of your paper could write that paragraph without Douglass’s three sentences. If the context would survive the deletion of the quotation, it is not serving the quotation. It is a different paper wearing the quotation as a badge.

Enough: one or two facts a reader needs in order not to flatten the line, followed by a return. “Auld’s household treated literacy as a threat to property. Douglass calls the thing he fought for a curse because it gave him the pit without the ladder.” The second sentence is already interpretation. Context earned it. That is the ratio you want: a little hearing aid, then the work.

The same test on two other objects

Elpenor. Enough context: in this poem-world, an unburied body is not only sad; it is a ritual failure the living still owe. The oar-mound is a public mark, not a private feeling. Too much: a lecture on Bronze Age burial, Linear B, and whether Homer was one person. Interesting in a different course. Fatal here if you never get back to the promise in the trench.

Dunbar, 1896 — you will need him in Unit 5. Enough: he is writing after Reconstruction’s collapse, as a Black poet whose public “dialect” poems were easier for white editors to buy than his standard-English lyrics. “We Wear the Mask” is standard English on purpose. Too much: a full biography, every magazine that printed him, a unit on minstrelsy that never returns to “grins and lies.” The mask line will not be heard if you replace it with a career.

A rule you can carry: context is what a stranger needs in order not to mishear this sentence. Everything else is a chapter you did not assign yourself. If you are unsure, write the context, then delete the quotation in your head. If the paragraph still sounds complete, you drowned. Cut until the sentence is the reason the paragraph exists.

You will not become a historian in this unit. You will become someone who can use a historian’s fact without surrendering the object. That is a first-year skill and an adult one. People drown works in context because they are afraid to claim. Context looks responsible. A claim looks like you could be wrong. Unit 4 will make you be wrong on purpose. This unit makes sure you can still hear the line when you do.

The practice asks you to keep one note and cut another. Both notes can be factually fine. The test is hearing. Homework: one note you would keep for Douglass or Elpenor, one you would cut, and why the kept one returns you to a word in the text. If your “why” does not contain a word from the passage, you are still drowning.

Practice

Which note helps you hear Douglass?

You are writing about the three sentences above. Pick the context note that functions as a hearing aid. The others may be true. They do not get you back to “curse,” “pit,” or “ladder.”

Practice

Keep or cut?

Check every note you would keep for a short paper on Elpenor’s oar. Leave textbook-dump notes unchecked. Then check your set.

Homework

180–250 words.

Pick Douglass’s three sentences or the Elpenor retelling. Write one context note you would keep (two to four sentences). Write one context note you would cut (it can be true). Then write why: the kept note must send you back to a word in the passage. Quote that word. If your “why” has no word from the passage, rewrite it. Do not open with “the 1800s were a time of great change.”
