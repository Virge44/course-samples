Unit 6 · Value, power, who is left out · ~40 minutes

What’s at stake

A reading that sounds complete is often a reading that left someone off the mound. Name the value, the power, and the person your account forgot.

3 objectives Who is left out 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Name what a reading values — who it treats as the center, whose pain it treats as scenery.
  2. Point to a person or position a complete-sounding account leaves out, using a line from the work.
  3. Rewrite a heroic or tourist sentence so the cost is visible to a living reader, without a sermon about “society.”

Lesson

Stakes are not a sermon

What’s at stake is not “the human condition.” That phrase is how a paper raises its voice after it has run out of object. Stakes in this course are local and named. A reading of a work always spends someone’s value. It treats some figure as the one who counts, some labor as leftover, some “we” as obvious. Your job is to say who is centered, who pays, and who is missing — then to decide whether your claim can stand once that missing person is in the room.

This is not a hunt for villains in every stanza. It is a refusal to let a smooth reading pass as complete. Smooth is a power. Plaques are smooth. Hero-stories are smooth. “Relatable” is smooth. If your paragraph could be printed on a poster next to the work, you have not named a stake. You have named a mood the institution already likes.

Who gets the “we”

Dunbar: “We wear the mask.” Who is we? A tourist reading says “everyone hides.” That “everyone” is a theft. It takes a stanza that makes a grin into a debt paid in public — “torn and bleeding hearts we smile” — and sells it back as general manners. The cost of that reading is a living person for whom the grin is not optional and the audience is not symmetrical. You do not need a lecture to say that. You need the word “debt” and a refusal to let “we” float free.

A careful claim can still leave someone out. If you stop at a racial “we,” you may miss the other pressure: the mask is talk, “myriad subtleties,” a linguistic payment. Stakes include the people your own precision forgot.

Who is leftover labor

Elpenor is the course’s plainest leftover. A heroic reading says: Odysseus is a complicated leader who learns to honor his men. That sentence makes Elpenor a lesson-prop for the hero’s growth. The retelling does something ruder. It stops the official errand. The prophet waits. The unburied man sets the terms: fire, mound, oar, a record “so that people yet to come will know a man was here.” If your paper is mainly about Odysseus becoming better, you have left Elpenor in the role the voyage left him — useful to the story of someone else.

Ask the living question. Who, in a group you actually belong to, is the person whose name is used until the project ships and then dropped? A campus reading group. A family. A shift. You are not writing a diary (Unit 4). You are testing whether your account of the oar can survive contact with a leftover person who is not ancient and not fictional. If it cannot, the heroic sentence was flattery.

Who is looked at, who looks

Equiano looks at chained people and a boiling copper, then faints. A tourist sentence: “He shows the cruelty of the slave trade.” The stake is in the look. He is looking at people he is about to be classed with, and the look ends his doubt about his own fate. A reading that treats those faces as background horror for Equiano’s later career has done to them what a ship’s inventory does: counted them as the scene. Douglass’s pit-without-ladder has a similar risk. If you use the three sentences only to praise literacy campaigns, you have left the man in the pit as a before-picture.

Receipt looks quieter, which is why students skip the stake. A tourist stake: “modern isolation.” A humanities stake: whose weather does the bus keep, and who is not faced? If you shrug about plans, you have sided with the weather. Name the woman whose last number could not refuse, and the boy whose verse could not be sounded — even if the poem will not give them names.

Rewrite so the cost is visible

Take a complete-sounding sentence. Ask three questions: What does it value? Who has the power to look, speak, or sail? Who would have to enter the sentence to make it less smooth? Then rewrite. Keep the object. Do not replace the rewrite with “society must do better.” Society is a cloud. A leftover crewman, a paid grin, a face in Equiano’s look, a woman with a receipt — those are visible. Unit 7 will ask you to write an essay that can carry this without becoming a sermon. This unit asks you to practice the rewrite while it is still one sentence.

Power is who gets to leave the roof, who gets the first-person, who gets to say “we,” who never turns around. Value is what the reading treats as the point: the hero’s education, the reader’s feeling, the poster. If you cannot name a left-out person from the lines, you are decorating a claim with seriousness. Practice the rewrite in one sentence. Homework: one left-out person, one rewritten sentence, a short cost for a living reader. No “society.” If you cannot point at a line, you have a vibe with a moral.

Practice

Who does this sentence leave out?

Heroic version: “The underworld scene shows Odysseus growing into a leader who honors his men.” Who is left out of that smoothness?

Practice

Rewrite so the cost is visible

Tourist / heroic version: “We Wear the Mask reminds us that everyone hides their pain, and we should be kinder.”

Rewrite it as one or two sentences. Keep Dunbar’s doing. Name who the “everyone” reading leaves out. Avoid “everyone,” “reminds us,” “be kinder,” “society,” and “human condition.”

Homework

200–300 words.

Pick one course text. Quote a complete-sounding sentence you might have written last month (invent it if you need to — label it invented). Name who it leaves out, with a word from the text. Rewrite the sentence so the cost is visible to a living person you can name by role, not by “society.” Then write four to six sentences: what changes in the room if that person is allowed to count. No sermon. No “be kinder.”
