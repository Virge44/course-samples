Who it is for

First-year college students, mixed preparation, often underprepared for the kind of looking college actually grades. You do not need to love “the arts.” You need to stay with one made thing long enough to say what it is doing — and to risk a claim a classmate could refuse.

If you already write fluent theme papers (“this work is about the human journey”), this course will still slow you down. A theme is a tourist plaque. If you freeze at poems and old books, start with the Elpenor retelling in Unit 1 and the bus poem in Unit 2. The sequence is the course; the units are short enough to finish tired.

What humanities is, here

Humanities is a public habit, not a museum mood. A history question asks what happened. A tourist question asks what to feel, or what the theme is so you can leave. A humanities question asks what a made thing makes of a living person — a reader, a citizen, someone who has been left on a roof in some other form.

Look hard at a made thing. Say what it is doing. Say why that matters to someone still alive. If nobody could disagree by pointing at the same lines, you filled a page.

Course outcomes

By the end of the eight units, you should be able to:

  1. Rewrite an event question or a tourist plaque into a humanities question that names a made thing, a doing, and a living person.
  2. Stay with one passage long enough to mark the line that turns it — and say what that line is doing.
  3. Use just enough context to hear a work, without substituting a textbook chapter for the object.
  4. Write a claim about a work that can be wrong, and put it in conversation with another voice without making a quote dump.
  5. Name what is at stake: value, power, and who is left out of a reading that sounds complete.
  6. Write a short interpretive essay, then close a portfolio with one work, one claim, and one public sentence you would say outside class.

Texts you will actually meet

Short on purpose. Public domain or invented teaching texts. No copyrighted criticism, no recent poems, no long chapters.

  • A teaching retelling of the Elpenor beat from Odyssey 11 (the Nekyia) — labeled as a retelling, not a published translation.
  • Three sentences from Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life (1845), on literacy as a curse.
  • The opening of Paul Laurence Dunbar, “We Wear the Mask” (1896).
  • A few sentences from Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative (1789).
  • Receipt, an original 12-line teaching poem written for this sample and attributed as such.

If an instructor adds other readings, they must be works you can legally quote in class. Do not paste a recent lyric or a critic’s paragraph into these files.

Sequence

Semester-shaped, compressed. Do the units in order. Each one is a lesson you can finish in one sitting, a clickable practice with feedback, and one homework you could actually turn in. Total student time if you do the practices: about four to six hours. This is not fifteen weeks of anthology coverage.

How the units work

Each unit has three measurable objectives, lesson prose a tired first-year can finish, clickable practice that talks back, and one homework of 150–400 words (Unit 7 is the longer essay). Practice is not a quiz for a score. It is a move you will need on the homework.

If an instructor is running this async or hybrid, turn homework in where they say — an LMS journal, a shared folder, paper. If you are working alone, write it anyway. The course does not store it.
