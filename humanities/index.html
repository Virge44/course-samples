Humanities 101

Start with one line you can point at on the page. Stay there long enough to say what it is doing. Close looking, not a theme tour.

Self-paced No login No server Public-domain texts

I taught Humanities I & II in University Studies at NC A&T. The objects here are short on purpose: a teaching retelling of Elpenor from Odyssey 11, a few sentences of Douglass, Dunbar, and Equiano, and one twelve-line poem I wrote for the sample. Public domain, or labeled as a classroom object.

A history question asks what happened. Useful as a check. The work here is different: what does this made thing make of a living person? If a classmate could not disagree by pointing at the same lines, you filled a page.

Texts you will actually meet

  • A teaching retelling of the Elpenor beat from Odyssey 11 (the Nekyia) — labeled as a retelling, not a published translation.
  • Three sentences from Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life (1845), on literacy as a curse.
  • The opening of Paul Laurence Dunbar, “We Wear the Mask” (1896).
  • A few sentences from Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative (1789).
  • Receipt, an original 12-line teaching poem written for this sample and attributed as such.

If an instructor adds other readings, they have to be works you can legally quote in class. Do not paste a recent lyric or a critic’s paragraph into these files.

If you finish the eight units

  1. Rewrite an event question or a theme sentence into a question that names a made thing, a doing, and a living person.
  2. Stay with one passage long enough to mark the line that turns it — and say what that line is doing.
  3. Use just enough context to hear a work, without substituting a textbook chapter for the object.
  4. Write a claim about a work that can be wrong, and put it next to another voice without making a quote dump.
  5. Name what is at stake: value, power, and who is left out of a reading that sounds complete.
  6. Write a short interpretive essay, then close with one work, one claim, and one sentence you would say outside class.

Sequence

Do the units in order. Each sitting is a lesson, a practice in the page, and one piece you could turn in. Four to six hours if you do the practices. Not fifteen weeks of anthology coverage.

1. What a humanities question is
Not “what happened”		 ~35 min Tell an event question from a humanities question, and rewrite a theme sentence so it names a doing and a living person.
2. Close looking / close reading
Stay with one passage		 ~40 min Mark the line that turns a short poem or retelling, and say what that line is doing — not what the work is “about.”
3. Context without drowning
Enough history to hear		 ~35 min Choose the context note that helps you hear a sentence, and reject the textbook dump that replaces the work.
4. A claim about a work
Something that can be wrong		 ~40 min Pick the real claim over a theme sentence, and rewrite so a classmate could disagree by pointing at the same lines.
5. Other voices
Conversation, not a dump		 ~35 min Put two public-domain voices in pressure, not in a stack of quotes that all “support” you.
6. What’s at stake
Value, power, who is left out		 ~40 min Name who a complete-sounding reading leaves out, and rewrite a heroic account so the cost is visible.
7. A short interpretive essay
You write one		 ~50 min Draft 500–700 words: a claim, stayed-with evidence, one other voice, and a stake that is not “the human condition.”
8. Portfolio close
One work, one claim, one public sentence		 ~35 min Leave with a work you would stand behind, a claim that can be wrong, and one sentence you could say outside class.
