Unit 4 · Something that can be wrong · ~40 minutes

A claim about a work

A claim about a work is a sentence a classmate could disagree with by pointing at the same lines.

3 objectives Choose + rewrite 1 homework

When you finish

  1. Tell a real claim about a work from a theme sentence (theme, feeling, plot, “I relate”).
  2. Choose the stronger of two claims by asking which one can be wrong.
  3. Rewrite a theme sentence into a claim a classmate could start with “No, because…”

Lesson

Could a classmate disagree?

Unit 1 gave you a question. This unit asks you to risk an answer. A claim about a work says what the work is doing — not what happened in it, not how it made you feel, not why it is important. The disagreement has to be about the object. “No, because the last couplet is doing the turning, not the receipt” is a humanities refusal. “No, because I didn’t feel that” is not.

Dead claims have empty phrases you already know. “This poem is about the human journey.” “Douglass shows the importance of education.” “I can relate to wearing a mask.” Importance cannot be wrong. Relatability cannot be wrong. That is why first-year papers love them. This course wants a sentence you would have to revise if the lines went the other way.

Narrow until the object can break it

Large claims fail because no line can touch them. “Dunbar’s poem is about race in America.” True in a slack way, and useless. Narrow until a couplet could hurt you. “Dunbar’s opening makes the smile a debt — ‘this debt we pay to human guile’ — so the mask is not a costume a person puts on for fun; it is a payment extracted in public.” Now a classmate can say no: the smile is strategy, not debt; “we” is not paying anyone. Good. You have a fight small enough to finish.

Here is the opening you will need. Public domain. 1896.

Public-domain excerpt · Dunbar 1896

We wear the mask that grins and lies,
It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,—
This debt we pay to human guile;
With torn and bleeding hearts we smile,
And mouth with myriad subtleties.

Paul Laurence Dunbar, “We Wear the Mask,” Lyrics of Lowly Life (1896). Opening stanza. Do not paste later copyrighted commentary.

A theme sentence: “People hide their true feelings, which is a timeless struggle.” Delete Dunbar. The sentence survives. A real claim: “The stanza makes speech itself part of the payment — ‘mouth with myriad subtleties’ — so the mask is not only a face. It is a way of talking that costs blood.” Delete Dunbar and the claim dies. That is how you know you are on the work.

Theme sentences of the objects you already have

Elpenor, theme sentence: “Odysseus is a flawed leader, and the scene shows the importance of honoring the dead.” Live: “The retelling will not let the prophet speak until the leftover crewman has extracted a public mark — an oar — from the hero who sailed.” The second sentence can be wrong. Argue that. Do not upgrade the first sentence with fancier adjectives.

Receipt, theme sentence: “The poem is about how we don’t end up where we planned.” Live: “The poem spends its counterfactual on numbers that cannot refuse a last stop, then lets the driver withhold the one face that could acknowledge the miss.” A classmate can say the last couplet is the turn, not the numbers. That argument still has to point at lines.

Douglass, theme sentence: “Reading sets you free.” The three sentences you have are a refusal of that poster. A real claim has to hear the curse: “Douglass names literacy as a view without a remedy, so the sentence is not a school-poster. It is an account of knowledge that increases the pit.” Someone can disagree by saying the later Narrative supplies the ladder. Fine. Bring the later chapter if you have it. Do not bring a poster.

Rewrite; keep the object

Take the theme sentence. Cross out “important,” “timeless,” “theme,” “shows us that,” “I relate.” Put the made thing back in. Put a doing verb in (turns, costs, extracts, withholds, retracts, records). Put the claim in a size a page can test. Then write one honest refusal — a reason a real reader would say no. If you cannot write the refusal, the claim is still a theme sentence. Change it while it is cheap.

A claim about a work is not a claim about history, and it is not a claim about you. “Slavery was wrong” is true and not a reading of Douglass’s three sentences. “I also hide my feelings on the bus” is a diary entry next to Dunbar. History and your life can come in later, as stakes (Unit 6), once the object has a claim on it.

The page will catch empty phrases. It will not catch a smooth sentence that still refuses to be wrong. Read your rewrite as a slightly hostile classmate. If they have nothing to point at, you are not done.

Practice

Choose the better claim

Same object: Dunbar’s opening stanza. Which claim can be wrong in a way that still points at the lines?

Practice

Rewrite a quick reading

Quick version: “The Odyssey shows us the importance of honoring the dead and teaches a timeless lesson about leadership.”

Rewrite it as one or two sentences a classmate could disagree with. Name a doing in the Elpenor retelling. Avoid “shows us,” “importance,” “timeless,” “lesson,” “theme,” and “human condition.”

Homework

180–280 words.

Write a claim about one course text (Elpenor, Receipt, Douglass, or Dunbar). One or two sentences. Then write one honest refusal — a reason a real reader would say no, pointing at a line. Then write one sentence: what you would change in the claim if that refusal turned out to be true. Do not defend yourself in the refusal. Let it land. No “important.” No “I relate.”
