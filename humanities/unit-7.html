Unit 7 · You write one · ~50 minutes

A short interpretive essay

Five to seven hundred words. A claim, stayed-with evidence, one other voice, a stake that is not “the human condition.” Not a five-paragraph theme. Not a biography dump.

3 objectives Diagnose + sequence 1 essay

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Reject an opening that is biography, plot, or a tourist plaque, and start from a claim about a doing.
  2. Sequence an interpretive essay: claim, stay with one passage, one hearing-aid fact, one other voice, a named stake.
  3. Produce a 500–700 word draft that a classmate could refuse by pointing at the same lines.

Lesson

What you are not writing

You are not writing a report on an author. You are not writing “Homer was a blind poet who…” You are not writing a plot card. You are not writing five paragraphs that promise three themes and deliver three captions. You are not writing a personal essay that uses a poem as a launchpad for your bus ride. Those are recognizable school objects. They can even get a B in a tired room. They are not this assignment.

An interpretive essay in this course is a public act. You put a claim about a made thing on the table. You stay with enough of the thing to risk being wrong. You let one other voice pressure you. You say who the smooth version of your claim would leave out. Then you stop. Seven hundred words is enough if you do not drown.

A working sequence

Open with the claim, or with one line and then the claim by the third sentence. Do not open with the century. Do not open with “according to the dictionary.” Do not open with a question you do not answer. “The Elpenor retelling will not let the prophet speak until a leftover crewman has extracted an oar” is an opening. “Since the dawn of time, humans have wondered about death” is a refusal to begin.

Stay. One passage, maybe two short moments from the same work if the second one is the turn. Quote little. Name the doing. Use Unit 2 verbs: turns, costs, extracts, withholds, retracts, records. If a paragraph has no word from the text, it is a commercial. Cut it or send it back to a line.

One hearing-aid fact, not a context chapter. Auld treating literacy as a threat to property. An unburied body as a living debt. Dunbar’s standard English against a market that wanted dialect. Equiano writing later, in English, to a British public. Then return. If the fact does not change how a stranger hears a word you already quoted, it is decoration.

One other voice. Two to four sentences. Job named: answers, limits, complicates. No third writer. No “similarly.” “Similarly” is the dump adverb. If you need it, the second voice is not working.

A stake that can be pointed at. Who is centered, who pays, who is missing. A living person by role — a student left off a group name, a worker whose grin is extracted, a rider whose last number cannot refuse. Then stop. Do not add a paragraph about what “we as a society” must do. The essay’s last move is to leave the cost visible, not to run a campaign.

What a bad opening sounds like

Invented, for diagnosis. Not student work:

Invented opening · do not imitate

Paul Laurence Dunbar was a famous African American poet born in 1872 in Dayton, Ohio. He wrote many poems about race and identity. In today’s world, everyone wears a mask, and this is an important theme in literature throughout history. In this essay I will discuss how Dunbar shows the human condition and why we should be more understanding of others.

Count the failures. Biography first. “Many poems about race and identity” — shelf label. “Today’s world / everyone / important theme / throughout history / human condition / I will discuss / be more understanding.” That paragraph can be written about any work. It has not looked. If your draft starts this way, delete the first paragraph and begin at the first sentence that names a doing. Often that sentence is already in paragraph two or three, hiding.

A usable opening, also invented, not a model to copy word for word:

Invented opening · a direction, not a template

Dunbar’s first stanza makes the smile a debt. “This debt we pay to human guile” is not a mood. It is a public extraction, and the next line puts the payment in the mouth: “we mouth with myriad subtleties.” A reading that turns the poem into everyone’s manners has already left out the person for whom the grin is not optional.

Claim, line, doing, stake — in four sentences. The rest of the essay has work to do: stay with “mouth,” bring one hearing-aid fact about the market for his voice, let Douglass’s published “I” pressure Dunbar’s “we,” and return to who “we” leaves available. That is a 600-word job. It is not a research paper. Do not add a works-cited page of articles you did not read. The course texts are enough. If an instructor assigns another public-domain work, use that the same way. Do not paste a critic.

Ugly is allowed; empty is not

The draft you turn in can be uneven. It cannot be a theme paper with better adjectives. Write past the fear of being wrong. A live claim you later revise is the course working. A smooth plaque you are proud of is the course failing politely. If you stall, steal your Unit 4 claim and your Unit 2 marked line and start typing. Do not outline five boxes. Sequence is a path, not a five-paragraph jail.

Word count is a discipline, not a target to game. Under 500 usually means you never stayed or never let a second voice in. Over 800 usually means you drowned or sermonized. Aim 500–700. Quote under a few dozen words at a time. The Elpenor piece is a teaching retelling: say so if you use it. Receipt is a teaching poem: say so. Douglass, Dunbar, Equiano are 1845, 1896, 1789. Name the year once. Then work.

Practice: diagnose the bad opening, then put the essay moves in order. Homework is the essay. If you are alone, write it anyway and keep the file. The page will not store it. Unit 8 will ask you to take one sentence of it public.

What is this opening doing?

The invented Dunbar paragraph above (“famous African American poet born in 1872…”). Pick the accurate diagnosis.

Practice

Sequence the moves

An interpretive essay in this course needs these five moves. Check them in the order you would put them on the page. Then compare to the sequence the lesson argued for.

Homework — the essay

500–700 words. Turn this in if an instructor is running the course.

Write a short interpretive essay on one course text, with one other course text in pressure. Open from a claim about a doing, not from a biography or a plaque. Stay with a passage. Use at most one hearing-aid fact. Name a stake (who is left out) for a living person by role. Quote little. Do not write “human condition,” “since the dawn of time,” “in this essay I will,” or “we as a society.” If you use the Elpenor retelling or Receipt, label them teaching texts. Copy the essay out of this box; the file does not keep it.
