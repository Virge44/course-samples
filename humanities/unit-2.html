Unit 2 · Stay with one passage · ~40 minutes

Close looking / close reading

Do not survey. Stay. Mark the line that turns the work, and say what that line is doing.

3 objectives Mark a line 1 homework

When you finish

  1. Stay with one short passage long enough to point at a single line (or image-detail) as the turn.
  2. Say what that line is doing — turning, costing, refusing, recording — rather than what the work is “about.”
  3. Reject a reading that replaces the passage with a theme, a plot recap, or a private vibe.

Lesson

Stay means stay

Close reading is a refusal to leave. The first-year reflex is coverage: mention three images, name two themes, close with “overall.” That paper can be written without having looked. You pick one passage — twelve lines, a paragraph, one beat — and you do not get to be finished until you can put your finger on the place the work turns.

A turn is not “the most poetic line.” It is the place the work spends something. A word that costs more than the line next to it. A refusal. A record the speaker did not have to keep. If you cannot say what is spent, you are still sightseeing.

You will practice on a twelve-line teaching poem I wrote for this sample. It is not a student poem and not a famous lyric. It exists so you can stay with it without Googling a theme essay. After this unit you will use the same habit on Douglass, Dunbar, Equiano, and the Elpenor retelling.

Teaching poem

The bus keeps its own weather. A woman counts the stops on the back of a receipt as if the numbers could refuse the last one. Across the aisle a boy mouths a verse he is not supposed to know in this hour. The driver does not turn. Rain writes the same sentence on the glass twice. Nobody gets off where they said they would.

What “doing” means

Students say “this poem is about a bus ride” and think they have read it. That is a caption. A caption names the furniture. Close reading names the work the furniture is made to do.

Look at “as if the numbers could refuse / the last one.” The woman is not “stressed about her stop.” The poem gives her a counterfactual: numbers with the power to say no. They cannot. The last stop arrives anyway. The receipt is not a cute urban detail. It is the cheapest paper that will take a count — leftover proof from another transaction, now asked to hold a life. If you skip those two facts, you will write “the poem shows that life is a journey.” You will have left.

Look at the boy who “mouths a verse / he is not supposed to know / in this hour.” The poem does not tell you the verse. It tells you the prohibition and the hour. If you invent the verse (church, school, rap) you have stopped looking and started decorating. Stay with what is there: a forbidden knowing, timed, silent, across the aisle from a woman whose numbers cannot refuse.

The driver “does not turn.” Rain “writes the same sentence on the glass / twice.” Nobody gets off where they said they would. Those three closings are easy to theme. Resist. Ask what they do to the earlier lines. The driver who does not turn withholds the one face that could acknowledge either passenger. The last couplet does not moralize. It reports a miss. Say how the miss was built — receipt, last number, unsounded verse, unturned driver — not that missing your stop is “relatable.”

Close looking is the same habit

If the object were an image — a public-domain vase, a photograph you are allowed to use, a mural on the way to class — you would do the same thing. Do not inventory the whole scene. Pick the detail that spends something. Say what that detail is doing to a viewer who is still alive. “The vase is beautiful” is appreciation. “The runner’s feet leave the ground while the mourners’ feet do not” is looking.

You do not need a technical vocabulary to start. You need a finger and a verb. Point. Then say the verb: turns, costs, refuses, records, withholds, doubles, drops. If your verb is “shows” or “is about,” you have not started.

Close reading is not hunting for secret symbols the author hid for English teachers. The receipt is a receipt. The oar in Unit 1 is an oar. The work is in what the object is asked to do. If your paper needs the receipt to “symbolize capitalism” before you have said it is cheap paper asked to hold a count, you skipped the looking.

In the practice, mark the line you think turns the poem. The page has a preferred turn — the numbers that cannot refuse — because that is where the poem spends the counterfactual. A different line can be a turn if you can say the spend. If a sentence was available before you looked, it is not a reading.

Practice

Mark the line that turns it

Click the line where the poem spends the most — the place a tourist reading is most likely to skip. Then check. Keyboard: Tab to a line, Enter or Space to mark.

No line marked.

Practice

What is that line doing?

Assume the turn is “as if the numbers could refuse / the last one.” Which account names a doing?

Homework

150–220 words.

Stay with Receipt or with the Elpenor retelling from Unit 1. Quote no more than two lines (or two short sentences). Name the turn. Write what that turn is doing — a verb other than “shows” or “is about.” Then write one sentence you are refusing: the theme-card this passage most invites. Do not invent symbols the lines do not earn. Do not write “life is a journey.”
