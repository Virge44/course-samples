Unit 8 · One work, one claim, one public sentence · ~35 minutes

Portfolio close

Leave with three things you would stand behind: a work, a claim that can be wrong, and one sentence you could say outside class.

3 objectives Assemble + refuse 1 close

When you finish

  1. Choose one work from this course (or another public-domain work you name) and say why it is the one you will stand behind — not why it is “your favorite.”
  2. State one claim about that work that a classmate could refuse by pointing at a line.
  3. Write one public sentence: what this made thing makes of a living person — a sentence you could say off campus without “theme,” “relatable,” or “human condition.”

Lesson

A close is not a souvenir

Portfolios in first-year courses often become scrapbooks: my best paragraph, a quote I liked, a feeling I had in week two. This close is three sentences that have jobs, plus a short paragraph that ties them. If you cannot say the three aloud to someone who did not take the course, you are not done.

One work. Not five. The work can be the Elpenor retelling, Receipt, Douglass’s three sentences, Dunbar’s stanza, Equiano’s look, or another public-domain piece an instructor added. If you choose something from outside this folder, name it, name the year, and keep the excerpt short. Do not choose a recent lyric you cannot legally quote. Choose it because you can still point at a turn.

“Favorite” is not a reason. Favorite is a mood. A reason sounds like: “This is the work I can still be wrong about.” Or: “This is the work whose leftover person I keep seeing on my campus.” If your reason is “I relate to buses” or “I like Greek myths,” pick again. Relating and liking are allowed in a life. They are not this close.

One claim that can still break

Take the claim from Unit 4 or Unit 7 and make it smaller if it got proud. A close claim is not a summary of your essay. It is the sentence you would defend in a hallway. “Dunbar’s opening makes speech a payment, not a face-covering.” “The retelling stops a prophet for a crewman’s oar.” “Douglass names reading as a pit without a ladder, so the sentence will not work as a school poster.” Each of those can be refused. If your close claim is “this work is powerful and still relevant,” you have advertised.

Write the refusal next to it. The refusal is how you know the claim is live. “No, because the last couplet is the turn.” “No, because later Douglass supplies a ladder and you froze a midpoint.” If you cannot imagine that “no,” the claim is still a theme-card. Change it here, cheaply, before you make it public.

One public sentence

The public sentence is not a thesis and not a slogan. It is what the work makes of a living person — said so that a roommate, a supervisor, a younger cousin, or a stranger on a bus could hear it without a glossary. It has to keep the object. “Be kind; everyone is fighting a battle” is a public sentence that does not need Dunbar. Useless here. “Dunbar’s ‘we’ will not let me treat a workplace grin as a private mood; somebody is paying” is public and still on the stanza.

Test it out loud. If you would be embarrassed to say it off campus because it sounds like a greeting card, cut the greeting. If you would be embarrassed because it sounds like a seminar, cut one abstract noun and put a concrete doing back in.

Examples, invented, not required. Elpenor: “The first dead voice is the man the voyage treated as leftover labor — and the official errand waits for a public mark.” Douglass: “He calls the literacy he fought for a curse because it gave him the pit without the ladder — which is not a school poster.” Neither is a moral. Each leaves a living listener with a job.

I taught Humanities I & II. This file is not those courses. You are not claiming you finished the humanities. You are claiming that you can look at a made thing, say what it is doing, and say why that matters to someone still alive. If that is all you leave with, the compressed semester did its work.

Practice

Build a close — or refuse a souvenir

Check every piece that could go in a real close. Leave souvenirs unchecked.

Practice

Write a public sentence

One sentence. Use any course text. What does the work make of a living person? Avoid “theme,” “relatable,” “human condition,” “important,” and “be kinder.”

Homework — the close

180–280 words, counting the three labeled sentences.

Write three labeled sentences, then a short paragraph. (1) Work: which object, and why you will stand behind it — not why it is your favorite. (2) Claim: what it is doing, in a form that can be wrong. (3) Public: what it makes of a living person, said so a stranger off campus could hear it. Paragraph: what theme-card you arrived with, and what the work did to it. No invented classmates. No “I learned so much.” If you use a teaching text, say so.
