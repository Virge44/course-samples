Who this is for

First-year and gen-ed college students in a large section, mixed preparation. Adult tone. You do not need a logic textbook. You need a habit: say why, show the step, leave something a classmate can check.

This sample is written in the grain of the large-enrollment Analytical Reasoning course Virgil Renfroe taught in University Studies at NC A&T, 2004–2016 — often 300–600 students a semester, many underprepared. The instructor also did GRE and LSAT logic tutoring. That muscle is here. This is still a 101, not test prep. No game-board items. No Latin bingo card.

What the course asks you to do

Reasoning is a public habit. A private hunch is not yet an argument. Each unit has three measurable objectives, a lesson, clickable practice with feedback, and one homework you could turn in. If you do the practices, plan on about four to six hours for the whole sample. A live semester would slow this down, add readings, and give you more drafts. This file is the compressed 101: the spine, not the full term.

A claim is a sentence that asks to be believed. A reason is a sentence that tries to earn that belief. If you cannot say the because, you do not yet have an argument.

How work is judged (if you have a section)

If an instructor used this sample in a live class, the standard would be public, not clever. Did you name a claim? Did you give a because someone could check? Did you mark an assumption? Did you leave a step visible? Fluency is not the grade. The checkable step is the grade.

Homework lives in your own words. Paste the prompt into a journal, a discussion board, or a printed page. This file does not collect it.
