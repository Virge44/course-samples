Unit 8 · They pick a real one · ~40 minutes

A civic or campus close

Close a short case on an issue you can point to. A posted notice, a public agenda item, a syllabus rule. No invented news.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

After the practice

  1. Choose a real civic or campus issue you can point to (a URL, a posted PDF, a notice on a door) and state a case-sized claim.
  2. Refuse invented facts — if a number or quote is missing, say it is missing and name the check.
  3. Write a close: claim, reasons, one fair objection, a reply, and a next step a person could actually take.

Lesson

This is the last unit of a compressed 101. A live semester would give you more drafts and a longer reading list. The spine is the same: say why, show the step, let someone check it. The close is one short case about something in the world that exists without you.

Pick a real one. Real means you can point to it. A dining or shuttle notice on a door or a campus site. A syllabus attendance line. A city or county agenda packet posted for a public meeting. A letter to the editor you can name by paper and date. If you cannot point to it, you do not have an issue yet. You have a mood.

This sample will not invent a headline for you, and you will not invent one either. Do not write “after last night’s protest” unless you can name a posted account. Do not write “the mayor said” unless you can point to the remark. Do not write “studies show” unless you can name the study (Unit 4). An honest hole — “I do not have ridership; a two-week sign-in would tell us” — is a better close than a polished fiction.

Choose a claim that fits Unit 7: something a meeting could put on an agenda. “The city should hear public comment on the posted parking change dated [date] before it takes effect” is sized right. “Local government has failed young people” is a theory. You may believe the theory. This assignment cannot carry it. Shrink until the pipe can hold the water you actually have.

Then build the four parts. Claim. Reasons a classmate could check — times, posted lines, an n you can see, a standard you are willing to let someone else apply. One objection a real opponent would sign. A reply that grants, checks, or holds. A next step a person could actually take: a comment window, a sign-in sheet, a posted survey, an office hour. Not “raise awareness.”

Campus and civic issues fail in the same ways. The because is a feeling. The assumption is hidden and load-bearing. The claim outruns the reasons. A percent has no base. Finish the habit once, on something you can point to. Then stop.

Practice

Draft a six-line close

Use a real thing you can point to — or, for this practice only, use this posted-style fact: a door notice dated this term says the writing center is closed Sundays; last exam week a sign-in sheet on that door listed 28 names. Do not add a fake quote from a dean.

Six short lines, labeled: Claim. Reason 1. Reason 2 (or standard). Objection. Reply. Next step.

Homework

300–450 words. Put the pointer at the top.

Close a short case (300–450 words) on a civic or campus issue you can point to. At the top, give the pointer: URL, agenda packet title and date, photo of a door notice (if your section allows), or syllabus line. Then label: Claim. Reasons. Assumption if one is load-bearing. Objection. Reply. Next step. Rules: do not invent news, quotes, or studies. If a number is missing, say so. Adult tone. A classmate who disagrees should still be able to check your steps. This is the course close, not a research paper.
