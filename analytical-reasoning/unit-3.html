Unit 3 · Pipe and water · ~40 minutes

Valid vs sound, in plain language

The pipe can be good and the water bad. A clean structure can carry a false start. A true start can sit in a leaky structure. Learn to say which problem you have.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Explain “valid” and “sound” in ordinary words: structure versus true premises.
  2. Identify an argument whose steps follow but whose starting point is bad.
  3. Rewrite a too-strong conclusion so it only says what the reasons actually buy.

Lesson

Picture a pipe. Water goes in one end. If the joints hold, whatever went in comes out the other end. That is validity: if the reasons were true, the claim would have to be true. The pipe does not care whether the water is clean. It only cares whether it leaks.

Soundness is a stricter word. The pipe holds, and the water is clean. The reasons are true, and they force the claim. In ordinary talk you can say it without the textbook: the steps follow, and the starting points check out. If either half fails, you do not yet have a sound case. You may still have a useful draft.

This is the unit where GRE and LSAT muscle is tempting, and where a gen-ed 101 should refuse the costume. You will not sort “all G are H” items. You will sort campus sentences. The skill is the same. The audience is a first-year section, not a test center.

Example of a good pipe and bad water. “Every course that uses a bluebook is a writing course. This exam uses a bluebook. So this course is a writing course.” The steps follow. If those two reasons were true, the claim would be true. But the first reason is false. Plenty of quantitative exams still hand out a booklet. The pipe is fine. The water is not. People often feel this as “the argument sounds logical but I don’t buy it.” That feeling is pointing at the first premise. Name it that way.

Example of leaky pipe and decent water. “The tutoring center was full Tuesday. I waited twenty minutes. So the university does not care about first-year students.” The wait may be true. The fullness may be true. They do not force the claim. A full room is compatible with caring and understaffing, or with a one-day rush, or with a sign-up system that bunches people at 4. The water might be fine. The pipe leaks. The conclusion is larger than the reasons.

That second problem is the one students produce most often in a large section: a true observation, then a jump. The fix is not to throw out the observation. The fix is to shrink the claim until the pipe holds. “The tutoring center was full Tuesday, and I waited twenty minutes, so the 4 o’clock slot needs another tutor or a different sign-up” is a smaller claim. It might still be wrong. It is no longer a leak from a wait to a theory of the university.

Two questions, in this order:

1. If I grant the reasons, must I grant the claim? If no, the pipe leaks. Tighten the claim or add a reason. 2. Should I grant the reasons? If no, the water is bad. Check the starting point. Do not pretend a clean structure can rescue a false start.

People skip question 1 when they already like the claim, and skip question 2 when the speech sounds neat. Both skips show up in civic talk. A tidy chain from a rumor is still a tidy chain from a rumor. A messy true report can still fail to prove the policy someone wants. Your job is to say which problem you are looking at, out loud, so a classmate can look at the same joint.

Validity is not a compliment and unsound is not an insult. They are diagnoses. “Valid but unsound” means: I see how you got there; I do not grant the start. “Invalid” means: even if I grant the start, I do not have to grant the finish. Students who grew up being told they were “bad at logic” often relax when they hear it this way. You are not being sorted into a type. You are checking a pipe and then checking the water.

One more everyday mix-up. A story can be moving and still leak. A statistic can be accurate and still fail to force a should-claim. Emotion is not the enemy. An unchecked jump is the enemy. If a late walk across a dark lot is the reason, say the walk and the lot and the time. Then say only the claim those facts can buy: better lighting on that path, or a later shuttle, not “the campus is unsafe in every direction.” Shrink until the joints hold. Then ask whether the facts are true.

Practice: sort pipe from water, mark a valid-but-unsound case, and fix a bad inference by shrinking the conclusion. Homework asks you to diagnose one real short argument — pipe, water, or both.

Practice

Pipe or water?

“Every 8 a.m. class is easy. This class is at 8 a.m. So this class is easy.” What is the main problem?

Practice

Which diagnosis?

“I waited twenty minutes at tutoring Tuesday. The room was full. Therefore the university does not care about first-year students.”

Practice

Fix the inference

Reasons you may grant: The last lab lets out at 8:05. The posted last shuttle is 7:50.

Bad claim: So commuter students are being ignored on purpose.

Rewrite the claim so it only says what those two reasons can buy. One sentence. No motive story.

Homework

Find one short argument (posted notice, class discussion, roommate, a public comment on a city or campus agenda you can point to). Write it as two or three sentences: reasons, then claim. Diagnose it in 150–220 words: (1) If the reasons were true, must the claim be true? (pipe) (2) Should we grant the reasons? (water) (3) If the claim is too big, rewrite a smaller claim the reasons could actually buy. Do not invent facts to “help” the speaker.
