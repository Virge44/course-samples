Unit 7 · Claim, reasons, objection, reply · ~40 minutes

Building a short case

Four parts, in public: a claim, reasons a classmate can check, one fair objection, and a reply that does not dodge. That is a case. Not a vibe. Not a thread.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Assemble a short case in four labeled parts: claim, reasons, one objection, a reply.
  2. Write an objection the other side would recognize — not a weaker restatement.
  3. Reply to that objection without changing the subject or attacking the speaker.

Lesson

Units 1–6 were tools. This unit is a small structure that uses them. A short case is four parts you are willing to leave on a page:

  1. A claim someone could agree or disagree with.
  2. Reasons — because-clauses a classmate could check (Unit 1), with the load-bearing assumption named if it is doing real work (Unit 2).
  3. One fair objection: the best reason someone could have for not granting the claim.
  4. A reply that answers that objection, not a worse one you invented (Unit 6).

Why four parts in a first-year course? Because a take with no objection is a speech, and a speech does not teach the public habit. In a 400-person section, the instructor cannot debate every draft. The objection has to be on the page so a peer can see whether you faced a real one. If you cannot write an objection that would make you pause, you are not yet done. You are still in the choir.

Keep the claim small enough for the reasons (Unit 3). “Add a late shuttle on chemistry-lab nights” is a case-sized claim. “The university does not care about commuters” is a theory. Theories belong in a longer paper, and even then they need a pipe that can hold them. Here, prefer a claim a facilities meeting could put on an agenda.

Reasons: two is plenty. One fact-because and, if you are making a should-claim, one standard. “The last lab lets out at 8:05 and the last posted run is 7:50” is the fact. “Students who are still in required labs should have a posted way off campus that does not depend on a private car” is the standard. If you skip the standard, people will hear a complaint. If you skip the fact, people will hear a sermon.

The objection is where students either grow or perform. A performed objection sounds like: “Some people might say money grows on trees, but…” Nobody serious said that. A fair objection sounds like: “A late run costs driver hours and may be empty if only one section lets out late.” That is a real cost. It uses a number-shaped hole (Unit 4) you should not fill with a fake ridership figure. If you do not have the ridership, say you do not have it. The case can still ask for a two-week count.

The reply should do one of three honest things. Grant and adjust: “If the run is empty, cut it to lab nights only — that is already the claim.” Add a check: “We do not have ridership; the next step is a sign-in for two weeks, not a fleet.” Hold the standard: “Even a small ridership still meets the ‘required lab, no posted ride’ rule.” What a reply should not do: change the subject, attack the objector, or restating the claim in a warmer voice and calling it an answer.

Order on the page matters less than labels. Some writers put the objection second so they do not fall in love with the claim. Either way, label the parts. In a large-enrollment course, labels are a kindness to the peer who has twelve minutes. They are also a kindness to you: if a part is missing, you can see the hole before anyone else does.

This is still not test prep. LSAT “point at issue” items train a cousin of the fair objection. Use the cousin. Write the sentence the other side would sign. Then answer it. Unit 8 will ask you to do this with a real civic or campus issue you did not invent. This unit is the rehearsal: a small campus-shaped case, parts visible, no fake news.

Practice: put the four parts in order, mark a weak objection, and write a fair one. Homework: build a short case you could actually turn in.

Practice

Which set is a case?

Pick the set that has all four parts doing real work.

Practice

Which objection is fair?

Claim: add a late shuttle on lab nights. Reasons: lab ends 8:05; last posted run 7:50; required labs should have a posted ride.

Practice

Write a fair objection and a reply

Claim: Keep the writing center open 2–5 on Sunday during exam week. Reason you may use: last exam week the Sunday sign-in sheet (posted on the door) listed 28 names for a closed desk.

Write two labeled sentences: Objection: … Reply: … The objection should be one the budget office could actually make. The reply should not dodge it.

Homework

Build a short case (250–350 words) on a small campus-shaped claim you can point to — hours, a shuttle, a lab policy, a posted notice. Label four parts: Claim. Reasons. Objection. Reply. The objection must be one a real opponent would sign. Do not invent ridership, quotes, or a study. If a number is missing, say it is missing and name the check (a two-week count, a posted survey n). You will use this muscle in Unit 8 on an issue you choose.
