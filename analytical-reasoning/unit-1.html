Unit 1 · Claim vs because · ~35 minutes

What counts as a reason

A claim asks to be believed. A reason tries to earn that belief. If you cannot say the because, you have a take, not an argument.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Split a short passage into a claim and a because-clause.
  2. Mark a “because” that only restates the claim instead of supporting it.
  3. Write a because-clause a classmate could check without already agreeing with you.

Lesson

A claim is a sentence that asks to be believed. A reason is a sentence that tries to earn that belief. The word that usually sits between them is because. If you cannot say the because, you do not yet have an argument. You have a take.

This course is written in the grain of a large-enrollment Analytical Reasoning class: first-year and gen-ed students, mixed preparation, a room that could hold a few hundred people. It is a draft sample, not a live institutional course and not official NC A&T curriculum. The habit it teaches is public. You say why. You show the step. You leave something a classmate could check.

People often treat the because as decoration. They write “The dining hall should stay open later because students deserve better” and think they have argued. Deserve better restates the claim in warmer language. It does not give a fact, a principle, or a comparison someone else can test. A reason a classmate can check sounds more like: “The dining hall should stay open later because the last class block ends at 8:10 and the line still has thirty people at 8:40.” Now there are times and a count. Someone can go look.

Three tests help.

First: Can you split the sentence? Put the claim on one line and the because on the next. If you cannot find a second line, you do not have a reason yet. “Late labs are a problem” is a claim looking for work. “Late labs are a problem because the last campus shuttle listed on the posted schedule leaves before the 8:00 section lets out” is an argument. Thin, but an argument. A peer can check the schedule.

Second: Does the because say something the claim does not already say? “We should require attendance because showing up matters” fails this test. Showing up is attendance. You have circled. The same circle shows up in civic talk: “The city should fund the library because libraries matter.” Maybe they do. The sentence has not yet said why this budget line, this year, against these other lines. A reason has to add a piece of the world, or a standard, that the claim itself did not already contain.

Third: Could a skeptical person check the because without first agreeing with the claim? If the only way to accept the reason is to already want the conclusion, the reason is doing no work. “We should keep the late lab because it is obviously the right thing” asks the skeptic to join the choir. “We should keep the late lab because the posted waitlist for the daytime section is forty names and the room seats twenty-four” gives the skeptic a door. They can dispute the numbers. They cannot say you hid the step.

You will hear people say “I feel like” and then a policy. Feeling can start a question. It is not, by itself, a reason. “I feel unsafe walking from the late lab” can become a reason if you add what would have to be true: lighting on that path, time of night, a posted report, a pattern you can point to. The feeling names the stake. The because has to name the world.

This is not a course in winning. It is a course in making the step visible. In a 400-person section, the instructor cannot sit beside every draft. The check has to live on the page: claim, because, something a peer can mark. That is why this unit will not grade you on how strongly you feel. It will ask whether a roommate who disagrees could still see what you are asking them to check.

A claim can be a fact-claim (“the shuttle skipped two stops Tuesday”) or a should-claim (“the shuttle should run after 10”). Both need because-clauses. Fact-claims need sources or observations. Should-claims need a fact plus a standard: what counts as enough service, enough safety, enough time. If you skip the standard, people will fight about taste when they could be fighting about a rule. “Enough” is not a vibe. “Enough” is a line you are willing to let someone else apply.

GRE and LSAT tutoring uses this same split under heavier costumes: conclusion, premise, a gap. You do not need the costume. You need the split. Practice here is not a quiz you pass once. You will mark a claim, reject a fake because, and write a because-clause that could survive a roommate who disagrees. That last move is the one this course keeps asking for.

Claim
The writing center should add Sunday hours.
Because (checkable)
The Sunday sign-in sheet last month listed 28 names for a space that was closed.
Claim again
The writing center should add Sunday hours.
Because (circle)
Students deserve weekend support. Warmer. Not a reason yet.

Practice

Mark the claim

Read the short passage. Which sentence is the claim — the thing the speaker wants you to believe?

The last chemistry lab lets out at 8:05. The posted shuttle schedule shows the last run at 7:50. The university should add a late shuttle on lab nights.

Practice

Which because is doing work?

Same claim: The dining hall should stay open until 9. Pick the because-clause that a classmate could actually check.

Practice

Write a because-clause

Claim: The library should keep the second floor open until midnight during exam week.

Write one sentence that starts with because and gives something a roommate could check — a time, a count, a posted rule, a comparison. Do not restate “students need a quiet place.” That is the claim in a softer coat.

Homework

Turn this in if a section wants it. The box below stays in this browser only.

Find one claim in ordinary campus talk this week — dining hours, parking, attendance, a posted notice, a roommate argument. Do not invent a story. Copy or paraphrase the claim in one sentence. Then write a because-clause that would support it (or that the speaker seemed to mean). Then write one more sentence: how could a classmate check that because without already agreeing? 150–200 words. If you cannot find a checkable because, say so, and write the because the speaker would need.
