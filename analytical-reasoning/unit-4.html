Unit 4 · Percents, base rates, “studies show” · ~40 minutes

Everyday numbers

Not a statistics course. Name the base. Ask what the percent is of. Do not let “studies show” do the arguing.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

After the practice

  1. Restate a percent once you know what it is a percent of — including the base count.
  2. Spot a missing base rate — a number that sounds large until you see the group size.
  3. Ask one check question of a “studies show” claim before you repeat it.

Lesson

Numbers show up in campus and civic talk as if they were reasons. Sometimes they are. Sometimes they are costumes. This unit is not Intro to Statistics. You will not compute a standard deviation. You will ask three ordinary questions: Of what? Compared with what? Who was counted?

Start with percents. “80% of students want Sunday hours” is not yet a reason. 80% of whom? Of the 25 people who answered a hallway poll, or of a posted campus-wide survey with a response count you can see? 80% of 25 is 20 people. 80% of 4,000 is a different object. Write the base next to the percent, even in the margin: 80% (n = 25, hallway).

A close cousin is the raw number with no base. “200 students signed the petition.” 200 of 500 in a residence hall is one story. 200 of a 12,000-person campus is another. Neither story is automatically “small.” Both are incomplete until the base is in the sentence. In a large-enrollment course this is the error that travels fastest: a number that feels big because it is a number.

Base rate is the usual rate in the larger group. If a headline says a test “catches 90% of cases,” you still need the usual rate of the thing being tested. If the thing is rare, most of the positive flags can still be false. Campus version: “Half the people who used the late shuttle last month were commuters.” If almost everyone on that route is a commuter, “half” is not a finding. It is the weather.

“Studies show” is a because that has not clocked in. Which study? How many people? Compared with what? Did the study measure the thing the speaker is now selling?

You are not required to become a cynic. You are required to keep the step visible. A fair use of a number sounds like this: “The posted dining survey (spring, 412 responses) said 61% wanted a later weekday line. That is a majority of respondents, not of the campus.” The limit is part of the reason.

Practice

Name the base

“80% of students want the late shuttle.” Which follow-up does the most work?

Practice

Same percent, different world

Which sentence is the honest restatement?

Practice

Write one check question

Someone says: “Studies show that later dining hours raise student success.”

Write one question that would have to be answered before this sentence can work as a because. Not a speech. One question.

Homework

150–220 words. Find one number you can point to.

Find one number in the wild this week: a posted campus survey, a budget or enrollment figure, a city or county agenda packet, a news story you can link or name. Copy the sentence that uses the number. Then, in 150–220 words: (1) What is the number a percent or count of? If the source does not say, write that. (2) What comparison is missing? (3) What claim can the number actually buy, and what claim is too large? Do not invent a study to fill the gap.
