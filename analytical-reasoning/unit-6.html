Unit 6 · Five moves that show up · ~40 minutes

Fallacies that actually show up

Not a Latin bingo card. Five patterns from campus and civic talk. Name the move. Repair the sentence. Leave a step someone can check.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Name five common moves: weaker restatement, attacking the speaker, fake either/or, unchecked chain, and “everyone knows.”
  2. Mark which move a short campus or civic sentence is using.
  3. Rewrite one sentence so the claim stands without the trick.

Lesson

A fallacy list can become a sport. Students collect Latin names and pin them on people they already dislike. That is not this unit. Five moves show up in hallways, comment threads, and public comment periods. You will learn their plain names, what they hide, and how to put a real because back on the page. If you already know the Latin, you may keep it in a footnote. The grade, in a live section, would be the repair.

1. Weaker restatement (often called straw person). You recast the other side as something easier to knock down, then knock that down. “They want the library open later, so they must think staff should never go home.” Nobody said never. The live claim was a posted hour. Repair: quote the actual claim, then answer that. Kindness is not the point. Accuracy is. If you cannot write the other side so they would nod, you are not yet arguing with them.

2. Attacking the speaker instead of the claim. “Of course she wants a late shuttle — she lives off campus.” Maybe she does. The schedule gap in Unit 1 does not become false because of her address. Motive can be a later question (who benefits). It is not a substitute for checking the because. Repair: grant the biography in a clause, then return to the times. “She is a commuter. The last lab still lets out after the last run.”

3. Fake either/or. “Either we require attendance or students will never learn.” Those are not the only bins. A course can take attendance in lab, not in lecture; can grade process work; can drop the lowest day. Fake either/or hides the menu. Repair: name a third option, even if you will reject it. The point is to show that the fork was carved too early.

4. Unchecked chain (the slide). “If we allow one make-up, we will have to allow them all, and then grades will mean nothing.” Sometimes a line really does not hold. Sometimes the speaker skipped the differences that would stop the slide (Unit 5). Repair: name the stopping rule. “One make-up with documentation, already on the syllabus, is not ‘all.’” If you cannot name a stopping rule, say that. Honesty about a thin line is better than a prophecy.

5. “Everyone knows.” A crowd is asked to do the work of a because. “Everyone knows the dining hall is a disaster.” Maybe many people are unhappy. You still need a checkable piece: a time, a wait, a posted survey with an n. Popularity can be evidence that a feeling is common. It is not evidence that a policy is right. Repair: replace the crowd with one thing a roommate could look up.

These five share a habit: they hide the step. Weaker restatement hides the real claim. Attacking the speaker hides the because. Fake either/or hides the other options. The slide hides the stopping rule. “Everyone knows” hides the base (Unit 4). Once you see the hide, you do not need a poster of twenty Latin names. You need a rewrite.

Two cautions for a large first-year section. First, do not hunt fallacies in people you already oppose and forgive them in people you like. The move is the move. Second, not every short sentence is a fallacy. “I don’t buy the first premise” is Unit 3. “What has to be true?” is Unit 2. Save the fallacy label for a hidden step, not for a disagreement.

Logic tutoring uses these patterns because they show up on tests. They show up in life because they are fast. Fast is not the same as checkable. In a public habit of reasoning, speed is allowed. Invisibility is not. Say which move you think you are seeing, then offer the repaired sentence. Let someone disagree about the label. The repaired sentence is what they can work with.

Practice: match a sentence to a move, choose a fair repair, and rewrite one yourself. Homework: catch one in the wild and repair it without inventing a worse opponent.

The five, on one card

  • Weaker restatement — answer the claim they made, not a smaller one you handed them.
  • Attack the speaker — biography is not a because.
  • Fake either/or — name a third option.
  • Unchecked chain — name the stopping rule.
  • “Everyone knows” — replace the crowd with a checkable piece.

Practice

Name the move

“They want a late dining line, so they must think staff should work all night with no say.”

Practice

Same five, different sentence

“Of course he wants the lot lights fixed — he’s always complaining.”

Practice

Repair the sentence

Original: “Everyone knows the attendance policy is unfair, so we should drop it.”

Rewrite it so the claim can stand without the crowd. Give a because a classmate could check, or shrink the claim. One or two sentences.

Homework

Catch one of the five moves in ordinary talk this week — a comment thread, a class discussion, a roommate, a public meeting clip you can point to. Do not invent a villain. In 150–220 words: (1) quote or paraphrase the sentence; (2) name the move in plain language; (3) write the repaired version; (4) say what a classmate could now check. If you are not sure it is a fallacy, say which unit it might belong to instead (assumption, leaky pipe, missing base).
