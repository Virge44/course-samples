Unit 2 · Rotation, seasons, Moon · ~40 minutes

The sky as a clock

Day, year, and Moon phase are geometry you can say in plain language. Mood is not a mechanism.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Explain day and night as Earth’s rotation, not as the sky turning on its own.
  2. Explain seasons as tilt, and reject “Earth is closer to the Sun in summer.”
  3. Describe Moon phase as the Sun–Earth–Moon angle, and separate phase from eclipse.

Lesson

The sky looks like it moves. Stars rise and set. The Sun crosses the day. The Moon changes shape over a month. Those appearances are real. The mechanisms are not “the sky is a wheel we live under.” The mechanisms are Earth’s spin, Earth’s tilt as it goes around the Sun, and the Moon’s place in sunlight.

Earth rotates once a day. The conservative classroom figure is about 24 hours for a solar day — noon to noon. Because Earth is also moving along its orbit, the rotation that brings the same star back overhead is about four minutes shorter. You do not need that distinction to pass this unit, but you should know it exists so you do not treat “a day” as one magic number. The useful claim is simpler: we spin. The Sun appears to move because we turn. Night is the half of the ball that faces away from the Sun. There is no separate dark fluid that pours over the ground.

If you watch stars for an hour, the whole pattern appears to shift together. That common motion is the same rotation. Planets still wander slowly against the pattern from night to night; that is their own motion around the Sun, seen from a moving Earth. One night of watching mostly shows our spin. Many nights start to show the other clocks.

Seasons are the next clock people get wrong. Earth is closest to the Sun in early January — perihelion, a public and ordinary fact — and farthest in July. If distance to the Sun ran the seasons, January would be the global hot month. It is not. Seasons come from tilt. Earth’s axis is tilted about 23 degrees relative to its path around the Sun. In June, the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun: longer days, sunlight hitting the ground more squarely. In December, that hemisphere leans away: shorter days, sunlight spread thinner. The Southern Hemisphere runs the opposite calendar. Same Sun. Same orbit. Different lean.

Tilt also explains why polar summers can have very long days and polar winters very long nights. This course will not ask you to compute sunrise tables. It will ask you not to say “summer means we are closer.” That sentence is common and false.

The Moon is a third clock. It orbits Earth in about a month (a conservative figure: about 27 days to return to the same stars, about 29.5 days to return to the same phase). Half the Moon is always lit by the Sun, the way half of Earth is always in day. What changes is how much of that lit half faces us. New, crescent, quarter, gibbous, full — those words are geometry, not weather and not Earth’s shadow.

Earth’s shadow on the Moon is an eclipse, and it is rare compared with phases. A lunar eclipse can happen only near full Moon, when Sun, Earth, and Moon line up so Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon. A solar eclipse can happen only near new Moon, when the Moon passes in front of the Sun. Most months, the Moon’s path is a little above or below that line, so there is no eclipse. Phase happens every month. Eclipse does not. If someone says “the crescent is Earth’s shadow,” they have mixed the two.

You can check phase without a telescope. Look at the Moon, or at a public daytime photograph of it. Is a thin curve lit? A half disk? A full coin? Write the word. Then write what must be true: the Sun is lighting one side, and we are seeing a slice of that side. You do not need to stand on the Moon. You need the triangle: Sun, Moon, you.

Calendars grew from these clocks. Months remember the Moon. Years remember the orbit. Days remember the spin. Cultures named the patterns in many languages. Naming is not astrology. Astrology is the extra claim that a pattern governs a personality or a week. We will not make that claim. We will not spend class time on it.

If you live in the Northern Hemisphere, the north sky appears to turn around a point near Polaris. That is a useful local fact, not a universal center. In the Southern Hemisphere the story is a different pole. “The sky turns around the North Star” is a local description. It is not a claim that Earth is the middle of everything. Unit 7 will put us in a galaxy. For now: your local clock has a local pole.

Three tests again.

First: Which clock? Spin (day), tilt-plus-orbit (season), or Moon angle (phase)? If you cannot name the clock, you are describing a mood.

Second: Did you use Earth’s shadow for an ordinary crescent? If yes, restart. Shadow is eclipse. Phase is a lit half seen at an angle.

Third: Did you put summer on a closer Sun? Check January. Then use tilt.

You will leave this unit able to run three clocks in plain language. That is the whole job. The sky will keep moving whether or not you romanticize it.

Phase
We see a slice of the Moon’s sunlit half.
Eclipse
Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon — rare, and only near full.

Crescent or shadow?

Someone says the crescent Moon is dark because Earth is blocking the Sun. What do you say?

Practice

Name the clock

In two or three sentences, explain either seasons or Moon phase. Use tilt or the lit-half angle. Do not use mood words as a mechanism.

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Write 150–200 words. Pick one: (a) seasons or (b) Moon phase. Sketch in words a classmate could redraw: which way Earth leans, or how much of the Moon’s lit half we face. Then write one sentence that is a common false mechanism (closer Sun, or Earth’s shadow on a crescent) and cross it out by explaining why it fails. If you can see the Moon, name the phase. If you cannot, say so — do not invent a viewing.
