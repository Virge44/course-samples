Unit 4 · Spectrum and color · ~40 minutes

Light as data

A color is a clue, not a complete file. Stars glow. Many planets only bounce. Say which one you are looking at.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Say that hotter glowing objects peak toward bluer light, and cooler ones toward redder light.
  2. Describe a spectrum as light spread by color, with lines that can mark gases.
  3. Refuse to read a planet’s color as if it were a star’s surface temperature.

Lesson

Light is how almost every distant fact in this course arrives. We do not travel to a star and cut a sample. We catch what it sent. That makes light a kind of data — limited, delayed (Unit 1), and still the best public evidence we have.

Spread sunlight through a prism or a grating and you get a rainbow: violet through red. That spread is a spectrum. The conservative classroom add-on: the rainbow is not the whole story. Invisible light continues past red (infrared, then longer) and past violet (ultraviolet, then shorter). You do not need a military catalog of bands. You need the idea that “color” is a slice of a longer family, and that instruments can sort the family.

A hot, glowing solid or a dense glowing gas does not pick one crayon. It sends a mix. The mix has a peak. For ordinary stars, a hotter surface peaks toward the blue-white side; a cooler surface peaks toward the red-orange side. This is a public rule of thermal glow (Wien’s idea, without the formula). The Sun is a roughly middle case: white if you do not stare, yellowish when the air scatters. A red star in a textbook is not angry. It is cooler at the surface than a blue star. Cooler still means thousands of degrees; “cool” is relative.

Here is the trap. A planet seen with the eye is usually not showing you its own heat-glow in rainbow colors. It is showing you sunlight bounced off clouds, dust, ice, or rust. Mars looks reddish because of iron-rich dust, not because Mars is a red star. Jupiter’s bands are clouds. A blue star is telling you about surface temperature. A blue planet may be telling you about oceans or haze. Same word — blue — different ledger. Always name the object class before you name the meaning of the color.

Lines make the next kind of data. If you spread the Sun’s light carefully, the rainbow is crossed by thin dark lines. Those lines are public and old (Fraunhofer’s list; Kirchhoff’s lab reading). A gas in front of a hotter continuum can swallow particular colors; a hot thin gas can emit particular colors. The pattern of lines is a fingerprint of the kinds of atoms and simple molecules present. This 101 will not ask you to identify a line list. It will ask you to say what a line is for: composition, and sometimes motion or temperature, when someone with a spectrum has done the work.

Motion, conservatively: if a source moves toward you or away from you, the lines can shift a little toward blue or red (Doppler shift). That is a real public tool. It is also easy to overclaim. You cannot see a line shift with the unaided eye. You cannot turn a single color photograph into a speed. If a caption says a galaxy is receding, that claim was earned with spectra, not with a postcard. Unit 7 will use the idea at the level of “on large scales, distant galaxies show recession.” It will not ask you to reduce data.

Brightness and color together still do not give a full biography. Dust can redden starlight the way haze reddens a sunset. A star can be born behind a cloud. Apparent color is a start. A spectrum is better. This course will not pretend your phone camera is a spectrograph. If you use a photograph, read the caption. If the caption does not say whether the color is true, processed, or assigned to a band we cannot see, do not claim you know the surface crayon.

What a color can tell you, then, is conditional:

If the object is a glowing star (or a glowing filament), color is a rough temperature clue — bluer hotter, redder cooler — unless dust or the atmosphere has stained it.

If the object is a planet, moon, or daytime sky, color is often chemistry and scattering: rust, ice, haze, air molecules. It is not a stellar surface temperature.

If the object is a processed public image (X-ray assigned to blue, for example), the color is a legend, not a crayon the eye would have seen. Those images can still be honest if the legend is printed. They are not what Unit 8 means by “naked-eye.”

Three tests.

First: Glow or bounce? Starlight is mostly the object’s own glow. Planet-light, to the eye, is mostly bounce. Do not mix the dictionaries.

Second: Rainbow or line? A smooth color speaks to temperature for a glowing body. A thin line speaks to a particular gas. If you did not spread the light, you do not have the line.

Third: Is the caption doing work you just claimed with your eye? If yes, give the caption the credit. Your eye did not measure a redshift.

This unit stays public. No classified sensors. No “secret bands.” Sunlight, starlight, bounced planet-light, and the idea of a spectrum are enough. You will leave able to slow down at the word blue and ask which object, which kind of light, which claim.

The sky will still have color. Color is allowed. Unlabeled color is not yet a temperature.

Homework

Write it from what you saw, or from a public picture.

Write 150–200 words. Pick one public object: the Sun, a named bright star, the Moon, or Mars. Say whether you are dealing with glow or bounce. Say what a color is allowed to mean in that case, and what it is not. If you use an image, it must be a public labeled image (for example a NASA public-domain still) and you must quote or paraphrase the caption’s honesty about color. No astrology. No invented spectrum you did not take.
