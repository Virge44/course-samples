Unit 8 · Naked-eye or a public image · ~40 minutes

A short observing log

Write what you saw, then what you infer. If you did not see it, do not log it. A labeled public image is allowed. A telescope is not required.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Fill a short log: date, time, place, sky conditions, object, source (eye or public image).
  2. Split observation from inference in two labeled sentences.
  3. Refuse a fake session — no invented telescope, no unlabeled picture, no horoscope.

Lesson

A course that talks about the sky and never asks you to look is only a pamphlet. This unit asks you to look, or to open a public image, and to write a log a classmate could check. A telescope is not required. A dark mountain is not required. Honesty about conditions is required.

A log, in this 101, is a short public record. It is not a diary of awe, though awe can sit in a last optional line. The required lines are dull on purpose:

When. Local date and time, and a timezone if you know it. Night of the 12th is not enough if you mean 1 a.m. on the 13th.

Where. A place a classmate could find: campus quad, a named park, a window facing east, or “this public web image,” with the title and the archive. City-level is enough. Do not publish a private address if you do not want it on a page.

Conditions. Clear, haze, cloud, rain, indoor-only. Bright streetlights? Moon up? If you used an image, conditions means: what the caption says about filter, color, or exposure, in a phrase. “I imagined a desert” is not a condition.

Object. Moon, a named bright planet if you can justify the name, a star pattern as a region of sky (not as a fate), the Sun only with safe public practice (never stare; a projected image or a public solar still is the safe 101 path), or a labeled public photograph of a leftover world, a nebula, or a galaxy.

Source. Naked eye, or public image. If image, name the agency or archive and whether the page says public domain or similar reuse. NASA and ESA public galleries are the intended well. A random social post with no caption is not a source.

Then two sentences that this whole course has been training:

Observation: what the light showed. “The Moon was a thin crescent, horns pointing left from my window.” “The public Hubble still shows a spiral disk with dark dust lanes; the caption says the color is close to true.”

Inference: what you add from Units 1–7. “A crescent is a slice of the sunlit half, not Earth’s shadow.” “If this is a spiral galaxy, we are looking at another island, not a cloud in the Milky Way.” If you cannot tell observation from inference, write them on two lines and label the lines. The label is the skill.

What a good log refuses:

It refuses a telescope you did not use. “Through my 8-inch I saw the rings” is a fail if there was no 8-inch. It refuses a dark-sky band you did not see. It refuses astrology: no week, no sign, no compatibility. It refuses classified or military systems. It refuses a color claim a caption did not earn (Unit 4). It refuses a distance from brightness (Unit 1). It refuses a supernova Sun (Unit 6).

What a good log allows:

A failed outdoor attempt. “Clouds, 9:10 p.m., north quad; I opened a public Moon image instead” is a complete, honest log. A Sun image from a public solar observatory page. A classmate’s shared public still, if you cite it. Uncertainty: “Bright point in the west; I think it is a planet because it does not twinkle as much, but I am not sure.” Uncertainty is a claim with a hedge. A hedge is legal. A costume is not.

Safety, said once and plainly. Do not stare at the Sun. Do not use an ordinary telescope or binoculars on the Sun. This sample will not walk you through solar filters. If you want the Sun, use a public image or a projection method a live instructor has already set up. Night sky: watch your footing. That is the whole safety talk this file will give.

Why bother, if the image is already on the internet? Because the habit is not the picture. The habit is the split. The same split works on a campus argument (claim vs because) and on a sky (light vs story). This is a gen-ed close, not an observer’s certificate. I did not teach astronomy and am not certifying you. I am asking whether you can leave a record someone else could audit.

Three tests for the close.

First: Could a classmate redo the look? Time, place or URL, conditions. If those are missing, you have a mood.

Second: Are the two sentences labeled? Observation / inference. If they are mixed, unmix them.

Third: Did you invent an instrument? If yes, delete the instrument. The unit name said no telescope required. Believe it.

You will leave the course with a one-page log and a stack of conservative claims: delay, geometry, fall, light as data, leftovers, fuel, address. The log is where those claims have to sit down next to a real photon — from the sky or from a public archive. That is the whole close.

Draft two lines

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Write a one-page observing log (150–250 words). Include when, where (or URL), conditions, object, and source (naked eye or public image). Then two labeled sentences: Observation / Inference. Then one refusal: a sentence you will not claim (false distance, fake telescope, horoscope, Sun-supernova, or unlabeled color). If the sky is closed, a labeled public image is a complete substitute. Do not invent a session. Do not stare at the Sun.
