Unit 7 · A nested address · ~35 minutes

Galaxies and “where are we”

We are not the middle of our galaxy, and our galaxy is not the only island. An address is enough. A throne is not.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Give a nested address: Earth, Sun, disk of the Milky Way, Local Group.
  2. State that we live in the disk, not in the galactic center.
  3. Treat other galaxies as other islands of stars, and keep large-scale recession at caption level.

Lesson

“Where are we?” is a 101 question that invites a sermon. This unit will give an address instead. Earth orbits the Sun. The Sun is one star in a flattened city of stars, gas, and dust we call the Milky Way. We live in the disk of that city, out on a modest arm, not in the downtown middle. The Milky Way is one galaxy among others. The nearest large neighbor that a 101 names is Andromeda, a couple of million light-years away (a conservative classroom figure: about 2.5 million). A handful of smaller neighbors sit closer. Together, these make a small group — the Local Group. That is enough address for this sample.

The Milky Way as we see it from a dark place is a pale band. That band is the disk viewed from inside, the way a city glow looks thicker when you look along the street. From a lit campus you may see none of it. Not seeing the band is a light-pollution fact, not a claim that the galaxy vanished. Do not invent a dark-sky viewing you did not have. Unit 8 will ask for honesty about conditions.

We are not at the center. The center of the Milky Way is a crowded inner region tens of thousands of light-years from the Sun (a conservative figure often taught: about 25,000–27,000 light-years). The exact catalog number is less important than the refusal: the Sun is not the hub. Older public stories put us in the middle of everything. The sky does not. The band, the distances, and the orbits of stars say we are residents of a disk.

Other galaxies were once argued as nearby nebulae inside our own system. The public turn, a century ago, was to treat the bright spirals as other islands — other Milky Ways. Andromeda is the one you are allowed to name without a catalog. Through a small public telescope it is a faint patch; on a long public photograph it is a disk with dust lanes. Those two views are both light. They are not the same claim about what your eye can do from a sidewalk. Keep the instrument in the sentence.

On still larger scales, spectra of distant galaxies (Unit 4’s Doppler idea, earned by instruments) show that most far galaxies are receding, and farther ones recede faster. That is the conservative Hubble pattern. It is evidence that the space between distant islands is stretching. It is not a claim that the Milky Way sits at a unique center with everything fleeing us personally. A rising loaf of bread is the tired, still-serviceable picture: every raisin sees the others recede. We will not run a cosmology course. We will not name classified surveys. We will say: large-scale recession is a spectral result, not a naked-eye one.

A dark night sky is itself a clue. If every line of sight ended on a nearby star’s surface, the night would not be dark. The dark is compatible with a universe that is finite in age and changing — stars have not always shone in every direction. That is Olbers’ question at 101 depth. It is not a proof you will write out. It is a reason not to treat the night as an infinite wallpaper of old suns.

What you can claim, conservatively:

You can claim a nested address. You can claim we are in a disk, not the galactic center. You can claim other galaxies exist, with Andromeda as the named neighbor. You can claim that on large scales, distant galaxies recede, if you let a spectrum (or a caption that reports one) do the work.

What you cannot claim from this sample:

You cannot claim a precise headcount of stars or galaxies. Published numbers change as surveys change; a 101 that locks a cute billion is performing. You cannot claim we have mapped classified space systems. You cannot claim the universe was built for us because the address is pretty. Pretty is allowed. Purpose is a different course.

Three tests.

First: Did you put the Sun at the Milky Way’s center? If yes, move us into the disk.

Second: Did you make Andromeda a nearby nebula inside our own sky-city? It is another island, millions of light-years out.

Third: Did you see expansion with your eye? You did not. You may repeat a public spectral result. Give the method the credit.

A last address, said as a person would say it to a roommate: we live on a rocky leftover world, around a middle-aged star, in the disk of one galaxy, in a small group of galaxies, in a universe whose distant islands are receding. Every clause in that sentence was earned in this course. None of them requires a throne.

You will leave this unit able to say where we are without inflating it. Unit 8 will ask you to log one thing you actually saw — or one public image you actually opened — from somewhere on that address.

We are
In the Milky Way’s disk, far from the center.
We are not
The hub of the galaxy, or the only island.

Practice

Write the address

In one short paragraph, give the nested address: planet, star, disk, group. Do not put us at the galactic center. Do not claim a naked-eye redshift.

Homework

Write it from what you saw, or from a public picture.

Write 150–200 words. Give your nested address in full sentences. Include one refusal (not the center; not the only galaxy; or not a naked-eye expansion). If you have never seen the Milky Way band, say so — that is an honest observing condition, not a failing. No invented dark-sky trip. No classified systems. No astrology of “where we belong.”
