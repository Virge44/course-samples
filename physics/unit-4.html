Unit 4 · Bookkeeping · ~40 minutes

Energy and work

Where it went — not “energy is all around us.” Work is a force acting through a distance. Energy is the account you keep when you ask what that work bought.

3 objectives Where it went + number 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Compute a simple work or gravitational-energy number by hand (W = Fd, PE ≈ mgh with g = 10 N/kg).
  2. Tell physics-work from everyday “I was working hard” (holding a still box is not work on the box).
  3. Track where energy goes in one household event and refuse the sentence that says it disappeared.

Lesson

Work is a force through a distance

In physics, work is done on an object when a force acts on it and the object moves in the direction of that force. The unit is the joule (J). One joule is one newton of force through one meter. If you slide a box 3 meters with a steady 10-newton push along the floor (we are ignoring the messy real floor for a moment), you have done 30 joules of work on the box. That is the whole calculation: 10 × 3 = 30.

Work = force × distance along the force. Example: 10 N through 3 m → 30 J.

If you hold a 10-kilogram box still at chest height, you will get tired. Your muscles are doing messy biological work inside themselves. On the box, the physics-work is zero: the box did not move, so there is no distance. Students hate this sentence because it feels rude to a tired body. It is still the definition. If you then lift the box 0.5 meters, you have done work on it. Near Earth, its weight is about 100 N (10 kg × 10 N/kg). Work ≈ 100 N × 0.5 m = 50 J. That 50 joules did not vanish into a slogan. They are now stored as gravitational potential energy, more or less: you can get them back as motion if you let the box down carefully, or as a mess if you drop it on a bed.

Energy is the account, not the vibe

Energy is what you keep on the books so “where did it go?” has an answer. Kinetic energy is energy of motion. A heavier thing, or a faster thing, has more of it. The 101 formula, when we need a number, is ½mv² — mass in kilograms, speed in m/s, energy in joules. You do not need calculus to evaluate ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 J. Gravitational potential energy, for our hand numbers, is mgh: mass × about 10 N/kg × height in meters. A 2 kg kettlebell on a 3-meter landing (do not put one there; this is a thought number) would have about 2 × 10 × 3 = 60 J of gravitational energy relative to the floor.

Gravitational PE ≈ m × 10 × h. Example: 2 kg · 10 N/kg · 3 m = 60 J (hand g, not a lab measurement from this sample).

Conservation of energy, in this course, is bookkeeping, not a chant. In a closed account, energy changes form; the total holds. Real rooms are not closed: a warm kettle leaks heat into the kitchen; a bike radiates sound and warms its brake pads. That is not “energy disappeared.” That is energy leaving the object you were watching and entering air, metal, your hands. If you cannot name the next account, you are not done. “Energy is all around us” names no account. It is a slogan that skips the work.

Three household books, no stunts

Kettle. Electrical energy from the outlet becomes thermal energy in the water. You will meet current and voltage in Unit 7. For now: the water gets hotter; some energy leaves as steam and as warmth on the side of the kettle. Watch from a place you already stand. Do not open a pressurized cooker. Do not reach into steam. Do not heat a sealed jar.

Bike brakes. The bike and rider have kinetic energy. When you brake, that energy does not “get used up by stopping.” Friction in the pads and rims (or discs) and some sound take it. The pads warm. If you have ever smelled hot brakes after a long downslope, you have already met the account. Ride only a bike you already ride, in a place you already use. This is not a brake-test lab.

Phone onto a bed. A phone held 0.5 m above a mattress has a little gravitational energy. As it falls, that account becomes kinetic energy. When it hits the bed, the kinetic energy goes into deforming the mattress and the case, a small sound, a little heat. It does not vanish. It also does not become a reason to drop a phone off a landing or onto tile “for science.” Unit 8 will let you watch one drop onto a bed or a couch. Not a roof. Not a street.

Food energy is real and messy. A food “calorie” on a label is a chemical-energy estimate, not a physics lab you will run here. We will not convert diets into joules and we will not invent a metabolism experiment. If you mention food on the homework, say only that chemical energy in a snack can later show up as motion and as heat. Then get back to an event you can watch: the kettle, the bike, the phone on a bed.

The habit is the same as Unit 1. Name the starting account. Name the ending accounts. Name one observation that would change your mind (the water never warmed; the pads were still cold after a long brake; the phone is still 0.5 m up). If your paragraph could survive any measurement, you wrote a slogan. Rewrite it until a measurement could refuse you.

Homework

Write it. If someone is running a section, turn it in there. 200–300 words. Safe household observation only.

Track energy through one event you can actually watch this week: an electric kettle (from a safe distance, lid used as designed), a bike you already ride (braking on a path you already use), or a phone lowered or dropped a short distance onto a bed or couch. Write: the starting account, the ending accounts, one hand number if you can defend it (a height × about 10 N/kg, or a force × a distance), and one observation that would change your mind. Do not write “energy disappeared” or “energy is all around us.” Do not open appliances. Do not heat a sealed container. Do not drop anything onto tile or from a landing.
