Unit 5 · Everyday collisions · ~35 minutes

Momentum and collisions

Mass in motion. A catch, a bump, a step off a skateboard — not crash theater, not weapons, not a how-to.

3 objectives Catch + number 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Compute a simple momentum (p = mv) by hand and keep the unit (kg·m/s) and a direction.
  2. Describe a safe everyday collision (catching keys, two backpacks bumping, stepping off a skateboard) as a momentum story.
  3. Use impulse in one sentence: the same change in momentum, spread over more time, means a smaller average force — bending the knees, not building a device.

Lesson

Mass in motion

Momentum is mass times velocity. A 4 kg backpack moving at 2 m/s toward the door has momentum 8 kg·m/s toward the door. That is the whole calculation. The unit looks ugly (kilogram-meters per second). Keep it anyway. Direction stays on the sentence because velocity has a direction. A backpack coming back at 2 m/s has the same size of momentum and the opposite sign if you have chosen a positive direction.

Momentum p = m × v. Example: 4 kg × 2 m/s = 8 kg·m/s toward the door.

Why invent another word if we already have mass and velocity? Because in a bump, momentum is the account that is easy to keep. If two objects push only on each other for a short time — a closed pair, no big outside shove — the total momentum they share is the same before and after. One can lose some; the other gains that some. That is conservation of momentum, at 101 level, without a proof. It is why catching a tossed bag of flour moves you back a little. The bag’s forward momentum did not vanish. You and the bag now share it.

Stick, bounce, catch

If two carts on a smooth track hit and stick (velcro, clay — a thought picture, not a lab we ran), they move together afterward. Total momentum before equals total momentum after. Hand example: a 2 kg cart at 3 m/s hits a 1 kg cart at rest and they stick. Before: (2 × 3) + (1 × 0) = 6 kg·m/s. After: 3 kg together, so 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m/s in the same direction. If they bounce, the numbers change but the total still holds if the pair is isolated. You do not need the bounce algebra in this course. You need to refuse the sentence “the momentum disappeared when they touched.”

Catching is an everyday stick. A basketball arriving at your chest has momentum toward you. You take it, and you and the ball move a little back — or your shoes push the floor, and the floor becomes the outside force that lets the Earth join the account. That last sentence is why “closed system” is a fussy phrase: the floor is usually in the story. For homework, it is enough to say: the ball’s momentum had to go somewhere; I felt it; I stepped back or I braced through the floor. Do not throw hard objects at people “to feel momentum.” Toss a pair of socks to yourself, or notice a catch you already do (keys, a sweater, a pillow).

Impulse, without crash theater

Changing momentum requires a force acting for a time. That product — average force × time — is called impulse, and it equals the change in momentum. Same change in p, longer time, smaller average force. This is why you bend your knees when you land from a hop on a gym floor you already use. You are spreading the change in momentum over more time. It is why you pull your hands back a little when you catch an egg you were already cooking with. It is not a reason to design a bumper, test a helmet, or stage a collision. Those are engineering jobs with their own safety rules. This 101 will not teach you how to build a crumple zone and will not ask you to hit anything to “see the force.”

Safety. Everyday and thought-only. No weapons. No explosives. No “how hard could I hit.” No throwing tools. No stepping in front of bikes or cars. If you do not already skateboard, do not start for this unit. Catching keys or a pillow is enough object.

A skateboard you already own (or a wheeled chair you already sit in) shows the pair. You step off the back; the board or chair moves the other way. You pushed it; it pushed you. Momentum of you-plus-board is near zero if you started at rest; after, your forward and its backward add to about the same zero if the floor is slick enough. On carpet the floor joins the account and the demo is messier. Say so if you try it. Do not try it in a stairwell.

Energy and momentum are different books. A stick-together bump usually loses kinetic energy to deformation and heat; momentum of the pair can still hold. Students mash the two words because both show up in “collision.” On the homework, pick one book and keep it: momentum. If you mention energy, use Unit 4’s sentence — where it went — and then return to p = mv. This sample did not film carts. The 2 kg and 1 kg numbers are a teaching object, not a class average from a lab that ran.

Practice

Which has more momentum?

Toward the same door: a 4 kg bag at 2 m/s, or a 1 kg bag at 6 m/s?

Practice

After they stick

2 kg at 3 m/s hits 1 kg at rest; they stick. What is their speed after, if total momentum holds?

Practice

Same change, more time

You catch a tossed pillow. Which sentence is the impulse idea?

Homework

Write it. If someone is running a section, turn it in there. 180–280 words. Safe and everyday only.

Describe one safe collision or catch you actually do: keys, a sweater, a pillow, two backpacks bumping, stepping off a wheeled chair on a flat floor. Write p = mv once with numbers you can defend (even conservative ones). Say where that momentum went (you, the floor, the other bag). Then one impulse sentence: how you spread the change over time, if you did. Do not design a bumper. Do not throw hard objects. Do not mention weapons. If you did not measure mass or speed, say so, and write the revision test: what a kitchen scale or a phone timer would have to show for you to take the number back.
