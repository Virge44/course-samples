Unit 2 · How things move · ~40 minutes

Motion

Speed is how fast. Velocity includes a direction. Acceleration is how velocity changes. A graph is a sentence you can read, not a decoration.

3 objectives Graph + number 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Compute an average speed by hand (distance ÷ time) and keep the unit.
  2. Tell speed from velocity, and velocity from acceleration, in one everyday trip.
  3. Read a simple position–time or velocity–time graph: say what the slope is doing in words, then give a number.

Lesson

Distance, time, speed

If you travel 90 kilometers in 1.5 hours and you want average speed, you divide: 90 ÷ 1.5 = 60. The unit is kilometers per hour. That is the whole calculation. Average speed is total distance divided by total time. It does not claim you held 60 the whole way. It claims that if your motion were spread evenly across that time, that is the speed that would match the distance.

Average speed = distance ÷ time. Example: 90 km ÷ 1.5 h = 60 km/h.

Distance is how much ground you covered, path and all. Displacement is the straight change from start to finish, with a direction. Walk 200 meters down a hallway and 200 meters back. Distance: 400 m. Displacement: 0 m, because you ended where you began. Average speed used the 400. Average velocity used the 0. Students mix these up because everyday English uses “speed” and “velocity” as twins. In this course they are not twins. Velocity is speed with a direction, or — more carefully — displacement divided by time.

Velocity is not a longer word for speed

A bus doing 12 m/s north and a bus doing 12 m/s south have the same speed and opposite velocities. If you only say “12,” you have dropped both the unit and the direction. If a problem in this 101 asks for velocity, give a number, a unit, and a direction a classmate could point: toward the river, east along Market, down the stairs. “Forward” only works if you have already named the nose of the object.

Acceleration is how velocity changes. Speeding up is acceleration. Slowing down is also acceleration — the velocity is changing. Turning at a steady speed is acceleration too, because direction is part of velocity. That last sentence surprises people who think acceleration means “the pedal.” In this course, if velocity changed, there was acceleration. The everyday word “deceleration” is allowed as a courtesy for slowing down. The physics job is the same: velocity changed.

Average acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time. Hand example: 0 to 10 m/s in 5 s is 2 m/s².

Near Earth, if you ignore air, a falling object’s speed increases by about 10 meters per second each second. That 10 is a hand number (about 9.8 in a more precise book). A phone that has been falling for 1 second is going about 10 m/s if air is ignored; after 2 seconds, about 20 m/s. In a real room, air matters more for a dropped sheet of paper than for a phone. This sample will not claim a feather and a phone hit a dorm carpet together. They do not. Unit 8 will let you watch one drop onto a bed — not off a roof, not into a street.

A graph is a sentence

A position–time graph plots where something is (vertical) against clock time (horizontal). A straight line that climbs steadily means constant velocity: equal distance in equal time. A flat line means it sat still — position is not changing. A curve that gets steeper means it is speeding up. You do not need calculus to say that. You need to look: is the line’s tilt staying the same, or is it changing?

The slope of a position–time graph is the velocity. In words: how much position changes for each second on the clock. The slope of a velocity–time graph is the acceleration. A velocity–time graph that sits at a constant height is constant velocity — acceleration zero — even if that height is not zero. Students see a high flat line and say “it’s accelerating because the line is up there.” No. Up there means it is moving. Flat means the velocity is not changing.

Read that graph out loud: “In 10 seconds the bike’s position increased 80 meters at a steady rate, so its velocity was 8 m/s along the path.” That is graph literacy. “The line goes up” is a tourist plaque. You will practice choosing the sentence that reads the slope, then doing one hand number. If a later homework asks you to sketch, a clean straight line with labeled axes is enough. This is not an art class and not a motion-capture lab. No one here filmed a bike. The picture is a classroom object.

A last caution, because mixed-prep gen-ed is the audience. You do not need the word “derivative.” You need: what is changing, how fast, and in what direction. If you can say that about a walk you already take — campus to the store, bus stop to the door — you are doing the unit. Do not stand in a street to time a car. Do not chase a measurement into traffic. Use a trip you already survive.

Practice

Read the bike graph

Use the position–time graph above (0 m at 0 s, 80 m at 10 s, straight line). Which sentence reads it?

Practice

A speed you can do by hand

A bus covers 90 km in 1.5 hours. What is the average speed in km/h? Type the number only.

Practice

Speed, velocity, or acceleration?

A student walks east at a steady 1.4 m/s, then slows to a stop at the door. Which statement is careful?

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words. Do not stand in a street to time traffic.

Describe one trip you already take (a walk, a bus ride, a bike you already ride). Give: (1) a distance and a time you can defend, and the average speed with a unit; (2) one sentence of velocity that includes a direction; (3) one moment velocity changed, so there was acceleration, in plain words. Then write one graph sentence: if you plotted position versus time for the steady part, what would the slope be, as a number and a unit? No “the line goes up.” No invented lab table. If you did not time it, say so, and use a conservative estimate you would revise if a classmate’s phone timer disagreed.
