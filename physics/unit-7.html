Unit 7 · A complete path · ~40 minutes

Electricity at the outlet

Charge, current, a circuit as a job. You will look at a lamp you already use. You will not open anything.

3 objectives Path + switch 1 homework

Safety — non-negotiable

Do not open an outlet, a switch plate, a charger, a power strip, or an appliance housing. Do not poke a receptacle. Do not “test” a socket with a paper clip, a meter you have not been trained to use, or a finger. Do not take apart a string of lights. Do not wire anything. This unit is a thought model plus looking at a closed, ordinary lamp. If a cord is damaged, unplug it from the appliance end if you can do so as you already would, and tell whoever owns the building. This course will not teach you how to repair it.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Give charge, current, and voltage a job in plain language (what is flowing, how much per second, what pushes).
  2. Explain a lamp and a switch as a circuit job: a complete path; energy converted at the device; charge not “used up.”
  3. Tell a series story from a parallel story at household level (one shared path vs separate paths) without opening a fixture.

Lesson

Charge is a property; current is a job

Electric charge comes in two kinds we still call positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. That sentence is 101-level and enough. We will not build a Van de Graaff or rub balloons into a research program. You have already felt a sock-on-carpet snap. That snap is charge rearranging through a tiny path, then gone as a spark and a bit of heat and light. Do not chase bigger sparks.

Current is charge flowing past a point, measured in amperes (A). A larger current means more charge per second. Voltage is the energy per charge the source can offer — the “push,” if you want a hallway word — measured in volts (V). A typical U.S. household receptacle is about 120 V. That 120 is a conservative handbook number for this country, not a measurement you will take. You will not measure it. A 1.5 V flashlight cell and a 120 V outlet are not “a little different.” They are different jobs. Do not mix their worlds. Do not put household voltage on a flashlight bulb as a test.

A circuit job, in one line: a source (energy in), a complete path, a device that converts energy (light, heat, motion), and usually a switch that opens or closes the path.

The circuit is a loop, not a store that empties

Charge is not fuel that the lamp burns up. Charge goes around. Energy is what the lamp converts: electrical energy becomes light and heat. If you imagine a single file of carts on a loop of track, the carts are charge. The motor that keeps them moving is the source. The lamp is a stretch of rough track that takes energy out of the carts as they pass. The carts return. Students write “the electricity gets used up in the bulb, so nothing is left for the second bulb.” That sentence is a broken model. In a single loop (series), the same current goes through both devices; they share the energy the source can offer. In a house, lamps are mostly on separate paths (parallel): one lamp off does not shut the others, because each path can still close back to the source.

A switch is not magic and not a volume knob by itself. It opens the path (off) or closes it (on). When the path is open, current in that loop is about zero and the lamp is dark. When you flip a wall switch for a lamp you already own, you are completing or breaking a designed path. You are not “letting electricity leak into the room.” You do not need to see the wires. You need to refuse the leak story and the used-up story.

Series and parallel, without a wiring lesson

Some holiday strings are series: one dead bulb can darken the rest because the one path broke. Many household lamps are parallel: separate paths, so the kitchen ceiling can be on while a desk lamp is off. That is the whole distinction you owe this 101. It is not a license to rewire a string or to open a fixture to “see which kind.” If a string dies and you do not already know the designed way to replace a bulb, you leave it. Observation of behavior — one lamp off, others still on — is enough evidence for “separate paths.” Observation of a whole string dying at one bulb is enough for “one shared path,” if that is the string you already have. Do not buy new hardware for this course.

Power, if you need the word, is how fast energy is converted, in watts (W). A label on a closed lamp that says 10 W is a rate. We will not compute a monthly bill here and we will not invent a kill-a-watt lab. If you read a label, you are reading a claim the manufacturer made. You may use it as a quantity with a unit. You may not open the lamp to verify it.

Unit 4’s bookkeeping still applies. The outlet is not a fountain of “energy all around us.” It is a designed connection to a source that can do work on charge. The lamp is where that energy becomes light and heat you can feel from a polite distance. If the lamp is dark after you close the switch you already use, the revision test is ordinary: is it plugged in as designed? is the bulb a type that lamp takes? is a building breaker already tripped — in which case you tell whoever owns the building, and you do not reset a panel you have not been trained to reset? This course ends at the explanation. It does not begin a repair.

I am not an electrician and did not teach physics as a content expert. The facts on this page are conservative on purpose. Homework is a paragraph about a closed lamp and a switch you already use. If you do not have a lamp, use a closed phone charger you already own: plug seated, brick warm after a while, phone’s battery percent rising. Still do not open the brick.

Practice

Used up?

A lamp is on. What is the careful sentence about charge and energy?

Practice

What the switch did

You flip a working lamp off. Which model holds?

Practice

One lamp off, the other on

Kitchen ceiling on; desk lamp off. What does that behavior already tell you?

Homework

Write it. If someone is running a section, turn it in there. 180–280 words. Closed devices only.

Watch one closed, ordinary thing you already use: a lamp and its switch, or a phone and its charger brick. Write the circuit job: source, path, device, switch (or plug as the thing that completes the path). Say what is converted (light, heat, chemical energy in a battery). Say what is not “used up” (charge). One revision test (what you would check, without opening anything, if it failed). Do not open housings. Do not poke a receptacle. Do not describe a repair. Do not give wiring instructions. If you have no lamp and no charger, write from a room light you already use and say you did not touch the fixture.
