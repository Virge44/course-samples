Unit 6 · A traveling disturbance · ~35 minutes

Waves and sound

The disturbance travels. The air mostly does not. Frequency, wavelength, amplitude — jobs, not a vocab dump.

3 objectives Wave words + v = fλ 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Describe a wave as a traveling disturbance and say what the medium is doing (mostly bobbing, not riding to the wall).
  2. Give frequency, wavelength, and amplitude a job in one sound you can actually hear.
  3. Compute once with v = fλ, using a conservative hand number (sound in room-temperature air ≈ 340 m/s).

Lesson

The thing that travels is the pattern

A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium, or — for light, which is not this unit’s object — through empty space. If you flick a jump rope that is already tied to a stair rail, a pulse runs down the rope. The pulse arrives. A bit of rope mostly moves up and down and stays in its neighborhood. Students write “the rope went to the rail.” A piece of tape on the rope would prove them wrong. The pattern traveled. The tape did not commute.

Sound is a pressure wave in a material, usually air in this 101. Your voice shoves a nearby bit of air; that bit shoves the next; a listener’s eardrum is pushed later. The air in the middle of the room does not walk over to their ear. If it did, a conversation would be a breeze. In a closed room you can talk without papers flying. That everyday fact is already the lesson: energy and a pattern travel; the medium mostly jiggles in place.

Sound needs a medium. A common 101 picture is a bell in a jar as the air is pumped out — the sound fades. This sample did not run that demo. Treat it as textbook lore, not as a lab you owe us. You already know a weaker version: a closed window cuts street noise because you have changed the path and the media the wave has to cross. Do not put a speaker in a vacuum chamber. Do not build a chamber.

Three numbers, three jobs

Frequency (f) is how many cycles pass a point each second. The unit is the hertz (Hz): one cycle per second. Pitch, in everyday talk, tracks frequency. A higher-pitched voice or a tighter guitar string (a string you already own, played as you already play it) is a higher frequency. Wavelength (λ) is the distance from one crest to the next, or from one compression to the next in a sound wave. Amplitude is how big the disturbance is — how far the rope moves from its quiet line, or how much the pressure swings. Loudness, in everyday talk, tracks amplitude. A shout and a whisper can share a pitch and differ in amplitude. They are not the same job.

The three are related by a sentence you can compute by hand:

Wave speed v = f × λ. Example: 2 Hz × 0.5 m = 1 m/s along a rope. For sound in room-temperature air, use about 340 m/s (a handbook approximation, not a measurement from this sample).

If sound travels at about 340 m/s and a tone has frequency 170 Hz, the wavelength is 340 ÷ 170 = 2 meters. If someone tells you a 500 Hz tone in air has a 10-meter wavelength, the product would be 5000 m/s — not air. The formula is a check, not a chant. Frequency up, wavelength down, if the speed of the medium stays the same. That is why a shorter guitar string, tension being similar, tends to sound higher: the wave on the string is forced into a shorter λ, so f rises if v does not drop as much.

Echo, without a canyon stunt

An echo is a sound wave that hit something and came back. Distance to the wall is about speed × time ÷ 2, because the wave ran out and back. If you clap once in a large empty hallway and the return is 0.4 s later, a conservative estimate is 340 × 0.4 ÷ 2 ≈ 68 meters to the far surface. That is a thought check. Do not wander into a parking deck at night to hunt echoes. Do not stand in a street. If you have a gym or a long hall you already walk, one clap is enough. If you do not, skip the clap and use the 0.4 s / 68 m sentence as a teaching object — labeled as such, not as your data.

Light is a wave (and a particle, in a later course) and does not need air. We will not pretend this 101 settled that. One sentence so you do not mash the two: you can see a lamp in a room; you cannot hear a shout from the Moon. Different jobs. Unit 7 will stay with charge and a household path. This unit stays with a sound you can hear without equipment: your own voice, a kettle click, a bus braking outside a closed window.

Safety. Listen to sounds you already hear. Do not take apart a speaker. Do not put a sound source in your ear. Do not stand near traffic or a track to “hear Doppler.” Doppler is real and we are not running it as a field trip.

Homework will ask you to give one real sound three jobs: a frequency word (higher/lower than your speaking voice, or a number if a phone app you already have shows one — optional), a wavelength comment if you use 340 m/s, and an amplitude word (quiet/loud, and what would change your mind). If you did not measure, say so. Inventing a tidy 256.3 Hz because it looks scientific is the opposite of Unit 1. This page invented the 2 Hz rope and the 170 Hz tone as classroom objects. They are not student records.

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 180–280 words.

Pick one sound you already hear this week: a kettle click, your own voice in a stairwell, a bus outside a closed window, a string you already play. Write three jobs: frequency (higher/lower than your speaking voice, or a number from a tool you already have), amplitude (quiet/loud), and one v = fλ sentence using about 340 m/s if it is air sound — or say why you will not fake a wavelength. Then one revision test. Do not take a speaker apart. Do not stand in a street or by a track. If you did not measure a frequency, do not invent 256.3 Hz. Invented tidy data is not a physical claim.
