Unit 1 · The claim type · ~35 minutes

What a physical claim is

Not “the hallway feels long.” A quantity, a unit, and the observation that would force you to take the sentence back.

3 objectives Sort + rewrite 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish a physical claim (measurable, with a unit) from a vibe (“it feels”) or a slogan (“energy is all around us”).
  2. Name the three parts a working physical claim needs: a quantity, a unit, and one observation that would force a revision.
  3. Rewrite a casual sentence so a classmate could check it with a tool they already have — a phone timer, a meter stick, a kitchen scale.

Lesson

A sentence the world can refuse

A physical claim is a sentence you can check. It names something you could measure, a unit that sentence would use, and what you would accept as a reason to take it back. “This hallway feels long” is a mood. “This hallway is 28 meters from the stair door to the elevator” is a physical claim. If a tape measure, used the same way twice, reads 19 meters, the claim is wrong. That is not a personality contest. The hallway does not care how it felt when you were late.

This course is conceptual physics with numbers you can do by hand. No calculus. You will not derive a law. You will say what a sentence claims, in what unit, and what would force a revision. Same public habit as a writing-class claim that can be wrong — except here the refusal is a measurement.

A unit is a job, not decoration

“Three” is not a physical claim. Three what? Three seconds is a duration. Three meters is a length. Three kilograms is a mass. If you drop the unit, you have a number looking for a job. First-year papers do this on schedule: “the speed was 20,” “it weighed 5.” Twenty kilometers per hour and twenty meters per second are not the same motion. Five kilograms is a mass; five newtons is a force. Near Earth’s surface, a 1-kilogram bag of flour has a weight of about 10 newtons. That 10 is a hand number for this course (about 10 newtons of weight for each kilogram of mass). The more precise 9.8 is real; this sample will not pretend a lab here measured it. If your sentence cannot say which of those you mean, it is not ready to be checked.

The units you will actually need in this 101:

  • meter (m) for length
  • second (s) for time
  • kilogram (kg) for mass
  • meter per second (m/s) for speed
  • meter per second squared (m/s²) for acceleration
  • newton (N) for force
  • joule (J) for energy and for work
  • ampere (A) for current and volt (V) for voltage, late in the course

Refuse a number with no unit. You do not need a conversion table for this 101.

What would change your mind

A claim that cannot be revised is a slogan. “Energy is all around us” cannot be wrong, which is why it is useless here. “The electric kettle ran long enough to take 1 liter of cool tap water to a rolling boil” can be wrong: if the water is still cool after ten minutes of a working outlet and a closed lid, something in the claim failed. You do not need a research-group notebook. You need a sentence that names the observation that would force a rewrite.

Measurement is a procedure, not a vibe with a ruler nearby. “I measured the table” is not yet a claim. How? From which edge? In centimeters or inches? Did you read the near side of the mark or the far side? Two people can disagree by a centimeter without anyone lying. That disagreement is not “science is just opinion.” It is a reason to say the procedure out loud. In this course, a working physical claim has three parts you can point at: the quantity (what kind of thing), the unit (the scale), and the revision test (what you would accept as a reason to take it back).

Teaching object · invented classroom sentences

Four sentences a first-year might say about the same walk across a quad. They are written for this sample. They are not student records and not data from a lab that ran.

  1. “That walk felt like forever.”
  2. “Motion is a fundamental part of the universe.”
  3. “I crossed the quad in 80.”
  4. “I crossed the 120-meter path in 80 seconds, so my average speed was 1.5 meters per second. If a classmate’s phone timer and a long tape both said I used 2 minutes, I would drop the 80-second claim.”

Invented for this sample. Sentence 4 is the physical claim: quantity (average speed), unit (m/s), revision test (timer and tape disagree).

Precision is not the same as being right

Precision is how tightly repeated measurements cluster. Accuracy is whether they are close to the thing you meant to measure. A clock that is consistently four minutes fast is precise and wrong. Two phone-timer readings of 18 s and 19 s are a cluster. If you timed the wrong door, you have a tidy wrong number.

The habit of the course is small and public. Say the quantity. Say the unit. Say what would change your mind. If nobody could disagree by pointing at a measurement, you filled a page. Later units apply that habit to a bike, a kettle, a circuit, a phone that falls onto a bed. They will not give you a slogan that survives any measurement, or a lab you did not do.

Hand numbers later are labeled. Your job is a sentence a measurement can refuse.

Practice

Which sentence is the physical claim?

Same situation: a walk across a quad. Pick the sentence that names a quantity, a unit, and what would change the speaker’s mind.

Practice

Rewrite a slogan

Slogan: “The library stairs are so steep. Gravity is intense in this building.”

Rewrite it as one physical claim. Name a quantity (height, time, speed, or a count of steps). Name a unit. Name one observation that would force a revision. Avoid “feels,” “intense,” “gravity is just a theory,” and any number with no unit.

Homework

Write it. If someone is running a section, turn it in there. 200–300 words.

Take two sentences you have actually said or heard this week — about a walk, a wait, a bag, a phone, a room’s temperature. One should be a vibe or a slogan. Rewrite both as physical claims. Then write one paragraph: which rewrite could a measurement refuse, and which one is still a souvenir? Do not use “energy is all around us,” “science is just a theory,” or a number with no unit. Name the quantity. Name the unit. Name what would change your mind.
