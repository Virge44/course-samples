Unit 8 · One real observable · ~45 minutes

A short investigation writeup

You pick one thing you can actually watch: a phone onto a bed, a kettle from a safe distance, a bike you already ride. Claim, measure, what would change your mind. Not a fake lab table.

3 objectives Spot the fake + write 1 homework

Safety — pick only from this list

  • Phone: a short drop onto a bed or couch, from a height you can reach while standing on the floor. Not a roof. Not tile. Not a stairwell.
  • Kettle: an electric or stovetop kettle used as designed. Watch from where you already stand. Lid as designed. No sealed jars. No reaching into steam. No pressurized cooker.
  • Bike: a bike you already ride, on a path you already use. Time a stretch you already travel. No traffic stunts. No brake tests on a hill you do not already know.

If none of those is available, a dropped sock onto a rug, or a walk you already take with a phone timer, is enough. Thought-plus-safe-household only. No weapons, no explosives, no “how to build.”

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Choose one real, safe observable and write a physical claim about it (quantity, unit, revision test).
  2. Describe a measurement you actually made — or honestly mark as an estimate you would revise.
  3. Refuse a writeup that invents a tidy table, uses a slogan, or asks for an unsafe stunt.

Lesson

Not a lab-report costume

An investigation, here, is a short public writeup of one thing you watched. It is not a fifteen-page methods section. It is not a data table you invented because lab reports you have seen had tables. It is Unit 1 applied to a real object: a claim the world can refuse, a procedure you can say out loud, and a result you would take back if the procedure came out otherwise.

Five parts, in order. (1) What you watched, and why it is on the safety list. (2) The physical claim, written before or as you measure — quantity, unit, revision test. (3) How you measured: which tool, from where, how many times. A phone timer and a meter stick or a known floor tile are enough. (4) What you actually saw, including the messy part. (5) What you now believe, and what would still change your mind. If part 4 is tidier than your life, you probably wrote part 4 before you looked. That is the costume. Take it off.

You may use one idea from an earlier unit. A phone drop is motion and energy. A kettle is energy and, if you talk about the click, a bit of sound. A bike stretch is speed and, if you brake, energy bookkeeping. Do not drag all eight units into one paragraph. One job, done in public.

A teaching model — not your data, not a lab that ran

Teaching object · invented writeup

I dropped my phone from about hip height onto a made bed, standing on the floor. Claim: the drop takes about 0.4 seconds from release to the first mattress sound, so the average speed during the fall is on the order of 1 m/s if the fall is about 0.8 m (distance ÷ time). I would take the 0.4 s back if three phone-timer tries, started on release and stopped on the sound, all landed closer to 0.2 s or closer to 0.8 s.

Procedure: I used the phone’s stopwatch, held in the other hand — which is already a problem, because I cannot drop and start with the same thumb cleanly. I did three drops onto the duvet, not the headboard, not the floor. I estimated height with a meter stick from the mattress to my hip. I did not climb. I did not use tile.

What I saw: 0.52 s, 0.41 s, 0.58 s. The cluster is messy. Average of those three is about 0.5 s, not 0.4 s. Height about 0.8 m. Average speed then about 0.8 ÷ 0.5 = 1.6 m/s. That is an average, not the speed at impact. Air is not the main story for a phone over half a second, but my reaction time on the timer is. The 0.4 s claim is already in trouble.

I now think the drop is closer to half a second with this sloppy timer method, and the honest revision test is a second person timing or a slow-motion video I already know how to take. I would not write a table of 0.400, 0.401, 0.399. I did not see that. Energy bookkeeping, one line: gravitational energy (about 0.2 kg × 10 N/kg × 0.8 m ≈ 1.6 J, if the phone is about 200 g) became kinetic energy and then mattress deformation, sound, and a little heat. I did not measure the joule. I will not pretend I did.

Invented for this sample as a model of tone: messy times, a claim under revision, no fake precision. Not student work. Not data from a lab at NC A&T. I did not run this as a physics section.

What to refuse

Refuse a writeup that could have been typed without looking: “Energy was conserved. The phone fell at 9.8 m/s². Gravity is a fundamental force. Table 1: Trial 1 0.400 s, Trial 2 0.400 s, Trial 3 0.400 s.” That is a souvenir with a costume table. Refuse a writeup that leaves the safety list: a drop from a landing, a kettle with the lid jammed, a bike in a live lane. Refuse a paragraph that never names a unit. Refuse “I feel like it was fast.” Feeling can start the note; it cannot finish the claim.

You are not being asked to confirm a textbook number. About 10 m/s² is a handbook figure if air is ignored. A hip-height drop onto a duvet, timed with a thumb, will not measure it cleanly. If you disagree with a handbook, suspect your method first. “My data prove Einstein wrong” after three thumb-times is not a physical claim.

Kettle: time to a rolling boil, the click-off, or steam and sound from a polite distance — do not touch a hot side to “measure.” Bike: one stretch you already ride, a distance you can defend, a phone time, average speed with a unit. One stretch is enough.

The course ends when you can hand a stranger a page that names a thing, a number with a unit, and the observation that would force a rewrite. You owe the page one real look. Then you write.

Homework — the investigation

Write it. If someone is running a section, turn it in there. 250–400 words. Do the looking first.

Write a short investigation of one safe observable from the list (phone onto a bed/couch, kettle used as designed, bike you already ride — or a sock onto a rug / a walk you already take). Use the five parts: what you watched and why it is safe; the physical claim (quantity, unit, revision test); how you measured; what you actually saw, messy if messy; what you now believe and what would still change your mind. You may use one idea from an earlier unit. Do not invent a tidy table. Do not write “energy is all around us.” Do not leave the safety list. If you did not look, do not submit a costume.
