Unit 3 · Pushes, pulls, pairs · ~40 minutes

Force and Newton in plain language

A force is a push or a pull that can change motion. The three statements are tools, not a biography and not a plaque about an apple.

3 objectives Which law + number 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Name the forces that matter in an everyday scene (gravity, a table, friction, a hand, a seatbelt) without turning it into a slogan.
  2. Say which of Newton’s three statements is doing the work in that scene, in plain language.
  3. Compute one acceleration from F = ma by hand (newtons, kilograms, m/s²).

Lesson

A force is a job, not a mood

A force is a push or a pull. It has a size and a direction. The unit is the newton (N). Near Earth, each kilogram of mass weighs about 10 newtons — another hand number, labeled as such. A 2-kilogram bag of flour is not “2 of force.” Its mass is 2 kg. Earth pulls down on it with about 20 N. Mass is how much stuff; weight is the pull. They are related. They are not the same word.

Forces can change an object’s velocity: start it, stop it, speed it, slow it, or turn it. If the forces on an object cancel — if they add to a net force of zero — the velocity does not change. That includes sitting still. Sitting still is not “no forces.” Sitting still is forces that cancel. A book on a table has Earth pulling down and the table pushing up. The book is not moving. The net force is about zero. Students write “there is no force on the book” because it looks peaceful. Peaceful is not the measurement.

Three statements, not a saint’s life

You do not need Newton’s biography. You need three tools that still earn their keep in a hallway.

First. An object keeps its velocity — including staying at rest — unless a net force acts on it. A hockey puck on smooth ice keeps sliding because the net force is small. A box on a sidewalk stops because friction is not small. Everyday life hides the first statement under friction and air. A seatbelt is the first statement made visible: when the car slows, you keep your velocity until a force (the belt, or something worse) changes it. That is not a dare to test a belt. It is a reason the belt has a job.

Second. A net force changes velocity. For a given object, more net force means more acceleration. For a given net force, more mass means less acceleration. In symbols, net force = mass × acceleration, or a = F ÷ m. The units match if force is in newtons, mass in kilograms, acceleration in m/s². Hand example: a net force of 6 N on a 2 kg cart gives 3 m/s². That is the whole calculation. This course will not ask you to integrate a variable force.

Net force = mass × acceleration. Example: 6 N on 2 kg → 3 m/s² in the direction of the net force.

Third. Forces come in pairs. If you push a wall, the wall pushes you. If Earth pulls a book down, the book pulls Earth up (the Earth does not noticeably accelerate toward the book because Earth’s mass is enormous — the second statement, applied to Earth). The pair acts on two different objects. That is the part first-year papers skip. “The table’s upward push and gravity’s downward pull on the book cancel” is a first-statement / net-force story about one object. It is not the third-statement pair. The pair to “table pushes book up” is “book pushes table down.” Same size, opposite direction, different objects. If you mash those stories together, you will write that forces cancel so nothing can ever start walking. People walk anyway.

Walking, without a myth

To start walking, you push the floor backward with your foot. Friction lets that push happen instead of a slip. The floor pushes you forward — that is the force that speeds you up. You are not “pulling yourself forward by will.” Will is not a newton. The floor’s forward push is. If the floor is ice and friction is tiny, the pair is tiny and you slip. That is the third statement in shoes, not a motivational poster.

A last conservative caution. “Gravity is just a theory” is a slogan, not a force diagram. In this 101, gravity near Earth is the downward pull we approximate as 10 N per kilogram. We are not settling a philosophy fight and we are not running a drop tower. We are naming a force you can feel as weight, measuring mass in kilograms, and refusing to call them the same thing. Do not build a device to “test gravity.” Do not drop anything off a landing. A book already on a table is enough object for this unit.

When you write a scene for homework, pick something you already do: a backpack on a chair, a phone on a palm, a bike you already ride at a speed you already use. Name two or three forces. Say which statement is doing the work. If you cannot say whether you are talking about one object’s net force or a pair on two objects, you are not done. The apple story can wait in the gift shop.

Practice

Which statement is doing the work?

A car slows. The rider keeps moving forward until the seatbelt pulls back. Which tool is the belt using?

Practice

Name the pair

A book sits still on a table. Earth pulls the book down. What is the third-statement partner of that pull?

Practice

One F = ma by hand

A net force of 6 N acts on a 2 kg cart. What is the acceleration in m/s²? Number only.

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words. Do not build anything. Do not drop objects off a landing.

Pick one everyday scene you can see without arranging a stunt: a backpack on a chair, a phone on your palm, standing still, starting to walk, a bike you already ride. Name the object. Name two or three forces on it (direction and about how large if you can, using ~10 N per kilogram for weight). Say whether the net force is about zero or not, and what that means for velocity. Then name one third-statement pair (two objects). Do not tell the apple story. Do not write “gravity is just a theory.” If you use a number, give it a unit.
