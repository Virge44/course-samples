Who it is for

First-year college students, mixed preparation, often in a gen-ed seat they did not choose. You do not need to like “science.” You need to stay with one measurable claim long enough to say how you would check it. No calculus is required. If a number appears, you can do it with a pencil: divide, multiply, keep the unit.

If you already write fluent slogan papers (“energy is all around us,” “Newton discovered motion”), this course will still slow you down. A slogan is not an explanation. If algebra makes you freeze, start with Unit 1 and the graph-reading in Unit 2. The sequence is the course; the units are short enough to finish tired.

What physics is, here

Physics, in this 101, is a public habit of making a claim the world can refuse. A vibe question asks how the hallway felt. A slogan question asks you to praise a law so you can leave. A physical question names a quantity, a unit, and the observation that would force a rewrite.

Say what you are measuring. Say the unit. Say what would change your mind. If nobody could disagree by pointing at a measurement, you filled a page.

Course outcomes

By the end of the eight units, you should be able to:

  1. Write a physical claim that names a quantity, a unit, and one observation that would force a revision.
  2. Read a simple position–time or velocity–time graph, and compute a speed by hand.
  3. Name the forces in an everyday situation and say which of Newton’s three statements is doing the work.
  4. Track where energy goes in a household event — work and bookkeeping, not a slogan.
  5. Use momentum to describe a safe, everyday collision, including one hand calculation.
  6. Describe a sound with wave words (frequency, wavelength, amplitude) and one v = fλ calculation.
  7. Explain a lamp and a switch as a circuit job — charge, current, a complete path — without opening an outlet.
  8. Write a short investigation of one real, safe observable (a falling phone onto a bed, a kettle watched from a distance, a bike you already ride).

Safety — read this once

Thought labs and safe household observation only. Do not open outlets, panels, or appliance housings. Do not climb for a “better drop.” Do not heat a sealed container. Do not stand in a street to time a car. No weapons, no explosives, no “how to build.” If a prompt asks you to observe, it means look at something you already use, from a place you already stand.

Sequence

Semester-shaped, compressed. Do the units in order. Each one is a lesson you can finish in one sitting, a clickable practice with feedback, and one homework you could actually turn in. Total student time if you do the practices: about four to six hours. This is not fifteen weeks of textbook coverage and not a lab course.

How the units work

Each unit has three measurable objectives, lesson prose a tired first-year can finish (about 600–900 words), clickable practice that talks back, and one homework of roughly 150–350 words (Unit 8 is the investigation writeup). Practice is not a quiz for a score. It is a move you will need on the homework.

If an instructor is running this async or hybrid, turn homework in where they say — an LMS journal, a shared folder, paper. If you are working alone, write it anyway. The course does not store it.

Hand numbers used on purpose: near Earth, weight ≈ 10 N for each kilogram of mass; free-fall speed increases by about 10 m/s each second if you ignore air; sound in room-temperature air is about 340 m/s; a typical U.S. household outlet is about 120 V. When a page uses those, it says they are approximations. This sample does not invent a lab that measured them.
