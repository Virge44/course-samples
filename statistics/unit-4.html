Unit 4 · Who is missing · ~40 minutes

Samples and bias

A sample is the part you counted. A population is the group you want to talk about. Bias is a who, not a mood.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Tell a sample from a population in a short description.
  2. Name a group a convenience or volunteer sample did not reach.
  3. Write a two-sentence limit: what the sample can support, and what it cannot.

Lesson

A population is the whole group you want to talk about. A sample is the part you actually counted. Almost every number you meet is a sample wearing a population coat. The coat is allowed if you say so. The coat is a costume if you do not.

This is still a gen-ed sample, not a survey-methods course and not a statistics-department appointment. You will not draw a random-number table. You will name who is missing. Analytical Reasoning 101 already asked you for a base. This unit asks who never made it into the base.

Start with a convenience sample, because that is what most of us actually have. EXAMPLE (invented teaching story, not a campus poll): a clipboard at one door, one afternoon, 30 people who stopped. Those 30 are a sample of people who walked through that door, had time, and were willing. They are not a sample of night-shift workers, of people who use another door, of people who dislike clipboards. The number can still be honest: “Of 30 people who stopped at the east door Tuesday, 22 said X.” The leap to “students think X” is the part that needs a trial.

Bias, in this unit, is not a synonym for meanness. It is a systematic tilt: some people are easier to count than others, and the easy people are not a miniature of the whole. Self-selection is a tilt (who volunteers). Undercoverage is a tilt (who has no phone, no app, no address on the list). Nonresponse is a tilt (who sees the form and walks by). Each tilt is a who.

Random sampling is a method for giving each person in a defined population a known chance to be in the sample. It is not a feeling, and it is not “we asked whoever was around.” A messy random sample with a clear population can beat a huge convenience sample that feels official. Size is not a cure for a tilt. A thousand people at one door are still one door.

You will hear “representative.” Ask: representative of what, on which features? A sample can match a city on age and miss it on night work. A sample of households can miss people who do not live in households. Representation is a claim with a checklist, not a compliment you pay a dataset to make it behave.

Voluntary response is a special costume. People with strong feelings show up. That can be exactly what you want if the question is “who is angry enough to write.” It is a bad coat for “what does the public think.” A call-in number, a quote-tweet, a comment box: those are samples of people who reached for the handle. Write that in the sentence.

Missing is sometimes the finding. If a form is only in one language, the missing group is people who do not read that language. If a survey runs at 2 p.m., the missing group is people who work that block. If a health count uses clinic visits, people who cannot get to a clinic are not in the count. You do not have to fix the whole design in a 101. You have to name the hole.

A census tries to count everyone in the population. Even a census misses people, and the misses are not random — that is a live research problem, not a slogan. For this course, the usable distinction is simpler: did they try to count the whole defined group, or a part? If a part, which part, and who is obviously not in it?

The step a classmate could check is a sentence: “This sample is n = ___, taken how, from whom. It can support a claim about that group. It cannot, by itself, support a claim about ___.” Put the missing group in the last blank. If you cannot name a missing group, you have not yet looked.

Practice: tell sample from population, name who is missing from a convenience clipboard, and write the missing group in a sentence. Homework: point at a real public survey or count — census, labor, weather stations, a city comment portal — and say who is missing. Do not invent a school poll.

Homework

Point to a real public count or survey you can name or link: a Census table, a Bureau of Labor Statistics release, a National Weather Service station, a city comment portal, a posted transit ridership count. Do not invent a campus poll. In 150–220 words: (1) What is the population the source seems to want? (2) What is the sample they actually have, as far as you can tell? (3) Name one missing group — a who, not the word bias. (4) Write the two-sentence limit from this unit. If the source is close to a census, say what a census can still miss.
