Unit 1 · Who was counted · ~40 minutes

A number is a claim

A number asks to be believed about a group. If you cannot name who was counted, you do not yet have a statistic.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Restate a number as a claim about a defined group, including the count n when it is given.
  2. Name who was counted and who was left out of a short rate or percent.
  3. Rewrite a headline-style number so the denominator sits in the same sentence.

Lesson

A number looks finished. It is not. A number is a claim about a group: that these people, these days, these events were counted in this way, and that the count is being asked to stand for something. If you cannot name who was counted, you do not yet have a statistic. You have a costume.

This sample is a first-year gen-ed Statistics 101. It is written to sit beside Analytical Reasoning 101, not to copy it. In that course a claim asks to be believed and a because tries to earn it. Here the number is the claim, and the group is the because. The habit is the same: leave a step a classmate can check.

The author did not teach a statistics department course. The muscle you will feel is from Analytical Reasoning and from logic tutoring: split the sentence, name what has to be true, refuse an invisible step. The objects here are data objects. Who was counted is the first check.

Start with a sentence you have heard in some form. EXAMPLE (invented for teaching, not a campus poll, not a school record): “72% of students feel prepared for college math.” The percent looks like a finding. Ask the group questions. Who was asked? How many answered? What counted as “prepared”? When? If the 72% is 18 of 25 people who stopped in a hallway during add/drop, the claim is “18 of 25 hallway-stoppers, that week, said they felt prepared.” That is a different object from “students.” The word students was doing unpaid work.

Three questions keep the claim honest.

First: Who is in the count? A rate of “households without broadband” changes if household means occupied housing units, or families, or people who answered a phone. A rate of “accidents” changes if a fender-bender counts. Operational definition is the unglamorous name for this. You do not need a methods chapter to ask it. You need a sentence: we counted X as a case of Y.

Second: Who is missing? People who do not answer, people who do not have the app, people who work the shift when the clipboard went around. Missing is not a moral accusation. It is a shape. If night-shift workers never see the form, the number is a claim about day-shift life wearing a campus-wide coat. A missing group can be larger than the counted group. That does not make the counted group fake. It makes the leap to “everyone” fake.

Third: What is the number being asked to buy? A count can support “this group, as counted, looks like this.” It cannot, by itself, support “everyone,” “always,” or “the real rate in the world.” Analytical Reasoning 101 asked you not to let a percent do the arguing. This unit stays with the data version of that habit: put the group back into the sentence until the percent has a job.

You will see this in news and in group chats. “Unemployment is 4%.” Of whom? Who is counted as in the labor force? “The average American sleeps six hours.” Average of what sample, self-reported or measured, including who? You are not required to become a cynic. You are required to refuse an orphan number — a number with no parents.

A useful rewrite sounds like this: “In this EXAMPLE hallway ask (n = 25, one afternoon), 18 people said they felt prepared. That is 72% of respondents, not of the college.” Notice the n, the time window, and the limit. A classmate can disagree about what it means. They cannot say you hid the group.

Measurement is a choice. Counting “study hours” as time with a book open is not the same as counting time an app was logged in. Both can be honest. They are different claims. When two numbers disagree, the first job is not to pick a team. The first job is to ask whether they counted the same thing.

A denominator does work that a headline will not do. “200 people signed.” Two hundred of a 12-person club is a landslide. Two hundred of a city is a Tuesday. The 200 did not change. The claim did. Write the denominator in the same sentence as the count, even in the margin. If the source will not give you one, that is already a finding: the number is underemployed.

Practice: mark who was actually counted, catch a percent that changes once the denominator is named, and write a one-sentence rewrite that puts the group back in. Homework: do it to a real public number you did not invent — a census table, a weather summary, a posted budget line. Do not invent a poll to look complete.

Orphan number
“72% of students feel prepared.”
Claim with a group

Practice

Who was counted?

Read this EXAMPLE (invented for teaching, not a school poll): “72% of students feel prepared for college math.” The fine print, if anyone asked, would say 18 of 25 people who stopped at a table on a Tuesday afternoon. Who was actually counted?

Practice

Same 200, different claim

EXAMPLE (invented): “200 people signed the petition.” Which rewrite is the honest one if the petition sat on a 12-person club table?

Practice

Put the group back in

EXAMPLE headline (invented, not a news story): “Most commuters support a later bus.” The only count in the piece is 9 of 12 people who answered a clipboard at one stop, one morning.

Write one sentence that keeps the 9 of 12 and refuses the word commuters as if it meant a city.

Homework

You could turn this in on paper or paste it into a journal. This file does not collect it.

Find one real public number this week — a census or labor figure, a weather summary, a city or county agenda packet, a posted budget line. Do not invent a poll. Copy the sentence that uses the number. Then, in 150–220 words: (1) Who was counted? If the source does not say, write that. (2) Who is missing, or might be? (3) What claim can the number actually buy, and what claim is too large? If you cannot find a denominator, that is the finding — say so.
