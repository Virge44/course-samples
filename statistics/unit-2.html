Unit 2 · Mean is not the story · ~40 minutes

Center and spread

A mean is a balance point. A median is a middle. Neither one is the data. Spread is the rest of the story.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Compute a mean and a median from a tiny list and say which center you used.
  2. Explain, in one sentence, what a mean can hide when a tail is long.
  3. Write a spread sentence a classmate could check (range, “most between,” or a named outlier).

Lesson

The mean is not a moral average. It is a balance point: every value pulls with equal vote. That is useful. It is also how one extreme value gets to speak for a room that did not invite it.

This unit is still a first-year gen-ed sample, not a statistics-department course. You will compute a mean and a median on a tiny EXAMPLE set. You will not be asked to worship a formula. You will be asked to tell the story the mean is hiding.

EXAMPLE (invented teaching set, not campus data, n = 8 one-way commute minutes): 8, 12, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 55.

Add them: 150. Divide by 8. The mean is 18.75 minutes. If you report “the typical commute is about 19 minutes,” you have told a true arithmetic sentence and a misleading human one. Six of the eight times are 16 minutes or less. The 55 is a late bus, a missed turn, a rain day. It belongs in the data. It does not belong on the throne.

The median is the middle once the numbers are lined up. For eight values, it sits between the 4th and 5th: 14 and 15, so 14.5. The median does not care how late the 55 ran. That is its job and its limit. A median can hide a crisis at the tail just as a mean can be bossed by it. Center is a choice. Name which center you are using, and why it fits the question.

Spread is the rest of the story. Range is the simplest: largest minus smallest, 55 − 8 = 47 minutes. That number is honest and almost unusable by itself — it is a single bad day plus a short trip. A plainer spread sentence is often better: “Most of these eight trips were between 12 and 18 minutes; one was 55.” You have not computed a standard deviation. You have refused to let 18.75 walk around alone.

Standard deviation, if you meet it later, is a way of saying how far the points typically sit from the mean. You do not need the formula to ask the question it is trying to answer: are we clustered, or are we all over the road? In a mixed-prep 101, the sentence beats the symbol. If a live instructor later wants the formula, they can add it. This sample will not pretend a symbol is the same as a claim.

Outliers are not villains. They are points that sit far from the others. Sometimes they are errors (someone wrote 55 hours). Sometimes they are the finding (one route is a disaster). The honest move is to show the point, then say what the center does with it. Dropping the 55 without saying so is a different dataset wearing the old name.

Two centers can both be right and still fight. Mean income is pulled by a few very large values; median income is a better “person in the middle” for that question. Mean household size can be a fine planning number; median commute may be a better “what should I expect on a typical day.” Match the center to the decision. “Typical” is not a synonym for mean.

A mode is the value that shows up most. In the EXAMPLE set, 12 appears twice. Mode is useful for sizes, shoe counts, or categories. It is a weak “typical commute” here because the stack is small. If someone reports only a mode from eight numbers, ask what question they thought they were answering.

Analytical Reasoning 101 taught you that a percent with no base is a costume. This unit is the sibling: a mean with no spread is a costume. The checkable step is small. Name the n. Name the center. Name one sentence of spread. If you cannot, you are not ready to argue with the number.

Practice: pick the median, say what the mean is hiding, and write a one-sentence story that includes spread. Homework: collect 8–12 real measurements of one ordinary thing in a household or on a walk — minutes, counts, temperatures from a public page — or use this EXAMPLE set and say so. Do not invent a campus survey.

Practice

Write a spread sentence

Write one sentence about the EXAMPLE set that a roommate could check. It must include either the 55, a range, or a “most between” window. Do not stop at “the average is 18.75.”

Homework

Collect 8–12 measurements of one ordinary thing you can actually count or time: minutes to walk a block, hours of sleep for a week plus a few extra nights, number of electrical outlets in each room of a home, daily high temperatures from a public weather page for 8–12 days. Or use the EXAMPLE commute list in this unit and say that you did. Do not invent a campus survey. Then write 150–220 words: the list, the mean, the median, and one spread sentence. Say which center you would use for a decision, and why. If an outlier is an error, say how you would know.
