Unit 3 · What a graph is hiding · ~40 minutes

Pictures of data

A graph is an argument with a ruler. Same numbers, different axis, different claim. Read the choices, not only the ink.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Tell a chart that starts at zero from a chart that uses a truncated axis to make a small gap look large.
  2. Name a missing piece on a graph: n, time window, comparison group, or what was counted.
  3. Write one sentence about what a given picture cannot show.

Lesson

A graph is an argument with a ruler. It can make a small difference look like a crisis and a real difference look like weather. The picture is not the data. The picture is a set of choices: where the axis starts, which years are in the window, whether the count n is anywhere on the page, whether two groups that were counted differently are drawn as twins.

This unit stays in the same gen-ed sample. You will not be asked to design a dashboard. You will be asked to say what a picture is hiding. The muscle is the one Analytical Reasoning already uses on a sentence: split the claim from the costume. Here the costume is visual.

Start with the axis. EXAMPLE (invented teaching numbers, not school data): Group A 48, Group B 52, out of 100. Drawn from 0, the bars are almost the same height. Drawn from 47 to 53, Group B looks like a tower. Both pictures use the same two numbers. Only one of them is doing theater. A fair chart usually starts a count or a percent at zero unless you have a reason — temperature is a famous exception, because 0°F is not “no temperature.” If you break the rule, say so on the chart, not in a footnote nobody reads.

Next, the window. A line that shows four good years and hides the six around them is a claim about a selected story, not about a series. Cherry-picking is not a software trick. It is a pair of scissors. Ask: why these years? Why this month? Why this comparison group? If the source will not say, the picture is underemployed in the same way Unit 1’s orphan number was.

Then the missing n. A bar labeled “support” with no count is a percent with no parents, drawn. Two respondents can make a 50/50 bar. Two thousand can too. The bars do not tell you which world you are in. Write n on the chart, or refuse to trust the drama.

Two more hiding places show up without any software.

Unequal bins, drawn as equal. A histogram that groups 0–1, 2–5, and “6 or more” as three equally wide bars is arguing that those piles are the same kind of thing. Maybe they are, for a decision. Maybe the last bar is a junk drawer. Ask what landed in the last bin.

Two axes, one picture. A line of “cases” and a line of “searches” on different scales can be made to kiss. That kiss is a design choice. Dual axes are not always a lie. They are always a place to slow down. If you cannot restate each line in numbers a roommate could check, you are looking at choreography.

3-D bars, exploding pies, and maps that color a whole county from one town have the same habit: they spend ink on feeling. Feeling can start a question. It is not, by itself, a because. A pie chart can be honest for parts of one whole. It cannot be honest for comparing six pies with different totals unless the totals are on the page.

What a graph cannot show is as important as what it can. A chart of yearly rainfall does not show who got flooded. A chart of test scores does not show who was absent. A chart of “engagement” does not show what the app counted as a case (Unit 1 again). When you caption a graph, caption the limit. “This picture shows monthly station totals. It does not show storms, and it does not show a neighborhood.”

You are not required to become a person who hates charts. You are required to read them as claims. In a large mixed-prep room, the check has to be small enough to do tired: Where does the axis start? What is the window? Where is n? What did they not draw? That is enough for a 101. Software can wait.

Practice: mark the truncated axis, name a missing piece, and write one sentence about what a picture cannot show. Homework: find a real public chart — weather, census, budget, transit — and say what it is hiding. Do not invent a school infographic.

What is missing?

EXAMPLE caption (invented, not a news chart): “Support soared last quarter.” The image is two bars, no numbers, no n, no definition of support. What would you demand first?

Practice

What can this picture not show?

A public monthly rainfall chart for one airport station. Write one sentence naming something the picture cannot show — people, neighborhoods, a single storm, or a policy.

Homework

Find one real public chart this week: a National Weather Service climate page, a census figure, a city budget graphic, a transit ridership chart. Copy a link or describe where it lives. Do not invent a campus infographic. In 150–220 words: (1) Where does the axis start? (2) What is the time window, and what sits just outside it if you can tell? (3) Where is n, or is it missing? (4) Write one sentence about what the picture cannot show. If the chart is fair, say that — fairness is allowed.
