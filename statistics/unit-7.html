Unit 7 · Plain language, no theater · ~40 minutes

Uncertainty and “significant”

Significant does not mean important, proven, or big. It is a jargon word with parents: a model, a sample, a way of looking. Translate it. Do not hunt a star.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Restate a margin of error as a window samples like this would often land in.
  2. Tell statistical unusualness from practical importance.
  3. Translate “significant” into a plain sentence that names assumptions or flags that they are missing — without treating it as a trick to run.

Lesson

“Significant” is a jargon word that escaped the lab and went to work as a gold star. In ordinary English it means important. In statistics it usually means something narrower: this pattern would be uncommon if there were nothing going on — under these assumptions, with this sample, with this test. Those last clauses are the job. Without them, “significant” is theater.

This unit will not teach you how to hunt a star. No p-hacking recipe, no “try these five tests until one lights up,” no instruction in squeezing a dataset until it confesses. That is the opposite of a public habit. You will translate. You will say what a small n cannot buy. You will distinguish a statistically unusual difference from a difference a person should care about.

Start with uncertainty you can see. A sample is a draw (Unit 4). Draw again, and the mean will move. A margin of error, in plain language, is a window: samples like this would often land here if we repeated the process. It is not a promise that the truth sits in the window, and it is not a force field around a poll. If the window is “44% to 52% support,” you do not have a majority. You have a cloud that includes majority and not-majority. Reporting 48% as a fact with no window is Unit 1’s orphan number wearing a suit.

Practical importance is a different question. A medicine that lowers blood pressure by a hair can be “statistically significant” in a huge sample and still not be a reason to change a life. A huge difference in a tiny sample can fail a significance test and still be a reason to look again. Size of effect and star-or-no-star are not synonyms. Ask both: is this unusual under a boring model, and is this large enough to matter for the decision?

A p-value, if you meet the phrase, is not the probability that the claim is true. It is not the probability that you lucked out. A usable 101 sentence: if there were no real difference, a result this extreme would be uncommon — given the model, the sample, and the choice to look this way. That sentence is already long. It should be. People want a shorter one so they can stop thinking. Do not give it to them.

Assumptions are the quiet parents of the number. Independent observations, a random sample, a model for noise, a single look at a pre-named question. If someone looked at twenty outcomes and published the one that sparkled, the “uncommon under a boring model” story is no longer the story they ran. Mentioning that is not a how-to. It is a warning label: a star you shopped for is not the same object as a star you predicted.

Small n makes windows wide. Eight commute times (Unit 2’s EXAMPLE) cannot pin a city’s typical trip. They can show a tail in that list. Precision is a thing you buy with design and with size, and even then only for the population you actually sampled. A huge biased sample can be precise about the wrong group. Precision plus tilt is a confident costume.

Not significant is not a proof of zero. It often means “this study could not tell,” especially when the sample is small or the effect is modest. “No effect” and “we did not detect an effect” are different claims. Analytical Reasoning 101 would call the swap a weaker restatement of the finding. Keep the weaker one if it is what you have.

The honest paragraph sounds like this: “In this sample, the difference would be uncommon if nothing were going on, under the assumptions the authors named. The size is ___. The window is ___. What it cannot buy is ___.” If the authors did not name assumptions, that is already a finding. You are not required to rerun their work. You are required not to repeat “significant” as if it meant important, proven, or big.

Practice: pick the honest translation, tell practical from statistical, and write a plain-language restatement. Homework: find “significant” in the wild and translate it. Do not invent a lab result.

Theater
“The result is significant, so it matters and it is proven.”
Translation
If nothing were going on, a difference this large would be uncommon — under these assumptions, with this sample.

Practice

Translate “significant”

Write one or two sentences that translate “the result was significant” without using important, proven, or gold star. You may use: uncommon if nothing were going on; under these assumptions; this sample.

Homework

Find one real public use of “significant,” “margin of error,” or a p-value: a news story about a study, a government release, a poll with a window printed. Do not invent a lab, and do not write a how-to for getting a star. Copy the sentence. Then, in 150–220 words: (1) Translate it in plain language. (2) What assumptions can you see, or what is missing? (3) Is there a size-of-effect question the star is being asked to skip? (4) What can the number not buy? If the piece treats not-significant as “no effect,” repair that too.
