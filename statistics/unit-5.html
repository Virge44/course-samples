Unit 5 · Not a lucky feeling · ~40 minutes

Probability as “how often, if…”

A probability names a process and a long run. One night is not the long run. “I’m due” is not a because.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Restate a probability as “if this process ran many times, we would see this about __ times in 100.”
  2. Tell a one-shot outcome from a long-run frequency, including the move “I’m due.”
  3. Flag a multiplication of chances that treats dependent pieces as independent.

Lesson

Probability, in this course, is not a personality and not a mystical due. It is a sentence of the form: if this process ran many times under these conditions, how often would we see this? The “if” is doing work. Change the process, and the how-often changes with it.

This is still a first-year gen-ed sample. You will not be asked for permutation formulas. You will be asked to restate a chance in frequency clothes so a roommate can see the process. Logic tutoring uses the same split under a heavier costume: what has to be true for this number to mean anything. You do not need the costume. You need the “if.”

A fair coin is the clean EXAMPLE (a teaching object, not an experiment you must run): if we flipped this coin many times under these conditions, heads would show up about half the time. One flip is not 50% in a box. One flip is heads or tails. The 50% lives in the long run of a named process. People get into trouble when they treat a single night as the long run. “I’m due” is that trouble, spoken out loud. The coin does not keep a tab.

Weather language is the civic version. “30% chance of rain” is not a mood, and it is not a promise that 30% of your afternoon will be wet. A usable restatement: in conditions like these, rain happens about 30 times in 100. The process is “days (or hours) that look like this, at this place, under this model.” If the model is thin, the 30% is thin. You are not required to rebuild the National Weather Service. You are required not to hear 30% as “probably not, so I skip the umbrella, and if I get wet the number was a lie.”

Independence is the next small idea. Two events are independent, for our purposes, if knowing one does not change how often the other happens. Coin flips of a fair coin, under ordinary conditions, are the teaching picture. Two days of rain are not automatically independent — weather has a memory of fronts. Two questions on a form are not independent if the second is “same as the last.” If someone multiplies chances, ask whether the pieces were allowed to ignore each other.

A probability is always a probability under a model. “Random person” is a model. “Random person who answered the phone” is a different model (Unit 4 just taught you that). “Random night from this EXAMPLE list of eight commutes” is a third. If you pick one of those eight minutes at random, the chance of drawing the 55 is 1 in 8, not “rare because 55 feels rare.” Feeling is not the denominator.

Out of 100 is a kindness to mixed-prep rooms. 0.03 becomes “about 3 times in 100, if this process ran that often.” 1 in 6 becomes “about 17 times in 100.” You will lose a little rounding. You will gain a picture. In a 101, the picture is the point.

What probability cannot do: certify a single case. A rare event still happens. A common event still fails. The honest sentence names the process and the long run, then stops. “This outcome would be uncommon if the process were fair” is a different claim from “this outcome is impossible” and from “someone cheated.” Analytical Reasoning 101 would call that a leaky pipe. Here the leak is from a frequency to a verdict.

You will meet probability as a warning label on tests and on headlines. Slow down. Name the repeatable process. Name the outcome. Restate how often, if. If you cannot name the process, you do not yet have a probability. You have a feeling with a percent sign.

Practice: pick a fair “how often, if” restatement, mark a fake independence, and write an out-of-100 sentence. Homework: find a real probability claim — forecast, game, health communication, public lottery — and translate it. Do not invent a campus risk poll.

One-shot
This flip is heads or tails. The coin does not owe you.

Write “out of 100”

Homework

Find one real probability claim this week: a National Weather Service forecast wording, a public lottery poster, a health-communication percent you can point to, a sports win probability printed by a public desk. Do not invent a campus risk poll. Copy the sentence. Then, in 150–220 words: (1) Restate it as “how often, if…” and name the process as far as you can tell. (2) What would it mean to treat a single case as the long run? (3) What can the number not buy — a verdict, a personality, a promise about tonight? If the source will not name a process, that is the finding.
