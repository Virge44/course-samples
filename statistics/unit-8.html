Unit 8 · Tiny public table or 20 measurements · ~45 minutes

A short data argument

A claim the rows can buy. The numbers in the same breath. A limit. A step a classmate can check. That is the whole 101, in one paragraph.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Write a claim a tiny public table — or twenty household measurements — can actually support.
  2. Name one limit the same data cannot buy (station vs city, one year vs trend, sample vs population).
  3. Leave a step a classmate could check: a cited public product, or a list a classmate could add.

Lesson

A short data argument is not a research paper and not a feeling with a chart. It is a public paragraph: a claim the numbers can actually buy, the numbers in the same breath, a limit, and a step a classmate could check. You have been practicing the pieces. This unit puts them in one object.

This is still a first-year gen-ed sample showing a course can be structured. It is not a methods sequence, not a lab, and not a claim that I taught Statistics at A&T. The standard is the one Analytical Reasoning 101 already uses on a case: claim, because, something visible. Here the because is a tiny table you did not invent, or twenty measurements you actually take.

Two tracks, same grade.

Track A — the public table on this page. The National Weather Service published a Greensboro, NC climate summary for 2021 for the Piedmont Triad International Airport station (KGSO). The product states a climate-normal period of 1991–2020 and, in that summary, a record period of 1903–2022. Precipitation is in inches. 2021’s total is 40.65. The 1991–2020 normal listed there is 43.95 (departure −3.30). The record maximum listed is 64.11 in 2018. The record minimum listed is 27.74 in 1941. Days with at least 0.01 inches: 107 against a normal of 116.0. Those rows are public. They are one station, one year against a normal and against records. They are not a neighborhood, not a flood map, and not a 2026 forecast.

Track B — twenty household measurements. Count or time one ordinary thing twenty times: minutes of sleep, steps from the door to the mailbox, outlets in twenty rooms across a home and a neighbor’s if you have leave to look, daily highs copied from a public weather page for twenty days. Write the list. Then do Units 1–2 on it: who was counted, mean, median, a spread sentence. Twenty is small on purpose. Small is honest when you say so.

What the argument must do, on either track:

1. A claim the data can buy. For the weather table: “At KGSO, 2021 was drier than the 1991–2020 normal listed in this summary, and not near the 1941 record dry year.” That sentence spends only what the rows contain. “Greensboro was in crisis” is too large. “Climate is solved” is too large. “My street flooded” is not in the table. For household data: “In these twenty nights, the median sleep was __ hours; one night was far below the others.” Not “Americans don’t sleep.”

2. A limit. Station is not city. One year is not a trend you personally discovered. A normal is a 30-year average, not a promise. Household twenty is not a population. Missing groups still exist (Unit 4). If you use mean sleep, the mean is not the story (Unit 2). If you draw a bar, the axis is a choice (Unit 3). If you want to say your late phone caused short sleep, Unit 6 is waiting in the hall.

3. A step a classmate could check. Name the product (NWS Greensboro 2021 climate summary, KGSO) or paste the twenty numbers. A classmate should be able to add, to compare 40.65 with 43.95, or to find the median you claimed. If they cannot, you hid the step.

What not to do. Do not invent a campus poll to look complete. Do not pad with a fake n. Do not treat “significant” as a gold star on four rows (Unit 7). Do not write a policy brief the table did not pay for. Named holes are honest. Fabrication is not a method.

A fair paragraph is short. Four to eight sentences can do it. Claim. Two or three numbers with their labels. One limit. One check. If you already write fluent pages, cut until the table is still visible. Fluency is not the grade. The buy is the grade: did the claim spend only what the rows contain?

Practice: pick the claim the NWS rows can buy, reject the claim that is too large, and write a four-sentence argument. Homework: finish Track A or Track B as a turn-in. Point at the table on this page, or at twenty measurements you took. Do not invent a school dataset.

Homework

Write a short data argument, 200–280 words, on Track A or Track B. Track A: use the NWS KGSO 2021 climate-summary table on this page. Track B: collect 20 household (or public-weather-page) measurements of one thing, list them, and argue from that list. Do not invent a campus poll or a lab. Your paragraph must include (1) a claim the data can buy, (2) the numbers with their labels, (3) one limit (station vs city, one year vs trend, n = 20 vs a population), (4) a step a classmate could check — the NWS product name or the list itself. If you want a causal verb, you are in the wrong unit; go back to Unit 6 and take it off the payroll.
