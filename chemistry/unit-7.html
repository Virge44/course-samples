Unit 7 · Felt experience + a number · ~35 minutes

Energy of reactions

When atoms rearrange, energy is part of the account. Exothermic: the surroundings feel warmer. Endothermic: they feel cooler. The signed number (ΔH) is how the books record that, in kilojoules — not as a mood.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework Commercial products as claims

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Match a felt change in the surroundings to endothermic or exothermic language.
  2. Read ΔH as a signed number: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic, with kJ as the unit.
  3. Use a commercial cold pack or hand warmer as a claim you read, not as a device you assemble or refill from bulk chemicals.

Lesson

Unit 5 treated a reaction as an account of atoms. Atoms are not the whole account. Energy moves when bonds rearrange. A conservative first picture: it costs energy to pull bonded atoms apart; energy is released when new bonds form. The net — which is larger — decides whether the surroundings get warmer or cooler. You do not need a full bond-energy table this week. You need the net, a feeling, and a sign.

Exothermic means the system releases energy to the surroundings. The surroundings feel warmer. A gas-range flame (the methane account you already audited) heats a pan because the combustion is strongly exothermic. A commercial disposable hand warmer, already purchased and already designed, is sold on the claim that a process inside it releases heat to your pocket. You may read that claim on the box. You may not pack iron powder and salt into a bag as a project for this class. The product is an example of a felt exothermic change. It is not a bill of materials.

Endothermic means the system takes in energy from the surroundings. The surroundings feel cooler. A commercial instant cold pack, the kind sold for a twisted ankle, is sold on the claim that a process inside it absorbs heat, so the pouch feels cold on skin. Again: read the package. Do not cut it open to harvest chemicals. Do not look up a bulk recipe and recreate it in a freezer bag. The felt experience is the point — cold on the ankle — and the chemistry sentence is “the process as sold is endothermic.” The homework will not ask you to manufacture one.

Chemists write a compact number for the net energy account: ΔH (delta H), often called the enthalpy change of the reaction under stated conditions. Sign convention you will use here: ΔH negative (ΔH < 0) means exothermic; energy left the system. ΔH positive (ΔH > 0) means endothermic; energy entered the system. Units in first-year tables are often kilojoules (kJ) or kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol). The number without the sign is incomplete. “890” is not a claim. “−890 kJ/mol” is a claim.

A conservative textbook value for the combustion of methane, matching the Unit 5 equation CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, is about ΔH = −890 kJ per mole of methane under standard conditions. Negative: exothermic. Large: a mole of methane’s worth of that reaction dumps a lot of heat into the surroundings — which is why a stove is useful, and why this course still will not ask you to build a burner. The felt experience (a pan getting hot) and the number (negative hundreds of kJ/mol) are the same story in two languages. That pairing is the unit.

Activation energy is a hill on the way, even when the net is downhill. A reaction can be exothermic overall and still need a spark, a flame already lit, or an enzyme in a cell to get over the hill. That is why gasoline in a closed can does not, by itself, do the stove’s job on a homework desk, and why you will not be invited to find out. The hill is a concept. The spark is not an assignment.

Physical changes have energy accounts too: melting ice absorbs heat (endothermic); freezing releases it. Those are useful comparisons and still Unit 1’s identity-survives story. Do not confuse “the bag feels cold” with “new substances must have formed.” Some commercial cold packs are built around dissolving a salt — a process that can be endothermic without being the same sentence as rusting. If the package does not say, you do not have to diagnose the mechanism. You have to match feel, sign, and the claim as printed.

Felt detail plus a number a classmate could check on a table, without a procedure. No explosives, no fuel mixing, no homemade warmers, no dry-ice how-tos, no “scale this ΔH to a weapon or a stove you built.”

You leave able to hear “the pack got cold” as endothermic, “the warmer got hot” as exothermic, and ΔH’s sign as the same information in a table. Feeling without a number is a diary. A number without a felt meaning is a floating integer. Gen-ed chemistry wants both, on paper.

Exothermic
Surroundings warmer. ΔH < 0. Example claim: a commercial hand warmer; a range already in a kitchen. Not a DIY kit.
Endothermic
Surroundings cooler. ΔH > 0. Example claim: a commercial instant cold pack on an ankle. Not a bulk recipe.
CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O ΔH ≈ −890 kJ/mol methane (negative = exothermic)

What this 101 will not ask

Homework

Read a package or a reputable product page. Do not assemble, refill, or cut open a warmer or a pack. Do not mix fuels.

Find one commercial product that claims to get hot or cold from a process inside it (hand warmer or instant cold pack are the intended examples). Transcribe the package claim in one sentence. Then 150–200 words: name the felt change, name endothermic or exothermic, and write the ΔH sign you would expect (− or +) if a table listed that process. If the box is silent on the mechanism, say what you cannot claim. Do not look up a DIY formula. Do not scale a kJ number into a stove, a bomb, or a homemade pack. If you cannot find a product, use this teaching sentence and still write the analysis: ‘Package: instant cold pack; claim: becomes cold when activated as directed.’
