Unit 2 · The table as a tool · ~40 minutes

Atoms and the periodic table

An atom is the smallest piece of an element that is still that element. The periodic table is a lookup tool ordered by atomic number — not a personality quiz for elements.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. State that atomic number (proton count) is what makes an element that element.
  2. Use a group (column) as a “similar behavior” hint and a period (row) as a size/energy trend hint — without pretending you memorized the whole table.
  3. Look up a conservative fact for C, O, Na, Cl, or Fe on a printed or on-screen table: symbol, atomic number, atomic mass.

Lesson

Matter is made of atoms. That sentence is famous enough to feel empty, so make it operational. An atom is the smallest piece of an element that is still that element. If you split a gold atom, you do not have smaller gold. You have pieces that are no longer gold. You will not be splitting atoms in this course. You will be using a table that already did the counting.

Inside an atom, the dense center (the nucleus) holds protons and neutrons. Electrons occupy the much larger outer region. The atomic number, written Z, is the number of protons. Atomic number is identity. Carbon is the element with Z = 6. Oxygen is Z = 8. Sodium is Z = 11. If a nucleus has six protons, the atom is carbon, whether it has six neutrons or seven. Different neutron counts are isotopes of the same element. That is a conservative fact worth keeping: identity lives in the proton count, not in the nickname on a bottle.

The periodic table is a tool arranged by increasing atomic number, in rows called periods and columns called groups. It is not a poster of elemental personalities (“carbon is creative”). It is a lookup sheet. Metals sit toward the left and center; nonmetals sit toward the upper right; a stair-step of metalloids sits between. Noble gases (Group 18: helium, neon, argon, and the rest of that column) are famously unreactive under ordinary conditions. Group 1 metals (lithium, sodium, potassium…) share a family resemblance in how they tend to form +1 ions. You do not need to handle sodium metal. You need to know that the table puts sodium under lithium for a reason, and that table salt is the compound sodium chloride, not a lump of metal.

Each square typically prints three things you will actually use: the symbol (C, O, Na, Cl, Fe), the atomic number (an integer), and an atomic mass (a decimal, a weighted average of isotopes, in atomic mass units). Later, Unit 4 will treat that atomic-mass number, in grams, as the mass of one mole of atoms. For now, notice that the table is already a conversion device waiting to happen. Carbon’s atomic mass is about 12.01. Oxygen’s is about 16.00. You look those up. You do not negotiate them.

Why “periodic”? Because properties repeat in a patterned way as Z increases. That is why a column is more useful than a vibe. If you are asked how chlorine might behave, looking at fluorine and bromine in the same group is a better first move than inventing a story. If you are asked whether something is likely a metal, looking at its place on the table is a better first move than asking whether it “feels industrial.” The table is a map of measured regularities, not a mood board.

A few symbols cause trouble because they do not match English spellings. Na comes from Latin natrium (sodium). K from kalium (potassium). Fe from ferrum (iron). Au from aurum (gold). Pb from plumbum (lead). The table is allowed to keep historical names. Your job is to look them up rather than guess from the first letter. “S” is sulfur, not sodium. “P” is phosphorus, not potassium. Those mix-ups show up on first-year exams because people treat the table as optional decoration.

Electrons matter for sticking (Unit 3), but you do not need orbital cartoons yet. A conservative picture: the electrons that do most of the bonding work are the outer ones. Elements in the same group often have the same number of outer electrons, which is a large part of why they rhyme. Noble gases already have a stable outer arrangement, which is a large part of why they do not rhyme with “eager to react” under ordinary conditions. Stop there. Do not build a homemade reactor, a spark apparatus, or a “try this with household bleach” extension. This unit’s verb is look up.

The table you use can be any standard printed or textbook periodic table. You do not need a secret version. Identity is Z; families are columns; numbers on the square are there to be read. If a claim about an element cannot be pointed at a square, it is not yet using the tool.

You leave this unit able to open a table and retrieve a fact a classmate could retrieve too. That is a public habit. Memorizing a jingle is optional. Reading the square is not.

Identity
Atomic number Z = proton count. Carbon is 6. Oxygen is 8. Sodium is 11. Chlorine is 17. Iron is 26.
Tool move
Same group (column) → similar outer-electron pattern → similar ordinary chemistry. Same period (row) → same general energy shell, left-to-right change.
Look up, don’t mythologize: Na is sodium, not “N-A.” Fe is iron. S is sulfur. The square wins arguments.

Homework

Do not buy or handle reactive metals.

Open any standard periodic table (textbook photo, classroom wall, or a reputable printed sheet). Pick four of these: C, O, Na, Cl, Fe, N, H, He. For each, copy symbol, atomic number, and atomic mass exactly as printed. Then write 150–200 words: what the table lets you say about one pair that share a group, and one pair that do not. Do not invent lab behavior you did not look up. Do not write a personality paragraph (“oxygen is social”). If you cannot see a table, say so and use only the conservative numbers printed in this unit (C 6 / 12.01; O 8 / 16.00; Na 11; Cl 17; Fe 26).
