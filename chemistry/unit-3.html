Unit 3 · Why things stick · ~40 minutes

Bonding, in plain language

Atoms stick because opposite charges attract and because a lower-energy arrangement beats a higher-energy one. Ionic and covalent are two common ways that happens — not two kinds of glue you can buy.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework No synthesis

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Explain ionic sticking as electron transfer plus a lattice of ions, using sodium chloride as a named compound, not as a recipe.
  2. Explain covalent sticking as electron sharing in molecules such as water and carbon dioxide.
  3. Use polarity in one careful sentence: why water is a good solvent for many ionic compounds, without a mixing how-to.

Lesson

Things stick. Salt stays a crystal until something dissolves it. A water molecule stays a water molecule in a glass. That is not because the universe applied craft glue. It is because charged pieces of atoms attract and because some arrangements of electrons have lower energy than others. Bonding language is a way to talk about those arrangements without pretending you can see them with a cafeteria lamp.

Ionic bonding is the name for a common pattern: a metal atom and a nonmetal atom end up as ions — charged atoms — and those ions pack into a lattice. Sodium chloride, the compound in table salt, is the usual classroom example. A sodium atom tends to lose one electron and become Na+. A chlorine atom tends to gain one electron and become Cl−. Opposite charges attract. In the solid, you do not have a single “NaCl couple” holding hands. You have a three-dimensional stack of ions. That is why we speak of a formula unit, not a little salt molecule. You will not make sodium chloride from sodium metal and chlorine gas in this course. Those starting materials are hazardous and this sample does not give procedures. You will read the name of a compound that already exists in a shaker and say why it holds together.

Many ionic solids share a family of ordinary properties: they are crystalline, they often melt at high temperatures compared with everyday molecular stuff, and they do not conduct electricity as dry solids, but many do conduct when melted or dissolved in water because the ions can then move. Those are conservative textbook patterns, not dares. “Dissolved in water” here means a thought about what a label already describes (saline, seawater) — not a kitchen protocol.

Covalent bonding is electron sharing. Nonmetal atoms can share pairs of electrons so that each atom has a more stable outer arrangement. Water is H2O: two hydrogen atoms each sharing with oxygen. Carbon dioxide is CO2: carbon sharing with two oxygens. Oxygen gas is O2: two oxygen atoms sharing. The result is a molecule, a discrete group of atoms that travel together. Molecular substances often melt and boil at lower temperatures than ionic lattices, which is why water is a liquid in a room where salt is a solid. That comparison is a property claim, not a ranking of moral worth.

Sharing is not always even. Oxygen pulls shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does. In a water molecule the oxygen end is slightly negative and the hydrogen ends are slightly positive. That is polarity. Water’s bent shape keeps those partial charges from canceling. Polar water molecules can surround many ions and help an ionic solid dissolve. That is why salt water is a mixture you already know, and why a nutrition label can list sodium as dissolved ions in a soup. Oil is mostly nonpolar covalent molecules. Polar and nonpolar liquids often separate rather than mix. You have seen a salad dressing bottle do this. You do not need to invent a new mixture to understand the sentence.

Metallic bonding is a third pattern for metals: a lattice of metal ions in a sea of relatively mobile electrons, which is part of why metals conduct and can be shaped. One paragraph is enough. This 101 will not ask you to smelt anything.

A fair first-year error is to treat “ionic” and “covalent” as personality types, or to say “sodium wants to be like neon” as if atoms had wishes. Atoms do not want. Arrangements that lower energy are more common under given conditions. Another error is to change a subscript when you are thinking about a bond: water is H2O because that is the molecule, not because someone added a coefficient. Subscripts are the compound’s identity. Coefficients wait until Unit 5.

Do not look up “how to make” a salt or a gas and paste it into homework. Explain sticking the way you would explain a lock: what the pieces are, what attracts, what the ordinary properties hint — on paper.

You leave able to say, without theater, why a salt crystal is a lattice of ions and why a water molecule is a shared-electron group with a polar shape. That is enough bonding for a gen-ed spine. Organic mechanisms, crystal-field theory, and synthesis routes are someone else’s course, taught by a chemist, with safety training this file cannot and will not provide.

Ionic (plain)
Electrons transferred. Ions packed in a lattice. Example already on a table: NaCl. Not a molecule of two atoms holding hands.
Covalent (plain)
Electrons shared. Discrete molecules. Examples already in air and taps: O2, CO2, H2O.
Polar water: oxygen a little δ−, hydrogens a little δ+. Bent shape. Good at surrounding many ions. Not a recipe.
