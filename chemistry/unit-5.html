Unit 5 · What is conserved · ~40 minutes

Reactions as accounts

A chemical equation is a set of books: what you start with, what you end with, and a promise that atoms were not created or destroyed. Balancing is bookkeeping. It is not a recipe and not a dare.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework No how-to-make

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Read a chemical equation as an account of substances, not as instructions for producing them.
  2. State what is conserved in ordinary chemistry: atoms and, in a closed account, mass — not “energy slogans,” not volume of gas by default.
  3. Balance a simple equation by changing coefficients, never subscripts, and count atoms on each side to show the books match.

Lesson

A reaction is a story about identity: substances you start with (reactants) become different substances (products). An equation is how chemists write the account so another person can audit it. Left of the arrow: reactants. Right: products. Plus signs separate species. A triangle, a temperature, or a catalyst name, if present, is a condition note — still not a procedure for you to run in a kitchen.

The law you are auditing is conservative and old: in ordinary chemical change, atoms are not created or destroyed. They are rearranged. If two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom go into a water molecule, those nuclei are still on the page after the arrow. Mass follows the atoms in a closed account. That is why a sealed bottle’s total mass does not jump when a change happens inside it, and why an open pan of boiling water seems to “lose” mass — the water left as steam (Unit 1). The missing mass is a missing line in the books, not a hole in physics.

Take a sentence you may have seen on a stove: methane in a gas range burns in oxygen and the account is carbon dioxide and water. Chemists write

CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O

Read it as books, not as a lab you should build. One methane molecule (or one mole of them) and two oxygen molecules account for one carbon dioxide and two water. Count atoms. Left: 1 C, 4 H, 4 O. Right: 1 C, 4 H, 4 O. The books match. The “2” in front of O2 and H2O are coefficients — how many of those units. The little 2 and 4 inside the formulas are subscripts — the identity of the unit. If you changed CH4 to CH3 to “make it fit,” you would have written a different compound. That is cooking the books. Balance by coefficients only.

Water’s formation account is the same idea:

2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O

Again: an account of atoms. This course will not ask you to produce hydrogen, store a gas, or light anything. Hydrogen gas is not a homework material. You may count the letters on the page. Left: 4 H, 2 O. Right: 4 H, 2 O. Match.

Rust as an account, simplified for a 101: iron and oxygen can be written toward iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, the compound in many brown rust samples. A balanced form is 4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3. Count: 4 Fe and 6 O on each side. The bike fender from Unit 1 now has books. You still do not scrape a fender into a bottle to “test” it. You read the account and check the tally.

States in parentheses — (s), (l), (g), (aq) — tell you the form in the account: solid, liquid, gas, or dissolved in water. They are labels. “(aq)” does not mean “go dissolve this.” It means the writer is talking about a species already in water in that story. Photosynthesis as a reading, not a greenhouse build: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O → C6H12O6 + 6 O2 (under light, in living plants). You can count atoms there too. You cannot treat it as a recipe for sugar in a dorm.

What is not automatically conserved: the number of molecules (coefficients can change the headcount), the volume of a gas (temperature and pressure matter), and “the slogan of energy” as a substitute for a number (Unit 7 will give the number a sign). What is conserved: atom identity and atom count of each element, in the ordinary chemistry this 101 will name.

This sample will not give synthesis routes, extraction how-tos, explosive accounts, drug chemistry, or chlorine-gas stories. If an equation would only be useful as a how-to, it does not belong here. You are auditing a public account the way you would audit a budget line. A coefficient is a quantity. A subscript is a name. Mixing them up is how first-year books fail an audit.

You leave able to tally a simple equation, say what was conserved, and refuse the sentence “balance it by changing the formula.” That refusal is the unit’s whole professional habit.

Coefficient
How many of that unit. Allowed to change when you balance. The 2 in 2 H2O.
Subscript
Identity of the unit. Not allowed to change when you balance. The 2 in H2O (water, not hydrogen peroxide).

Homework

Paper or journal. No procedures. No other reactions than the ones named here.

Audit these three accounts. For each, list atom counts of every element on the left and on the right. If the books do not match, fix them by coefficients only, and show the new counts. (1) 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O (2) Fe + O2 → Fe2O3 (3) CO2 + H2O → C6H12O6 + O2 (the photosynthesis reading; you may scale with a 6). Then 100–150 words: what would it mean, in Unit 1 language, if someone ‘balanced’ water by writing H2O2 instead of 2 H2O? Do not look up how to make any product. Do not add household chemicals to the page.
