Reading a label

A label is a public chemical claim with a list attached. Your job is a short evidence writeup: what the list names, what it does not prove, and one check a classmate could run on the same printed lines. Not a recipe. Not a how-to-make.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework Close of the 101

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Separate an ingredient list and a printed amount from marketing language (“pure,” “natural,” “chemical-free”).
  2. Name what a label does not tell you (how it was made, what mixing would do, whether “natural” is a test).
  3. Write a short evidence paragraph a classmate could audit against the same label — the 101’s closing habit.

Lesson

You have spent seven units learning to hear a chemical claim, look up an element, say why something sticks, count with a mole, audit an equation, place a pH number, and pair a felt energy change with a sign. This last unit asks you to do that work on an object you already own: a label. Food, a sports drink, a bottle of vinegar, a vitamin panel, a cleaner’s ingredient line. The assignment is reading and writing. It is not reverse-engineering a formula, not a synthesis, not “how this was produced at plant 4.”

A useful label has parts with different jobs. An ingredient list names substances, often in descending order by weight for foods in the U.S. A nutrition facts or supplement facts or drug facts panel, when present, prints amounts, servings, and sometimes percent daily values. A concentration line (vinegar’s “5% acetic acid”) is a quantitative claim. A warning (“do not mix,” “keep out of eyes”) is part of the evidence record, not fine print you skip because you are in a chemistry class. Marketing — “natural,” “pure,” “clean,” “chemical-free,” “doctor-grade” — is a different speech act. It may sit next to the list. It is not the list.

Unit 1’s split still governs. Sample: this bottle. Claim: what identity or amount is printed. Check: a classmate looking at the same panel. “Contains water, sugar, citric acid” is a list you can copy. “Hydrates better because it is natural” is a slogan until someone names a substance, an amount, and a comparison. “Zero chemicals” is almost always false in the ordinary sense: water is a chemical substance; ascorbic acid is a chemical substance; sodium chloride is a chemical substance. The adult rewrite is “which substances, in what amounts, with what printed evidence.”

What a label typically will not give you: a balanced equation for how the product was made; a full impurity profile; permission to mix it with the bottle beside it; a pH if the producer chose not to print one; a ΔH. Unit 5’s books and Unit 7’s signs live in tables and papers, not on a cereal box. You may say “the label does not support that.” That sentence is a high-grade move in this course. Pretending the missing line is there is a low-grade move.

Active vs inactive, when a drug-facts panel uses those words, is a legal-and-label distinction: which listed substance is the one the product is sold as doing a named job, and which are there as other ingredients. It is not a moral ranking and not a synthesis map. On a cleaner, the hazard sentence can matter more than the brand story. You already practiced that refusal in Unit 6. Bring it here unchanged. If the close of this 101 produces even one student who writes a cleaner fan-fiction procedure, the sample has failed. The close should produce a paragraph that could be taped next to the bottle.

A short evidence writeup has a shape you can reuse. Sentence 1: name the product and the panel you used (ingredient list, nutrition facts, drug facts, warning block). Sentence 2: copy or carefully paraphrase two or three listed substances or one printed amount. Sentence 3: state one chemical claim those lines actually support (this is a mixture; acetic acid is present at 5%; citric acid appears on a food list, which is acid language from Unit 6). Sentence 4: state one claim the label does not support. Sentence 5: name the check (a roommate reading the same panel). That is 120–180 words if you do not decorate. Decorating is how “natural” sneaks back in.

Teaching example, invented as a sample panel, not a real brand: “CAMPUS VINEGAR — White vinegar. 5% acetic acid. Ingredients: water, acetic acid. Do not mix with bleach or other household chemicals. Not a cleaning-product substitute without reading a cleaner’s own label.” What you can write: a water solution of acetic acid at a printed 5%; an acid story; a mixing refusal. What you cannot write: a neutralization demo, a salad-dressing emulsion how-to, a claim about ‘purity’ the panel never defined, a plant-by-plant synthesis of acetic acid.

This close is claim, because, a door a classmate can walk through — applied to matter on a package. Do not turn the writeup into a recipe.

You leave the course able to stand in an aisle, read a panel, and write five honest sentences. That is a gen-ed outcome. The periodic table, the mole, and ΔH were tools for those sentences. They were never a license to make the thing the label names.

Homework

The close. This file does not collect it. No synthesis. No mixing. No invented news, no invented brand if you can reach a real label.

Choose one real label you can hold or photograph (food, drink, vinegar, OTC drug-facts, or a household cleaner). Do not use a lab reagent bottle you do not own. Transcribe the product name and the panel (ingredients and any amounts or warnings). Then write 180–220 words in the five-sentence shape from this lesson: panel used; two or three listed substances or one amount; one chemical claim the lines support; one claim they do not support; the check (a classmate with the same photo or bottle). Forbidden: how-to-make, extraction, mixing steps, ‘chemical-free’ as praise, medical advice, and any sentence that would only be useful as a recipe. If you cannot reach a real label, use the CAMPUS VINEGAR teaching panel and say that you did.
