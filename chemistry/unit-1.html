Unit 1 · Substance, change, evidence · ~35 minutes

What a chemical claim is

A chemical claim is a sentence about what a sample is made of, or whether it turned into something else, that a careful person could check. If you cannot name the substance and the check, you have a slogan.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework No lab

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Split a short passage into a sample, a claim about identity, and a check that is not a lab recipe.
  2. Tell a physical change (identity survives) from a chemical change (new substances) in a written account.
  3. Reject a slogan (“chemical-free,” “there was a reaction”) that does not name stuff or evidence.

Lesson

Chemistry starts as a habit of claims, not a closet of glassware. A chemical claim is a sentence about matter — what a sample is made of, or whether it turned into something else — that a careful person could check against evidence. “This bottle holds water” can be a chemical claim if we mean the compound with formula H2O. “Chemistry is cool” is a preference. “This cleaner is chemical-free” is usually marketing. Everything you can pour, eat, or wipe with is made of substances. The adult question is which ones, in what amounts, with what check.

There are no experiments to run in this file. There are sentences to split. Subject facts stay conservative.

Three words do the work of the unit: substance, change, evidence.

A substance is a kind of matter with a definite composition. An element is a substance made of one kind of atom: gold in a ring, the oxygen in a tank labeled O2. A compound is a substance made of more than one element locked in a fixed ratio: water, carbon dioxide, sodium chloride. A mixture is more than one substance sharing a space without that fixed recipe: air, salt water, soil. The shaker on a cafeteria table may say “salt” and still be a mixture if anti-caking agents are listed. The compound sodium chloride is the substance. The jar is a sample. Read the label before you name the stuff. That habit is Unit 8; it starts now.

Change comes in two public kinds. In a physical change, the substances you started with are still the substances you have. Ice melts; steam condenses; sugar dissolves in tea. The water is still water. The sugar is still sugar, now spread out. In a chemical change, you have new substances with new identities. Iron in a bike fender becomes rust, a different compound of iron and oxygen. A brown cut apple is not the same set of molecules as the pale flesh you sliced. You will not be asked to rust anything or slice anything for this course. You will be asked to read an account of a change and say whether the identity of the stuff survived.

Evidence is what keeps a chemical claim from being a vibe. A color change is not automatically chemistry; two paints swirled together look new and remain a mixture. A temperature change is not automatically chemistry; a kettle on a coil gets hot because energy was added from outside. Stronger evidence that new substances formed is several signs traveling together: a color that does not look like blending, a solid appearing in a previously clear mixture, a gas forming when nothing was boiling, a lasting temperature change with no heater or freezer doing the work. None of those signs is magic by itself. A boiling pot makes a gas — steam — and that gas is still water. The question is identity, not drama.

This course will not give lab recipes, mixing instructions, extraction how-tos, or “how to make” steps. It will not discuss explosives, drugs, or weaponization. Household products will be named as things you already see on a shelf — vinegar, a cleaner, a commercial cold pack — so you can read what they claim. You will not combine them. If a label says do not mix, that sentence is the assignment, not a puzzle to solve.

A useful move from the writing classroom: split the sentence. What is the sample? What is being claimed about it? What would count as a check? “The liquid is acidic” is incomplete until you say how you would know — a printed pH value on a food label, a number someone recorded, a published typical range — not a dare to taste it. “There was a reaction” is incomplete until you name what you started with and what you think you have now.

You leave this unit able to hear a chemical claim, name the stuff, and ask for a check. Later units add tools: the periodic table, a counting word called the mole, an equation as a set of books, a pH number, an energy sign. They do not replace the question. If you cannot say what the substance is, whether it changed, and what would show it, you are not yet doing chemistry. You are decorating.

Physical (identity survives)
Ice on a sidewalk becomes liquid water. Still H2O. The account does not need a new name for the stuff.
Chemical (new substances)
A bike fender, left in wet air for months, shows brown rust. Iron metal is no longer the only iron story on that surface.
Slogan
“This wipe is chemical-free.” Matter is made of substances. The sentence hides the list.
Claim with a door
“The bottle is labeled 5% acetic acid by volume.” A classmate can read the same printed line.

Practice

Which sentence is a chemical claim?

Pick the sentence that names stuff or a change of identity in a way a classmate could check without running a lab.

Practice

Did identity survive?

Read the account. Is this written as a physical change or a chemical change?

A pan of water is left on a warm stove until the liquid is gone. The cook says the water “turned into nothing.” A classmate says the water became steam, which is still H2O, now a gas.

Practice

Which checks are actually checks?

A roommate says, “The brown spots on this old nail mean a chemical change.” Mark every item that could support that claim as a sentence in a notebook. Do not treat any item as a procedure to go try.

Homework

Do not mix anything. Do not open a lab.

Find one chemical claim in ordinary life this week — a food label, a cleaner label, a posted water-quality notice, a weather report about rust or ice. Do not invent a story and do not run a test. Copy or photograph the sentence (no other people in the frame). Then write: (1) what sample is named, (2) what identity or change is claimed, (3) what check is already on the page or what check would be needed. 150–200 words. If the sentence is a slogan, say so, and write the chemical claim it is pretending to be.
