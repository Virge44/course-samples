Unit 6 · Food and cleaning, conceptual · ~40 minutes

Acids, bases, and everyday pH

pH is a number about how acidic or basic a water solution is. Food labels and cleaner labels already publish clues. Reading them is the work. Mixing them is not.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework Do not mix products

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Place a pH number on a conservative scale: around 7 near-neutral; lower is more acidic; higher is more basic.
  2. Match everyday labeled products to acid/base language (vinegar as an acetic-acid solution; many cleaners as bases) without a procedure.
  3. Treat “do not mix with other household products” as a binding sentence — not as a puzzle, hint, or experiment prompt.

Lesson

In water, some substances donate hydrogen ions (H+, more carefully hydronium, H3O+). Those are acids in the Arrhenius picture this 101 will use. Some substances provide hydroxide ions (OH−). Those are bases in the same picture. There are broader definitions (Brønsted–Lowry: acids donate protons, bases accept them) that you can keep in a margin. You do not need a third named theory this week. You need a number and a refusal.

The number is pH. It is a compressed way to talk about how acidic or basic a water-based sample is. Pure water at ordinary room temperature sits near pH 7, called neutral in this introductory sense. Numbers below 7 are acidic; the farther below, the more acidic. Numbers above 7 are basic (alkaline); the farther above, the more basic. The scale is logarithmic, which is a conservative warning: a jump of one pH unit is a tenfold jump in that ion story, not a “one point” shrug. You do not need to compute logarithms here. You need to stop treating pH 3 and pH 5 as “pretty much the same.”

Typical published ranges, for reading, not for making: lemon juice often near pH 2; household vinegar (a water solution of acetic acid, often labeled 5%) often near 2–3; milk near 6–7; baking-soda solutions near 8; many glass-cleaner ammonia products near 11. Stomach gastric juice is strongly acidic; that is biology you may have heard, not a tasting assignment. Bleach-based cleaners are typically strongly basic. Those numbers are the sort of thing a chart or a label might already show. They are not invitations to line bottles up on a counter and combine them.

Food uses acid language you already speak. Sour often tracks with acids: acetic acid in vinegar, citric acid in citrus, lactic acid in yogurt. A nutrition or ingredient list will name those words. “Natural sour” is not a substitute for the name. Cleaning products use base language you should read as a warning as much as a description: many drain, oven, and some glass cleaners are strongly basic. The chemistry sentence is “this solution sits high on the pH scale and can damage skin and eyes.” The student sentence is “I read the hazard line.” It is not “I will see what happens if…”

Do not mix household products. That is not a theme. It is a rule of this course and of ordinary adult life. Labels on bleach and on many acid cleaners say not to mix with other chemicals. You will not be told here what specific mixtures produce, including any gas. You will not be given a “why” that doubles as a recipe. If a label says do not mix, the assignment is to leave the bottles closed and uncombined. Curiosity that wants a reaction belongs in a supervised lab with a chemist, ventilation, and a protocol this file will not write.

Neutralization, as a thought: H+ (from an acid) and OH− (from a base) can be written as accounting for water, H2O. That is Unit 5 language applied to ions. It is not a kitchen titration. It is not “dump the vinegar into the cleaner.” Antacids on a drugstore label are a commercial claim about neutralizing excess stomach acid; you may read the drug-facts panel. You may not design a homemade antacid from pantry goods for this class.

Indicators exist: papers or dyes that change color in different pH ranges. A thought experiment is allowed: “If a strip read 3, the sample would be on the acidic side.” Buying strips to test cleaners, pooling mystery liquids, or tasting anything to “check pH” is not the homework. Taste is not a laboratory instrument, and cleaners are not food.

I did not teach chemistry as a content expert. This unit is structured the way a writing teacher structures evidence: the label is the source; the pH number is a claim; mixing is not research. Lecturer courses were Critical Writing, Humanities, and Analytical Reasoning. Scientific Development & Social Change was design/ops. Do not apply this as a lab. Subject facts stay conservative: scale direction, a few typical ranges, acetic acid as the named acid in vinegar, a hard no on combining products.

You leave able to read a number, name acid or base in plain language, and treat a warning line as an instruction to stop. That last skill is the one that keeps a gen-ed 101 from becoming an accident report.

Safety

Household products in this course

Named only as labels and typical published values. No mixing instructions. No “try this with bleach.” No extraction. No tasting assignment. If two bottles are under the sink, they stay there, closed, uncombined.

Lower pH
More acidic. Lemon and vinegar sit low on typical charts. Named acids on food labels: acetic, citric, lactic.
Higher pH
More basic. Many cleaners sit high. Read the hazard. Do not combine with other products.

Practice

Place the number

A juice label’s published typical pH is about 3. A glass-cleaner chart shows about 11. Which reading is more acidic?

Practice

Vinegar on a food label

A bottle reads “white vinegar” and “5% acetic acid.” What is the chemistry claim you can actually defend in this course?

Practice

The warning line

A cleaner label says: “Do not mix with other household products.” What is the student move in this 101?

Homework

Read only. Do not taste cleaners. Do not mix bottles. Do not buy extra product to “test pH.”

Find one food label and one cleaning-product label in a home or store aisle (do not open industrial cabinets). Transcribe: product name, any acid or base-related ingredient words, any pH if printed, and any ‘do not mix’ or hazard sentence. Then 150–200 words: place each product on the acid/base story using only what the label (and this unit’s typical ranges, if the label is silent on pH) will support. One paragraph must be a refusal: what you will not do with these two bottles, and why that refusal is part of the chemistry, not a vibe. Do not combine them. Do not photograph other people’s faces or private addresses.
