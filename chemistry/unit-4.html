Unit 4 · Counting without magic · ~40 minutes

Moles and counting

A mole is a counting word, like dozen. It is large because atoms are small. The periodic table already printed the gram mass of one mole of atoms. That is the trick. There is no further trick.

3 objectives Practice with feedback 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Define a mole as 6.022 × 1023 things — a count, not a mysterious personality of matter.
  2. Read molar mass from a table: the atomic mass in grams is the mass of one mole of those atoms.
  3. Convert a simple mass of water or carbon dioxide to moles using a printed molar mass, showing the arithmetic.

Lesson

Atoms are inconveniently numerous. A drop of water holds on the order of 1021 molecules. You cannot count them one by one on a desk. Chemistry needed a counting word the way bakeries needed dozen. The word is mole (abbreviated mol). One mole is 6.022 × 1023 of whatever you are counting: atoms, molecules, formula units, electrons. That number is Avogadro’s number. It is large because the things are small. It is not magic, and it is not a secret extra ingredient in a reaction.

Why that particular number? By design, it makes the table’s atomic-mass number speak in grams. Carbon’s atomic mass is about 12.01 u (atomic mass units). One mole of carbon atoms has a mass of about 12.01 g. Oxygen: about 16.00 g per mole of atoms. That pairing is the whole convenience. The table was already a scale. The mole is the count that makes the scale read in grams you can write on a homework page.

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol). For a compound, add the atomic masses in the formula. Water is H2O: two hydrogens (2 × 1.008) plus one oxygen (16.00) ≈ 18.02 g/mol. Carbon dioxide is CO2: 12.01 + 2 × 16.00 = 44.01 g/mol. Sodium chloride is NaCl: 22.99 + 35.45 ≈ 58.44 g/mol. You look the pieces up. You add. You do not bargain.

The conversion is a bridge, not a spell. Mass and moles relate by

moles = grams / (grams per mole) and grams = moles × (grams per mole)

So 36.04 g of water is 36.04 / 18.02 ≈ 2.00 mol of water. 11.0 g of CO2 is 11.0 / 44.01 ≈ 0.250 mol of CO2. Write the unit every time: mol of what. “Two moles” of nothing in particular is how people get lost. Two moles of water is a different count of molecules than two moles of carbon dioxide, because each molecule is a different package — but each amount is the same number of packages. That is the point of a counting word.

A dozen eggs is 12 eggs, not 12 cartons, not 12 grams. A mole of water is 6.022 × 1023 water molecules, not 6.022 × 1023 grams, and not “a mole-sized splash.” People hear mole and picture a lump. Picture a number. The lump’s mass depends on what you counted. A mole of helium atoms has a small mass (about 4 g). A mole of iron atoms has a larger mass (about 55.85 g). Same count, different masses, because the atoms differ. The table already told you that.

Coefficients in chemical equations (Unit 5) are mole ratios once you are talking about amounts large enough to weigh. “2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O” can be read as two moles of hydrogen molecules and one mole of oxygen molecules accounting for two moles of water molecules. That is bookkeeping. It is not a lab procedure for producing gases, and this course will not ask you to generate hydrogen or oxygen. You may read the ratio on paper the way you read 2:1 in a recipe heading without cooking.

Two errors to refuse. First: treating molar mass as a percentage you invent. It is a sum from the table. Second: canceling zeros until the number looks friendly and then dropping the substance. 18 g of water is about one mole of water. 18 g of CO2 is not a mole of CO2. The same gram number is a different count when the package changes.

Do not use the mole as a costume for a synthesis calculation about a drug, an explosive, or a “how to make.” If a problem cannot be stated as grams and moles of water, CO2, NaCl, or a named element on a table, it does not belong in this 101.

You leave able to say what a mole counts, where the gram number comes from, and how to show one conversion a roommate could recompute. That is counting without magic: the large number is large for a boring reason, and the arithmetic is the arithmetic of dozen scaled up.

Dozen
12 of the thing. A dozen eggs ≠ a dozen cartons. The word is a count.
Mole
6.022 × 1023 of the thing. 1 mol H2O ≈ 18.02 g. 1 mol CO2 ≈ 44.01 g. Same count, different masses.

What kind of word is “mole”?

Read the table, then convert

Water’s molar mass is 18.02 g/mol. You have 36.04 g of water. How many moles of water is that?

Practice

Same grams, different packages

18 g of water vs 18 g of CO2 (molar mass 44.01 g/mol). Which sentence is true?

Homework

Show the arithmetic. This file does not collect it. No synthesis calculations.

Using molar masses printed here (H2O = 18.02 g/mol, CO2 = 44.01 g/mol, NaCl = 58.44 g/mol), complete these conversions on paper. Show units every line. (1) 9.01 g H2O → mol H2O. (2) 0.500 mol CO2 → g CO2. (3) 117 g NaCl → mol NaCl. Then 120–150 words: explain to a classmate why 18 g of water and 18 g of carbon dioxide are not the same number of moles. Do not introduce any other compound. Do not frame this as a lab.
