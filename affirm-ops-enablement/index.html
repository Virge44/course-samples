Why this matters

I designed this for Monday. A clock got named. That is the whole change.

Agents must name two public clocks out loud before they promise a day. People mash them into one. That mash is the quality miss.

If an agent promises “we’ll have this in 10 days” when the 60-day notice window is already the live question, they have taught the customer the wrong clock. If they invent a tighter internal deadline and speak it as law, they have made a regulated promise they cannot keep.

The public job this is for — Operations Enablement Manager, from Affirm’s public careers page, paraphrased, not pasted — writes SOPs, job aids, learning, and manuals for human and AI agents, and walks change through a regulated shop. This sit-down is that job in one change.

Affirm’s public brand line is honest credit, no hidden fees or compounding interest. A mashed clock is a hidden one. I am not describing how Affirm runs Regulation E. I am teaching the public rule.

The two clocks — lock these

Source: 12 CFR 1005.11 (CFPB). I did not invent a third clock.

  • Clock A — consumer notice window. Notice of error is timely if the institution receives it no later than 60 days after it sends the periodic statement on which the alleged error first appears. That is 1005.11(b)(1). If the notice is late, the institution is not required to follow 1005.11 for that notice. For an unauthorized electronic fund transfer it must still comply with 1005.6 before imposing liability. Do not tell the customer “you’re too late, goodbye.”
  • Clock B — institution investigation window. Determine whether an error occurred within 10 business days of receiving the notice. Report results within 3 business days after completing the investigation. Correct within 1 business day after determining an error occurred. If more time is needed: up to 45 days if the institution provisionally credits (including interest where applicable) within 10 business days, tells the consumer the amount and date within 2 business days, and gives full use of the funds. Use 20 business days instead of 10 if the transfer is within 30 days of the first deposit (new account). Use 90 days instead of 45 for a foreign-initiated transfer, a point-of-sale debit-card transfer, or a new-account transfer.

Keep-open card · human agent, live case

Job aid

Print this. Or leave it open. Short lines on purpose.

Take the notice

  • Oral counts. Start the investigation promptly.
  • Do not wait for a written confirmation to begin.
  • If written confirmation is required, say so and give the address.

Collect

  • Enough to identify the person and the account.
  • Why the customer believes an error exists.
  • Type, date, and amount — if the customer has them.

Name clock A

  • 60-day notice window, from the day the statement was sent.
  • If the statement-sent date is unknown, say you will confirm it. Do not guess.

Name clock B

  • 10 business days to determine — or 45 with timely provisional credit.
  • Do not promise a calendar Tuesday unless a lead has given you one.

Do not

  • Do not decide “this is unauthorized” on the call.
  • Do not invent a system name.
  • Do not charge the customer for asking.

If the notice looks late

  • Still take it. Flag it.
  • A human lead decides 1005.11 vs 1005.6.
  • You do not close the door.

Same rule, for an AI agent

This is a constrained instruction, not a toy chat. Paste it where the model reads its job.

Role: servicing assistant.

You do not determine whether an error occurred.

You may name only these public clocks from 12 CFR 1005.11:
- Clock A: notice is timely if received no later than 60 days after the institution sends the periodic statement on which the alleged error first appears.
- Clock B: determine whether an error occurred within 10 business days of receiving the notice; report within 3 business days after completing; correct within 1 business day after determining an error occurred. Up to 45 days if provisional credit (including interest where applicable) is given within 10 business days, the consumer is told the amount and date within 2 business days, and has full use of the funds.
- Extensions you may name: 20 business days instead of 10 if the transfer is within 30 days of first deposit (new account). 90 days instead of 45 for foreign-initiated, point-of-sale debit-card, or new-account transfers.

You may not invent a tighter clock. You may not invent an Affirm-internal SLA.

Collect the notice fields: enough to identify the person and the account; why the customer believes an error exists; type, date, and amount if the customer has them. Oral notice counts. Start promptly. Do not wait for written confirmation to begin. If written confirmation is required, say so and give the address.

Never dump a yes/no on whether a transfer was unauthorized.

If the statement-sent date is missing, or the 60-day math is unclear: say so and escalate to a human. Do not guess the date.

If the customer asks “so you’ll refund me today”: do not promise final credit. You may describe provisional credit only as a possibility the institution uses when it needs more than 10 business days — not as a promise you are making.

Never tell the customer they are too late as a closer. If the notice looks late for 1005.11, still take it, flag it, and escalate. A human lead decides 1005.11 vs 1005.6.

Never fabricate ticket IDs, merchant names, or account activity. Never invent a system name. Do not charge the customer for asking.

Same rule, two mouths. The card is what a human agent says on a live case. The block is what an AI agent is allowed to say. If those two diverge, the mash is already in the room. I wrote both from the same public clocks. I did not write an Affirm system.
