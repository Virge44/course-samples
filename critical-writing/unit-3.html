Unit 3 · Thesis · ~40 minutes

A claim that can be wrong

A thesis is not a topic in a costume. If a classmate could not disagree, it is not a claim yet — and it should be allowed to change.

3 objectives Rewrite a dead thesis 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish a topic from a claim that a classmate could reject.
  2. Rewrite a dead thesis — “important,” “I think,” a question, a both-sides promise — into a position that can be tested.
  3. Name one objection that would force you to change the thesis, not only to defend it.

Lesson

Can it be wrong?

A thesis in this course is a claim someone else can test. That is the whole definition. It is not a sentence that sounds academic. It is not the prompt rearranged. It is not “I will discuss.” If a reasonably informed classmate could not say “No, because…,” you do not have a thesis. You have a subject with a period at the end.

Dead theses have a short list of costumes. “X is important.” “X has been around for many years.” “There are two sides to X.” “I believe that X should be considered.” “What is the role of X in society?” A question is a prompt, not a claim. “I believe” is a mood. Importance is a shrug with a coat on. Both-sides is a way of refusing to stand somewhere.

A live thesis takes a position a reader could refuse. “The attendance policy treats night-shift work as a character flaw, and the late record is a labor record, not a motivation record.” A classmate can say no: night-shift is rare; the policy already has an appeal; lateness is still a choice. Good. Now you have an argument, not a theme.

Narrow until it can break

First-year theses often fail by being large. “Social media harms teenagers.” Nobody can test that in three pages. Narrow until a counterexample could hurt you. Which teenagers. Which use. Which harm, measured how, in what setting. “Required group Slack channels in this composition section increase the unpaid work of the student who already writes the paper” is small enough to be wrong. Small enough to be wrong is a compliment.

Narrowing is not dumbing down. It is choosing a fight you can finish. A large claim with no evidence is a performance of seriousness. A small claim with a reason and a piece of evidence a skeptic could check is the work.

It should be allowed to change

The other half of the title: a thesis that can change. Students treat the first thesis as a vow. If the draft discovers that the evidence points somewhere else, they hide the discovery and keep the vow. That is not integrity. That is theater. Revision (Unit 7) will ask you to re-see the argument. This unit asks you to write a thesis you would be willing to replace.

A practical check, before you draft: write the thesis. Then write one honest objection — not a cartoon of the other side. If you cannot write an objection that would make you change the sentence, the thesis is either too vague to wound or too proud to learn. Change it now, on purpose, while it is cheap.

You will rewrite a dead thesis in this unit. The machine cannot grade whether your rewrite is true. It can catch the costumes: “I think,” “important,” “today’s world,” a question mark, a both-sides promise. You still have to put a testable position on the table. That part is yours. Sample sentences here are invented. Do not treat them as research.

A last distinction. A claim can be modest and still be live: “This section’s discussion board rewards summary because the prompt never asks for a disagreement.” Modest. Testable. A classmate who has been on that board can say no. That is a better thesis than a paragraph about the importance of communication in the modern world. You already know how that paragraph ends. It ends without having begun.

Where first-year theses actually come from

Most dead theses are not failures of intelligence. They are translations of fear. The prompt asked for an argument. The writer heard: do not be wrong in public. So they wrote a sentence that cannot be wrong. Importance cannot be wrong. A question cannot be wrong. Both-sides cannot be wrong. “I think” builds a door you can leave through if someone pushes. College writing does not want that door. It wants a sentence you would have to revise if the evidence went the other way.

Another source of dead theses is the high-school reward for coverage. If the essay that mentioned three themes always scored higher than the essay that fought for one, you learned to stack topics. Unlearn it in this unit. One fight, small enough to finish, is the adult move. You can still be ambitious. Ambition here is precision, not acreage.

If you are stuck, steal the Unit 1 sentence and cut it down. “I am writing to a director who thinks absences are motivation, so they will count night-shift as structure.” The thesis is often the purpose clause, made into a claim: “The attendance policy treats night-shift work as a motivation problem.” Same thought. Now it can be refused. That is the upgrade.

Practice

Which theses can be wrong?

Check every sentence that is a live claim — a classmate could reject it. Leave dead theses unchecked.

Practice

Rewrite a dead thesis

Dead version: “I think attendance policies are an important issue in today’s college world and there are two sides to consider.”

Rewrite it as one or two sentences a classmate could disagree with. Name a reader or a mechanism if you can. Avoid “I think,” “important,” “today’s world,” questions, and both-sides promises.

Homework

150–250 words.

Write a thesis for the paper you started in Unit 1 (or a new one if that one died). One or two sentences. Then write one honest objection — a reason a real person, not a dummy, would refuse your claim. Then write one sentence: what you would change in the thesis if that objection turned out to be true. Do not defend yourself in the objection. Let it land.
