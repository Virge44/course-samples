Unit 4 · Reasons and evidence · ~40 minutes

Reasons and evidence

A feeling is not a reason. Rank what would actually test the claim.

3 objectives Rank evidence 1 homework

After this sitting

  1. Separate a reason from a feeling and from a restatement of the claim.
  2. Rank pieces of evidence by how well a skeptic could use them to test the claim.
  3. Name a gap in a given evidence set — what is still missing that would matter.

Lesson

Not “I feel”

Feelings happen. They are not banned. They are not reasons. “I feel like the attendance policy is unfair” tells a reader your mood. It does not tell them a mechanism they can inspect. A reason is still in your voice, but it is a because that could be checked: “The policy counts a night-shift clock-out at 7 a.m. as the same event as sleeping through an alarm.” A classmate can ask whether that is true, and how often.

First-year drafts hide feelings in academic clothes. “It is clear that.” “Anyone can see.” “In today’s society we all know.” If you delete the clothes and the sentence is “I strongly feel this,” you have not given a reason yet.

A reason is not a second thesis

A reason answers why the claim would be true. It does not repeat the claim in new adjectives. Claim: the visitor lot is a late-arrival policy. Restatement: the visitor lot is a problematic and unfair system that negatively impacts punctuality. That second sentence has done no work. A reason would be: the lot’s 10 a.m. opening sits two hours after the first lecture, so the empty spaces are reserved for people who are not in the class. Now a skeptic has something to push on — the clock, the empty spaces, who they are for.

Stack reasons, don’t stack synonyms. Two reasons should fail differently. If knocking one down knocks them both down, you had one reason written twice.

Evidence a skeptic could check

Evidence lives outside your certainty. A count. A scene a reader could recognize. A quoted line with a page. A procedure in a handbook. “Studies show” with no study is not evidence. A story from your hall can be evidence if you say what it is — one hall, one week — and do not pretend it is a census.

Rank evidence by how well it tests the claim, not by how impressive it sounds. A precise local count often beats a vague national statistic that does not touch your mechanism. A quoted sentence from the policy beats a general claim about “the university.”

Then name the gap. Every evidence set has one. If you only have stories, you need a count. If you only have a count, you need a mechanism. Naming the gap is how a reader learns you are not bluffing.

Sit with a piece of evidence

Do not drop evidence and run. After the count, the scene, or the handbook line, write one sentence that says what you think it proves — and one sentence that says what it does not. “Eighteen phones during announcements, four after a question was due, in one section on one Monday, suggests the opening of class is the variable. It does not prove that phones are the enemy of learning.” Those two sentences are the difference between using evidence and waving it.

Feelings can still enter as a scene, once. “I dread Monday 8 a.m. because I clock out at 7” is a scene. It is not a reason the policy is wrong. The reason is the mechanism: the clock, the gate, the appeal process built for rare emergencies. Put the feeling next to the mechanism or leave it in a draft note.

Practice

Rank the evidence

Same claim. Rank from strongest test (1) to weakest (4). Strongest = a skeptic could use it to test the mechanism. Weakest = it barely touches the claim.

Homework

250–350 words.

Take the thesis from Unit 3. Write: (1) the claim in one sentence; (2) two reasons that fail differently — if one falls, the other should still be able to stand; (3) one piece of evidence a skeptic could check, and say what kind of evidence it is (count, scene, quoted policy, comparison); (4) one gap — what you still do not have that would matter. Do not write “I feel.” Do not write “studies show” with no study. If the evidence you actually have is one scene, say it is one scene.
