Unit 1 · Audience, purpose, stakes · ~35 minutes

Who you’re writing to

A paper is not a diary with citations. Name a reader, a job for the sentences, and a cost if that reader shrugs.

3 objectives Annotate + rank 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Name a specific reader — not “anyone,” not “the teacher” as a vague slot — and one thing that reader already believes.
  2. Separate purpose (the change you want in the reader’s position) from topic (what the paper is “about”).
  3. State, in one sentence, what is at stake if that reader remains unconvinced.

Lesson

A public act

College writing is not a diary with citations. It is a public act. You put a claim on the table that a specific other person can test, refuse, or use. If nobody could disagree, you did not write an argument. You filled a page.

The first skill is not voice. It is knowing who is in the room. “The professor” is a job title, not a reader. The person grading you is standing in for a reader who has other things to do and a prior view of your subject. Name that reader. Name what they already believe. If you cannot, you will write as if the assignment itself were the audience, and the paper will sound like a receipt: I was here; I completed the prompt.

Audience is a person with a belief

Audience is not a demographic checkbox. It is a person with a role, a belief, and a limit on their patience. Write “parking on campus” and you have a topic. Write “a director of campus services who thinks late students are disorganized” and you have a reader. That reader will not be moved by a list of your mornings. They might be moved by a cost they have not counted: how many 8 a.m. sections start ten minutes late because the visitor lot stays gated until 10. Same campus complaint. Different job for the sentences.

A first-year paper written “for anyone” lists grievances. The same paper written for a registrar who has to defend a policy will have to show a cost the office has not priced. You do not need a famous reader. You need a named one.

Purpose is a change you can watch

Purpose is not the prompt restated in a to-infinitive. “To discuss phones in class” is still a topic. Purpose is the change you want in the reader’s position. They currently treat the phone ban as a manners problem. You want them to treat it as a design problem: the class has no job for the first five minutes, so the phone fills the gap. Success is not “they understand.” Success is a verb you can watch: they change the opening of class, or they keep the ban and say why the design point does not hold.

If you cannot say what would count as success with this reader, you do not have a purpose. You have a subject wearing a verb.

Stakes are local, or they are fake

What’s at stake is the cost if they stay unconvinced. Global stakes — society will fail, the next generation will suffer — are a first-year reflex and they usually fake. Local stakes are enough. If this reader is not moved, the section keeps starting late. The office keeps a rule that punishes night-shift commuters. The discussion board keeps rewarding summary because nobody named a claim. Small, named costs beat large, empty ones.

A working sentence:

I am writing to [reader, with a belief] so that they will [a change you can observe] because if they don’t, [a cost that lands on someone].

If any blank is “people,” “the issue,” or “it matters,” the sentence is not done. Fill it until a classmate could argue with it.

You will see the same three questions on every paper in this course, including the ones that look like “just a response.” Who is this for. What do you want from them. What happens if they shrug. A lab report is for a reader who might try to reproduce the work. A history paragraph is for a reader who has other sources. A discussion post is for classmates who will quote you or scroll past. None of those readers owe you their attention.

The samples below are invented. They are not student records and not a live campus case. The habit is the same when you write for an actual class this term: name the reader, name the job, name the cost of failing. You are not here to perform being a good student. You are here to make a claim that can survive contact with a reader who did not have to show up.

Practice

Annotate a claim

The paragraph is invented. Click a highlighted stretch, then pick the label it is doing: audience, purpose, stakes, or just a topic. Do all four, then check.

College attendance policies are a common first-year complaint. I am writing to a director of student success who thinks absences are a motivation problem so that they will count night-shift work as a structural cause of Monday 8 a.m. no-shows, not a character flaw. If they don’t, the office will keep a rule that punishes the students the retention report says it wants to keep.

No stretch selected.

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words.

Pick a real assignment you have this term — or, if you do not have one, a campus rule you would actually argue about (attendance, parking, group grades, dining hours). Complete this sentence, then write one paragraph as if that reader is in the room: I am writing to [specific reader + one belief they hold] so that they will [a change you can watch] because if they don’t, [a cost that lands on someone]. Do not write to “people” or “society.” Do not use “it matters.” Name the reader. Name the verb. Name the cost.
