Unit 8 · Sources and close · ~45 minutes

Using sources without disappearing

A source is a voice in your argument, not a substitute for one. Keep the claim visible. Then close a short portfolio you would stand behind.

3 objectives Spot the disappearing act Portfolio close

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Use a source as a voice that your claim has to answer — not as decoration and not as a replacement writer.
  2. Quote only when the exact words matter; otherwise paraphrase, and keep a citation habit your instructor can name.
  3. Write a short closing argument in which your claim stays visible before, during, and after the source enters.

Lesson

Do not vanish

First-year papers often disappear at the first citation. The student had a claim. Then a quote arrives, long, indented, unexplained, and the next sentence is another quote. By the third page the paper is a tour of other people’s sentences. The tour can be accurate and still fail, because nobody is home. Using a source without disappearing means your claim is still on the table when the other voice enters, and still on the table when it leaves. The source is a guest. You are the host. Hosts do not leave the room.

Decoration is the other failure. A quote is dropped in because the assignment required “two sources,” and the surrounding sentences do not need it. If you can delete the source and the paragraph still makes the same move, the source was not working. It was compliance.

Quote when the words are the evidence

Quote when the exact wording is what you are testing: a policy line, a definition the writer is stuck with, a phrase that performs confidence. Paraphrase when you need the move, not the music. A first-year habit is to quote because paraphrase feels like stealing. The reverse is closer to the truth: a long quote you have not framed is often a way of avoiding the work of putting the move in your own sentences. Either way, name the source so a reader can find it. This sample course does not police MLA versus APA. Your live class will. What this course polices is the disappearing act.

A usable pattern, ugly on purpose: My claim is X. So-and-so argues Y (citation). Y matters here because ___. That still does not settle X, because ___. The blanks are yours. If you cannot fill “still does not settle,” you may have let Y become the paper.

Invented sources, real habit

You will practice on invented lines labeled as invented. Do not treat them as research. Do not invent a school, a journal, or a student to make a citation look live. If you use a real assigned text from another class in the homework, name it honestly. If you do not have one, use the invented lines and say they are invented. Honesty about the source is part of not disappearing. Fabricating a citation is a different course’s integrity case. We will not practice that.

Then close. A compressed 101 does not pretend it was fifteen weeks. It does ask you to stand behind one short argument: a reader, a claim that can be wrong, reasons and evidence, a source that did not eat you, a last paragraph that is not a costume. Look back at Unit 1. If the reader-sentence has changed, that is not failure. That is the course working. Say what changed. That sentence is the portfolio — not a folder of everything you ever wrote, and not a claim that this was taught as a live NC A&T class. It is a short argument you would let a stranger test.

Writing stays a public act at the end. The reader did not have to show up. You still put a claim on the table.

A short close is still a public act

The portfolio close is not a reflection about your feelings as a writer. It is a short argument plus a few sentences on what changed. If you write “I learned a lot about my voice,” you missed the course. If you write “My reader was a director who treated absences as motivation; the claim narrowed from ‘attendance policies are unfair’ to ‘the appeal process is built for rare emergencies, not weekly shifts,’ and I used a source as a mechanism, not as a tour,” you did the work. The second version can be tested. The first cannot.

You may feel that 250–400 words is too small to be college. College is not a word count. College is a claim that survives contact. A compressed 101 that you can finish tired is honest about time. It is not a substitute for a fifteen-week research sequence, and it does not pretend to be. If a live instructor assigns this sample, they will say where the homework goes and which citation style they want. This file will not store your work and will not speak for a registrar.

Last instruction, which is also the first: name the reader. Put the claim where a stranger can find it. Let a source in without leaving the room. End on a cost or a change, not on importance. Then stop. The course is a sample. The habit is not.

Practice

Which use disappears the writer?

Invented source line (not a real article): “Shared grades measure the product, not the coordination” (Ortiz, invented, 2019, p. 14). Claim on the table: when the product is one paper, a shared letter hides the writer.

Pick the paragraph that keeps the writer visible. Then say why the others fail.

Practice

Patch a paragraph that vanished

The writer disappeared. Rewrite so the claim is visible before, during, and after the source. You may paraphrase. Keep the invented citation honest — do not turn Ortiz into a real author.

Ortiz says “shared grades measure the product, not the coordination” (invented, 2019, p. 14). “Collaboration is a learning outcome.” “Fairness is essential in higher education.” In conclusion, sources show that group work is important.

Homework — portfolio close

250–400 words. This is the last object in the sample course.

Write a short argument you would stand behind. Include: (1) the reader-sentence from Unit 1, revised if it changed; (2) a claim that can be wrong; (3) two reasons and one piece of evidence, labeled honestly; (4) one source used as a voice — a real assigned text from another class, named, or the invented Ortiz line, clearly labeled invented; (5) an ending that is not “this is important.” After the argument, 3–5 sentences: what changed from Unit 1 to now (reader, claim, or both). Do not invent a journal, a campus office, or a student. Do not claim this course is official NC A&T curriculum.
