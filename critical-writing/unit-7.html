Unit 7 · Revision · ~40 minutes

Revision is not editing

Re-see the argument. Commas come last. If the claim cannot survive a real objection, changing the claim is the work — not hiding the objection.

3 objectives What to change first 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish revision (claim, reasons, structure, audience) from editing (sentences, commas, spelling).
  2. Change a claim after a counterargument instead of only defending the original sentence.
  3. Cut a paragraph that does not serve the argument and say, in one sentence, what job it failed to do.

Lesson

Re-see, then mop

Editing makes sentences cleaner. Revision asks whether the argument is the right argument. Students reverse the order because editing feels like work you can finish. You can hunt commas until the file is due and never ask whether the thesis is still true. That is how a performed opening survives all the way to a grade. This unit puts the mop down until the house is in the right place.

A revision pass has a sequence. First: who is the reader, and does the draft still speak to them (Unit 1). Second: is the claim still a claim, and should it change (Unit 3). Third: do the reasons fail differently, and does the evidence actually test them (Unit 4). Fourth: does each paragraph do one job (Unit 5). Fifth, and only fifth: sentences. If you start at fifth, you will polish a wrong house.

The objection that should cost you something

In Unit 3 you wrote an honest objection. In revision you let it land. If the objection is right — night-shift is rare in this section; the group-grade paper was three students, not eight — you do not hide it in a footnote. You change the claim. “Rare” might mean you narrow to the students it does hit, and you say so. “Three, not eight” might mean you drop the census costume and argue from a scene. Changing the claim is not weakness. Keeping a claim the evidence cannot carry is the weakness.

A useful line to write in the margin: If this objection is true, my thesis becomes ___. Fill the blank. If the blank is the same thesis with “very” added, you have not revised. You have insisted.

Cutting is a revision move

First-year drafts keep paragraphs that were expensive to write. A story about parking in a paper about group grades. A definition of collaboration copied from a dictionary. A conclusion that restates importance. If a paragraph does not serve the claim you now have — the claim after the objection — it goes. You can keep a sentence in a leftover file. You cannot keep it in the argument out of loyalty to the hour you spent.

When you cut, say the job it failed. “This paragraph labeled a topic.” “This paragraph repeated Reason 1 in adjectives.” “This paragraph was for a reader I am no longer writing to.” That sentence is the revision. The deletion is just the tool.

Editing still matters. A claim a reader cannot parse is not a public act; it is a private code. Do the sentence pass. Do it last. Do not confuse a grammar check with having re-seen the argument. Sample drafts below are invented. They are not student records. Practice deciding what to change first, then rewrite one claim so the objection costs something.

A two-pass method you can finish tired

Pass one is structural and should fit on a notecard. Write: reader; old claim; objection; new claim; list of paragraphs with a job-verb each; one paragraph to cut; one hole in the evidence. Do this before you open the file if you can. If you open the file first, the sentences will hypnotize you into editing. The notecard is rude on purpose. It will not let a comma splice become the afternoon.

Pass two is the file. Change the claim. Move or cut the paragraphs. Fill or honestly keep the hole. Then, and only then, read aloud for sentences that hide the move — stacked prepositional phrases, “important,” a closer that restates the title. Fix those. Run a spell check because a public act that cannot be parsed is not public. Do not confuse the spell check with pass one.

If an instructor ever writes “revise” in the margin and you hear “proofread,” that is this unit’s whole problem in one mishearing. Ask them, or ask yourself: which part of the argument should I re-see. If you cannot get an answer, pick the claim. Re-seeing the claim will drag the rest with it. Re-seeing the commas will drag nothing.

One ethical line, because revision is where people panic: do not add a source you did not read in order to look revised. Do not add a number you do not have. Tightening a claim to fit what you actually possess is revision. Inflating the file is a different act, and Unit 8 will not bless it. The invented drafts in this unit exist so you can practice the decision of what to change first. They are not a bank of facts to paste into your homework.

Practice

What do you change first?

Invented draft notes. Pick the revision move — not the edit — you would do first.

Assigned job: short argument for an instructor who thinks group grades teach collaboration.

Draft claim: Group projects are important and also unfair.

What is in the file: a polished opening about “the modern workplace”; one story about doing all the work; a paragraph defining collaboration from a dictionary; no count; a closer that says the issue should be considered; three comma splices in paragraph two.

Objection on the table: some work in the world is shared, and a campus that never asks students to coordinate is lying about the jobs they are headed toward.

Practice

Let the objection cost something

Original claim: “Group grades always hide the student who did the work.”

Objection: not always — some groups split the pages, and some tasks are genuinely shared (a lab procedure, a built object).

Rewrite the claim so the objection changes it. Do not add “very.” Do not keep “always” and pretend you addressed it.

Homework

200–300 words. A revision memo, then the new claim. Bring Unit 6’s draft with you.

Write a revision memo on your Unit 6 draft (or on Object B from that unit if you did not draft). (1) One sentence: who the reader is. (2) The old claim. (3) One honest objection. (4) The new claim — changed if the objection required it; if you kept the claim, say why the objection did not land. (5) One paragraph you would cut, and the job it failed, in a verb. (6) Two sentence-level edits you are saving for last, named so you do not start with them. Do not turn this memo into a grammar list.
