Unit 5 · Paragraphs · ~35 minutes

Paragraphs that do a job

One move each. A paragraph that tries to claim, prove, concede, and conclude is four unfinished jobs in a coat.

3 objectives Label the job 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Identify the job of a paragraph: claim, evidence, concession, or transition — not “about X.”
  2. Spot a paragraph that is doing two jobs and say where you would split it.
  3. Rewrite a topic sentence that only labels a subject into a sentence that makes a move.

Lesson

One move

A paragraph is not a container for sentences that happen to be about the same noun. It is a move in an argument. One move. If you cannot say the job in a verb — this paragraph claims, this one puts a count on the table, this one gives the other side a point and limits it — the paragraph is a pile. Piles get a passing glance from tired readers because they look like pages. They do not change anyone’s position.

First-year drafts often write one giant paragraph that tries to do everything: open the paper, state a feeling, tell a story, mention a source, and conclude that the issue is important. That is not development. That is panic with indentations. Split it. Give each job its own block. The paper will look shorter and do more.

The topic sentence is a move, not a label

“Another aspect of parking is the visitor lot.” That is a label. It tells a reader you are still on parking. It does not take a position the rest of the paragraph has to earn. A move: “The visitor lot that opens at 10 a.m. functions as a late-arrival policy for 8 a.m. sections.” Now the sentences under it have a job: show the clock, show the empty spaces, show who they are reserved for. If those sentences do not do that job, the topic sentence was a bluff. Good. Now you know what to cut or what to fetch.

Labels feel safe because they cannot be wrong. That is why they are dead. A topic sentence that can be wrong is doing the same work as a thesis, at paragraph scale. Units 3 and 5 are the same habit at two sizes.

Four jobs you will actually use

You do not need twenty paragraph types from a handbook. You need four you can deploy on a Tuesday night:

  • Claim paragraph. States a reason that supports the thesis. The rest of the paragraph is not new claims. It is the because.
  • Evidence paragraph. Puts something a skeptic could check on the table and says what it is supposed to prove. Ends before you wander into a new reason.
  • Concession paragraph. Gives a real point to the other side, then limits it. If you only give the point, you folded. If you never give the point, you are performing confidence.
  • Transition paragraph (usually short). Turns the argument: “If that count is right, the handbook line does not mean what the office thinks it means.” Then stop. The next paragraph does the next job.

A paragraph doing two of those at once needs a split. The test is crude and useful: put a slash where the job changes. If you can put a slash, you can hit return. The new first sentence of the second block has to be a move, not “Also.”

Length is not a virtue. Five sentences that earn a claim beat twenty that circle a noun. If a paragraph is long because you are afraid to start the next one, that is drafting fear (Unit 6), not development. If it is long because you glued two jobs, split it.

When you revise someone else’s paragraph — including the invented one below — do not start with word choice. Ask what job it is trying to do. If you cannot answer, the rewrite starts with a new first sentence, not a synonym for “important.” Sample paragraphs here are invented. They are not student records.

Read your own paragraph as Unit 2

You already practiced naming another writer’s moves. Do that to yourself. Print the paragraph or read it aloud. After each sentence, say the job: claim, reason, evidence, concession, restatement, transition, or dead. If three sentences in a row are restatement, you do not have a long paragraph. You have a short one wearing a costume. Cut two. If the first sentence is a label and the third sentence is the actual claim, promote sentence three. The label can go.

Aloud is not a personality trick. It is a way of hearing the pile. A paragraph that does two jobs will usually change pitch in the middle — from a story to a handbook, from parking to group work. That pitch change is the slash. Hit return there. Write a new first sentence for the second block that is a move. If you cannot, the second block may not belong in this paper. That is a Unit 7 problem arriving early, which is a gift.

Do not decorate the topic sentence with “indeed,” “thus,” or “it is evident that.” Those words do not make a move. They make a nervous writer look dressed. The move is the claim, the clock, the limit on the concession. Put the move in ordinary words. Tired first-year readers — including you, at 11 p.m. — can hear ordinary words. They cannot always hear costume.

Practice

Label the job

Four invented paragraphs. Click one, then pick its job. All four, then check.

No paragraph selected.

Practice

Rewrite a dead topic sentence

Dead: “Another thing about group projects is communication.”

Rewrite it as a move the rest of a paragraph would have to earn. One or two sentences. A classmate should be able to disagree.

Homework

200–300 words. A revision, not a new essay.

Take one paragraph you have already written this term (from Unit 1 or 4 homework, or from another class). Paste the original. Then rewrite it so it does one job. If it was doing two jobs, split it and turn in both new paragraphs. Under the rewrite, one sentence: what job the paragraph now does, in a verb. If you do not have a paragraph of your own, rewrite the mixed paragraph from the practice (the one that needs a split) into two paragraphs, each with a topic sentence that is a move.
