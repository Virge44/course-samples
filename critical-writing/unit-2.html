Unit 2 · Reading as a writer · ~35 minutes

Reading as a writer

Do not mine a text for “what it’s about.” Watch what the other person is doing with the sentences.

3 objectives Mark the moves 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Identify a writer’s claim — a sentence that could be wrong — as distinct from the topic.
  2. Name the move a sentence is making: claim, reason, evidence, concession, definition, or restatement.
  3. Write two sentences of analysis that do not repeat the passage in different words.

Lesson

What the other person is doing

Most first-year reading is hunting. You hunt for the main idea, the three examples, the definition that might be on a quiz. That hunt will get you a summary. It will not get you a paper. Reading as a writer means you watch what the other person is doing with each stretch of language. A writer who defines a term is doing a different job from a writer who concedes a point, or one who plants a reasonable-sounding claim they will later cut down. If you cannot name the move, you can only repeat the content.

Summary says what is there. Analysis says what the writer is doing, and what that doing is for. “The article talks about group projects” is a topic. “The article argues that group grades hide the student who did the work” is a claim. You cannot argue with a topic. You can argue with a claim. That is the whole difference, and first-year papers die on it.

Find the claim first

Before you outline, find the sentence that could be wrong. Not the title. Not the first definition. The claim is the position a skeptical reader would push on. In a news feature it may be buried. In an academic paragraph it often sits near the top or just after a concession. If you cannot point to it with your finger, you do not have it yet. “This is about X” is never it.

A usable test: could a classmate write a sentence that starts “No, because…” and still be talking about the same text? If they could only say “No, it isn’t about that,” you found a topic. If they could say “No, group grades do not hide labor — they force it into the open,” you found a claim.

Moves, not labels for English class

You do not need a long list of rhetorical devices. You need a short list of jobs sentences actually do in college prose:

  • Claim — a position that can be tested.
  • Reason — why the claim would be true, still in the writer’s voice.
  • Evidence — something outside the writer that a skeptic could check: a count, a scene, a quoted line, a procedure.
  • Concession — a point the other side gets, on purpose, before the writer takes it back or limits it.
  • Definition — a term being nailed down so the argument can use it.
  • Restatement — the claim said again with fancier words. Dead weight. First-year drafts are full of it.

Contrast and transition are real jobs too. They get a paragraph from one move to the next. They are not a claim. If a sentence only says “On the other hand,” it has not done work yet. The work is in the next sentence.

When you annotate, do not highlight everything. Highlight the claim in one pass. Then mark two moves that support it and one move that complicates it. If you cannot find a complication, the writer may be performing confidence. Note that. Performing confidence is also a move. It is not the same as having evidence.

This unit’s passage is invented. It is not a published article and not a student’s paper. You are practicing the habit you will use on assigned reading in an actual class: what is the claim, what is the sentence doing, what would you do if you were writing back. Reading as a writer is not a warmer personality. It is a way of refusing to disappear into summary.

Write back, even in the margin

Reading as a writer ends in a sentence you could send back. Not “I agree” and not “this was interesting.” A send-back names the move and asks the next question a skeptic would ask. “You concede that shared work exists, then you limit it to ‘not one letter for four names.’ What would a shared grade be for, then — a built object? a lab procedure? If you do not say, the concession is a gesture.” That is analysis. It uses the passage. It does not recap the passage.

Do this in the margin while you read, in ugly fragments. You are not producing a paragraph yet. You are refusing to let the text wash over you. Later, when you draft, those fragments are the difference between a paper that has met a mind and a paper that has met a highlighter. If the assigned reading is hard, that is not a reason to summarize harder. It is a reason to mark fewer sentences and name them more precisely. Four marked moves beat a page of yellow.

One more refusal: do not outsource the claim-hunt to a classmate’s post or to a tool that will cheerfully invent a thesis the pages do not support. You own the pointing finger. If you cannot point to the sentence, you are not ready to write back. Stay on the page until you can.

Homework

200–300 words. Use a short assigned reading from another class this term if you have one. If you do not, use the invented passage above. Do not summarize the whole thing.

Quote or paraphrase one claim (one or two sentences). Then, for four consecutive sentences in the passage, name the move each one is making (claim, reason, evidence, concession, definition, restatement, transition). End with two sentences of analysis that do not repeat the content: what is the writer doing, and what would a skeptical reader still ask. If you use a real assigned text, name the title and author. If you use the invented passage, say so.
