Unit 6 · Drafting · ~40 minutes

Drafting without performing

Get a bad full draft. A polished first page and no ending is not a draft. It is a performance of being a good student.

3 objectives Diagnose the draft 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish a complete messy draft from a polished fragment that never reaches an ending.
  2. Name what a bad full draft must still include: a claim, at least two reasons, some evidence, and a last paragraph that is not “in conclusion, this is important.”
  3. Produce, under a time box, a full draft you would not show a stranger — and keep it, because revision needs a whole object.

Lesson

The performance

A lot of first-year writers do not have a drafting problem. They have a performing problem. They write the first paragraph as if it will be carved. They hunt a better synonym before they have a second reason. They stall at sentence four because sentence four does not sound like college yet. Two hours later they have a gleaming opening and nothing a reader could disagree with, because the argument never arrived. That object is not a draft. It is an audition.

This unit asks for the opposite: a bad full draft. Bad means unfinished sentences, ugly transitions, a claim you may have to abandon, evidence that is one scene and you said so. Full means it has a start, a middle that tries to prove something, and an ending that is not a costume. You cannot revise a performance of an opening. You can revise a complete messy argument.

What “full” has to contain

Even a bad draft in this course must include four things, in some form:

  1. A claim a classmate could refuse (Unit 3). If you do not have one yet, write a candidate in brackets and keep going. Brackets are allowed. Silence is not.
  2. Two reasons that fail differently (Unit 4). They can be crude. They cannot be the same sentence twice.
  3. Some evidence a skeptic could check, labeled honestly: one scene, one handbook line, one count. If you do not have the count, write [need the tally from Monday] and move. A hole you named is usable. A hole you hid is not.
  4. An ending that does a job: restates the claim in light of the evidence, or names the cost if the reader shrugs (Unit 1), or says what would change your mind. “In conclusion, this issue is important in today’s world” is not an ending. It is a white flag.

Audience does not disappear because the draft is ugly. Put the Unit 1 sentence at the top as a note to yourself: I am writing to __ so that they will __ because if they don’t, __. Then write as if that person is waiting, not as if a judge is scoring your personality.

A time box, not a mood

Set a timer for 25–35 minutes. Write the whole object. Do not go back to sentence one until there is a last paragraph. If you are stuck, write the stuckness into the draft: [I don’t know if the visitor lot numbers are real — inventing would be a lie, so the claim has to rest on the clock for now]. That sentence is more useful to tomorrow-you than a fake statistic. This course does not want invented campus data. Named uncertainty is honest. Fabrication is not drafting; it is a different failure.

You will look at two objects in the practice: one performed fragment and one ugly complete draft. The complete one is the one you can take into Unit 7. The fragment will feel better in the moment. It always does. It cannot be revised at the argument level because there is no argument yet — only a tone.

Homework is the bad full draft. 300–400 words is enough for a short argument in this compressed 101. It is not enough for a research paper, and it is not trying to be. Finish it. Misspell if you have to. Do not perform.

What to do when the timer is running and the claim is thin

You will sit down for the 25 minutes and discover you do not believe your thesis anymore, or you never did. That is not a reason to open a new document and write a polished fragment about the importance of the issue. Write the disbelief into the draft. “Candidate claim: the visitor lot is a late policy. I am not sure that is fair to visitors. Alternate: the lot is a sign the campus priced commuters last. I will run both for two paragraphs and keep the one that still has evidence at minute 20.” That paragraph is ugly and adult. It is also how arguments actually get made, including by people who publish.

Do not chat your way out of the time box. A tool will give you a fluent opening that has no reader and no cost. You already know what that opening looks like. It looks like Object A. If you use a tool at all in a live class, that is your instructor’s policy, not this sample’s. What this unit requires is a whole object in your sentences, with holes named rather than filled with invented counts. Inventing a tally to look complete is not drafting without performing. It is performing completeness. Leave the hole. Unit 7 can decide whether the claim must shrink to fit the evidence you actually have.

When the timer ends, stop. Save the file under a name that says draft, not final. The urge to “just fix the first paragraph” is the performance returning. Let it wait. You now have something a stranger could disagree with. That was the job.

Practice

Which object can you revise?

Two invented drafts, same assignment: a short argument about group grades. Pick which one is a draft — a whole messy argument — and which one is a performance of an opening.

Object A

In today’s academic world, collaboration is a vital component of student success, and group projects have long been a cornerstone of higher education. It is important to consider the various aspects of this issue. Communication is key. Many students feel that fairness is essential. This paper will discuss both sides in order to better understand this complex and thought-provoking topic.

Object B

Claim (rough): a shared letter grade hides the writer of the paper. Reason 1: the product is prose, so the person who can draft becomes the group. Reason 2: the rubric scores the product, not the chasing of missing members. Evidence: in one invented section, three of eight groups said one student drafted every paragraph — treat as a hypothetical count, not campus data. Ending: if the reader (an instructor who likes group work) keeps the shared letter, they should at least grade coordination separately. I would change this claim if a section used individual drafts plus a shared task.

Practice

What must a bad full draft still include?

Check every item a complete messy draft in this course still needs. Leave unchecked anything that is performance, not drafting.

Homework

300–400 words. A bad full draft. Time-box it. Do not polish the first paragraph until the last one exists.

Write a complete short argument on the claim you have been carrying since Unit 1 (or a replacement if that claim died in Unit 3). Include: the Unit 1 reader-sentence as a note at the top; a claim that can be wrong; two reasons; at least one piece of evidence labeled honestly (scene, count, quoted policy — and if it is hypothetical, say so; do not invent campus data as fact); an ending that is not “this is important.” Ugly is allowed. Incomplete argument is not. Turn in the whole object, misspellings included.
