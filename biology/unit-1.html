Unit 1 · The explanation type · ~35 minutes

What counts as a biological explanation

Mechanism, not a tidy made-up reason. Name the parts. Say the interaction. Say what would come out different.

3 objectives Sort + rewrite 1 homework

What you'll do

In this sitting:

  1. Distinguish a tidy made-up reason, a label, and a mechanism that names parts and an interaction.
  2. Name the three pieces a working biological explanation needs: parts, what they do to each other, and a consequence that could be wrong.
  3. Take a sentence that says what something is for (“so that,” “nature wants”) and rewrite it so a classmate could check it by pointing at the same chain.

Lesson

Three kinds of sentence

A tidy made-up reason sounds like an answer and cannot be checked. “Giraffes have long necks so they can reach high leaves.” That sentence names a purpose and stops. A label names a part and stops: “Mitochondria.” A biological explanation names parts, says what those parts do to each other, and says what would come out different if a part failed or a condition changed.

This is a gen-ed habit, not a lab course. You do not need a coat or a protocol. You need to refuse a sentence that only restates the outcome as a reason. “Birds have hollow bones because flight is important” is a poster. It tells you that flying matters and then uses that feeling as a because. “Many flying birds have bones with air spaces tied into the breathing system; that arrangement can lower mass for a given span of bone and is part of how air moves. A heavy-boned flyer, or a hollow-boned bird that does not fly, would pressure a claim that hollowness exists only to make flight possible.” The second version can be refined. The first cannot.

If you skip this unit, later pages become a name dump. If you stay here, you already have the course in miniature.

The three pieces

A working explanation has three pieces you can point at:

  1. Parts at a scale you can hold: a molecule, a membrane, a leaf, a bird, a population. Not “nature.” Not “the body” as a person with plans.
  2. Interactions: what acts on what. Verbs that do work — absorb, bind, block, evaporate, copy — not “wants,” “knows,” or “is designed for.”
  3. A consequence that could be wrong. If nothing could count against the sentence, it is a mood. “Sweat cools you so you don’t die” is a poster. “Sweat on skin evaporates; evaporation takes heat from the skin; if the air is already holding as much water as it can, evaporation slows and cooling fails” is a chain. You can stand in a humid room and feel the difference. That is the standard here: a chain a classmate could check without becoming a biologist.

Watch the verbs. “So that,” “in order to,” “nature designed,” “the body knows” are purpose-talk. After a mechanism is known, people sometimes use purpose as shorthand. Shorthand is not the explanation. This course will keep asking you to unpack it. You are not being picky about style. You are refusing a story that skips the middle.

Where tidy made-up reasons hide

They show up on schedule. “The cheetah is fast because it needs to catch gazelles”: needs is not a mechanism — Unit 5 will make you say variation, selection, time. “We have an appendix because we used to eat leaves”: “because we used to” is not a chain you have shown. “Men are more aggressive because of testosterone”: a hormone can change rates; the sentence skips development, context, and every other signal.

A common costume is a Latin name. “Homeostasis maintains equilibrium.” That is a label plus a mood. Name the variable — temperature, water, sugar — the process that responds, and what happens when the response is blocked. If you cannot, you have a vocabulary word, not an explanation. Another costume is a correlation wearing a lab coat. “People who sleep more get sick less, so sleep boosts immunity.” Maybe. The sentence has not said what sleep does to which cells, or what else differs between the groups. “Boost” is a slogan. Mechanism is a chain.

You will also meet the slogan that everyone already knows. “Mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell.” It does not tell you what is being broken down, what is being captured, or what would happen if a membrane gradient failed. You do not need the full pathway in a gen-ed seat. You need to hear that the slogan has not done the work. Unit 4 will give you a short food-to-work chain. Until then, treat “powerhouse” as a slogan that skips the work.

What this course will not pretend

Subject facts stay conservative. Where a textbook would dump a twenty-step pathway, we keep the chain short so a classmate could check it. Where popular science would say “scientists have found the gene for,” we will say a sequence difference that changes a protein’s shape can change a rate. No genome-editing how-to. No pathogen work. No medical advice. The skill is explanation, not performance of science-sounding nouns.

A protocol sentence or a diagnosis sentence does not belong here. A classmate should be able to say “No, because…” and still be talking about the same chain. If they can only say “No, flight isn’t important,” you still have a tidy made-up reason.

Practice will catch “so that,” “nature wants,” “the body knows,” and “important.” It cannot catch a pretty sentence that still refuses the middle. That part is yours. Stay with the parts. Ask what they do to each other. Ask what would come out different. If you cannot name those three, you have a souvenir, not a biological explanation.

Practice

Which sentence is the mechanism?

Same topic: why many leaves look green. Pick the sentence that names parts, an interaction, and something that could be wrong.

Practice

Rewrite a tidy made-up reason

Tourist version: “Birds have hollow bones so that they can fly.”

Rewrite it as one mechanism. Name parts. Name an interaction. Name a consequence a classmate could check. Avoid “so that,” “in order to,” “nature,” “designed,” “the body knows,” and “important.”

Homework

Copy this out. The page won't keep it. 200–300 words.

Take two sentences you have actually heard — in a class, a documentary, a feed, or your own first draft. One should be a tidy made-up reason or a slogan. Rewrite both as mechanisms: parts, interaction, a consequence that could be wrong. Then write one paragraph: which of your rewrites could a classmate check by pointing at the chain, and which one is still a poster? Do not use “so that,” “nature wants,” “the body knows,” or “important.” No lab steps. No medical advice.
