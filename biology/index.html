Who it is for

First-year college students in a gen-ed science seat, mixed preparation. You do not need to like “science.” You need to stay with one living system long enough to refuse a slogan and name a mechanism a classmate could check.

If you already write fluent theme papers (“life is a balance,” “DNA is the blueprint,” “evolution means the strongest survive”), this course will still slow you down. A slogan is not an explanation. If you freeze at cells and molecules, start with Unit 1. The sequence is the course; the units are short enough to finish tired.

What biology is, here

Biology is a public habit of explanation, not a museum of Latin names. A just-so story restates an outcome as a purpose (“giraffes have long necks so they can reach leaves”). A label names a part and stops (“mitochondria”). A biological explanation names parts, says what they do to each other, and says what would change if a part failed or a condition changed.

Name the parts. Say the interaction. Say what would come out different. If nobody could disagree by pointing at the same chain, you filled a page.

What this course will not do

Conceptual only. No pathogen work. No lab protocols for microbes. No culturing, plating, or “grow your own mold” instructions. No genome-editing how-to. No CRISPR steps. No synthesis, reverse genetics, or enhancement. No medical advice — pulse-after-stairs is an observation of a trade, not a diagnosis. If a prompt could be read as a protocol, skip it and use a household observation you can see without making anything grow.

Course outcomes

By the end of the eight units, you should be able to:

  1. Tell a just-so story from a mechanism, and rewrite a purpose-sentence so it names parts, an interaction, and a checkable consequence.
  2. Treat a cell as a bounded system with jobs and traffic, not a bag of vocabulary words.
  3. Say in plain language what DNA and proteins do — information and work — without a genome-editing fantasy.
  4. Trace energy from food to work as a transformation, not a vibe about “boosting.”
  5. State evolution as a tested claim: variation, selection, time — not “the strongest survive.”
  6. Describe organisms in a real place, with constraints, not a poster about “the balance of nature.”
  7. Read the human body as a set of trades (homeostasis), not a machine that should maximize everything at once.
  8. Write a short evidence note from a public-domain teaching figure or a household observation a classmate could refuse.

Sequence

Semester-shaped, compressed. Do the units in order. Each one is a lesson you can finish in one sitting, a clickable practice with feedback, and one homework you could actually turn in. Total student time if you do the practices: about four to six hours. This is not fifteen weeks of textbook coverage and not a lab course.

How the units work

Each unit has three measurable objectives, lesson prose a tired first-year can finish, clickable practice that talks back, and one homework of about 150–350 words (Unit 8 is the evidence writeup). Practice is not a quiz for a score. It is a move you will need on the homework.

If an instructor is running this async or hybrid, turn homework in where they say — an LMS journal, a shared folder, paper. If you are working alone, write it anyway. The course does not store it.
