Unit 3 · What the molecule does · ~40 minutes

Information: DNA and proteins

Sequence can be copied and used. Proteins do most of the work. Not a blueprint slogan. Not a genome-editing lesson.

3 objectives Sort + rewrite 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Say in roommate English what DNA does: it is a sequence that can be copied and used as a template for messages.
  2. Say what proteins do: they are the cell’s working parts — catalysts, structures, pumps — built in an order set by those messages.
  3. Refuse “blueprint of life,” “the gene for X,” and any sentence that turns this unit into an editing how-to.

What this unit will not do

This is not a guide to changing genomes. No CRISPR steps. No “how to edit.” No synthesis, no reverse genetics, no enhancement talk. If you came here for that, leave. The question is what the molecule does in an ordinary cell, in conservative language.

Lesson

A sequence, not a soul

DNA is a long molecule made of four kinds of units, lined up. The order of those units is a sequence. Cells can copy that sequence when they divide, so daughter cells get a matching lineup. Cells can also use stretches of that sequence as templates for shorter messages (RNA) that leave the DNA behind and go where protein-making happens. That is the conservative core: copy, and use as a template. It is not a spirit in the nucleus. It is not “you.”

“DNA is the blueprint of life” is a tourist plaque. A blueprint is a drawing a builder follows on purpose. DNA does not decide. Enzymes bind, copy, and stall. Some stretches are used often; some almost never; some help with regulation rather than coding a protein. If your paragraph cannot survive without “blueprint,” “code of life,” or “secret of you,” you have a slogan, not an explanation.

A gene, in the modest sense this course will use, is a stretch of sequence that can be used to make a product — usually a protein, sometimes an RNA that has a job of its own. That definition is already a little rough, and biologists argue about edges. For a gen-ed seat it is enough: a gene is not a destiny card. It is a stretch that can be used.

Messages and work

The usual flow in a textbook is written DNA → RNA → protein. Read it as jobs, not as a ritual. DNA stays (in cells that keep it in a nucleus, it stays in that room). A message is made. Ribosomes join amino acids in an order that matches the message. The finished chain folds into a shape. Shape is not decoration. A protein’s shape is how it binds, blocks, or speeds a reaction. Change the sequence, and you may change the shape. Change the shape, and you may change a rate. “May” is doing work in that sentence. Many sequence differences do little. Some change a lot. Popular science likes “the gene for.” This course will not. A trait is usually many stretches, many proteins, and a history in a place.

Proteins are the cell’s working parts. Some speed reactions (enzymes). Some hold structures. Some sit in membranes and move things across (Unit 2’s traffic). Some carry signals. DNA is not the worker in most of those scenes. DNA is the lineup that can be used to make the workers, and that can be copied so the lineup is not lost at division. If you catch yourself saying “DNA does the digesting” or “DNA fights the infection,” you have given the filing cabinet the job of the tools.

Mutation, here, means a change in sequence. It is not a moral. It is not automatically “bad DNA.” A change can do nothing noticeable, can break a protein, or can change a rate in a way that matters in a given environment. Unit 5 will need that last idea: variation has to exist before selection can do anything. You do not need a catalog of mutagens. You need to stop treating mutation as a movie plot.

What “information” means in this room

Information, in this unit, is not a metaphor for meaning. It means: the order of units can be copied with few mistakes, and that order can specify the order of another kind of unit (amino acids). That is already a lot. It is not “DNA tells the story of your ancestors” as a personality test. It is not a reason to buy a kit. It is not permission to talk as if one stretch explained a grade, a crime, or a talent. Those are just-so stories with a double helix drawn on them.

You will also hear that we can now “edit DNA.” That sentence is a headline. This course will not walk you through any method. Stay with what sequence is for in a living cell. If a draft lists tools or “how scientists swap a gene,” tear it up and write what a protein does when a message arrives.

A roommate test

Say this out loud without the plaques: DNA is a sequence the cell can copy. Stretches of it can be used to make messages. Those messages are used to build proteins in a set order. Proteins do most of the chemical and structural work. A change in sequence can change a protein, which can change a rate. That paragraph is the unit. Everything else is either a detail you do not need yet or a story this sample will not tell.

Subject facts stay conservative. We are not mapping pathways. We are not naming diseases as if this were medical school. We are not giving advice about tests, ancestry, or health. If you have a personal reason to care about a medical genetic question, that is a conversation with a clinician, not with this HTML file.

Practice will ask you to pick the sentence that says what the molecule does, and to strip a slogan. Homework is a roommate paragraph. If the roommate could only repeat “blueprint,” you have not done the unit. If the roommate could tell a classmate what is copied, what is built, and who does the work, you have.

Practice

What does the molecule do?

Pick the sentence that stays with copy-and-use, and does not turn into a slogan or an editing fantasy.

Practice

Strip the slogan

Tourist version: “DNA is the blueprint of life that makes you who you are, and scientists can now edit it to improve people.”

Rewrite in roommate English: what DNA does, what proteins do. No blueprint, no “who you are,” no editing, no improvement, no CRISPR, no disease advice.

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words.

Write a roommate paragraph: what DNA does and what proteins do. Use the three pieces from Unit 1 (parts, interaction, a consequence that could be wrong). Then add four sentences you refuse to write — slogans or editing talk you will not use — and say why each is not a mechanism. Do not give any how-to. Do not name a method for changing a genome. Do not give medical or ancestry advice.
