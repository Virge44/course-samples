Unit 2 · A bounded system · ~40 minutes

Cells as systems

A cell is not a bag of vocabulary words. It is a bounded place with jobs that depend on other jobs.

3 objectives Sort + depend 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Describe a cell as a bounded system: an inside, an outside, and a membrane that controls traffic.
  2. Name three conservative jobs (information, energy capture, protein-making) and say how one job depends on another.
  3. Reject a “bag of parts” sentence and rewrite it as a system sentence a classmate could check.

Lesson

Inside, outside, traffic

A cell is the smallest unit that still does the living things this course cares about: it keeps a difference between inside and outside, it uses information, and it transforms energy. Bacteria are cells. Skin cells are cells. A virus is not a cell — it has no metabolism of its own — and this course will not treat viruses as a lab object.

The difference that matters first is the membrane. A membrane is not a wall that never opens. It is a boundary that lets some things through and holds others back. Water, food molecules, waste, and signals do not float in and out as a mood. They cross, or they don’t, because of the membrane’s structure and the proteins sitting in it. If that boundary fails, the inside and the outside become the same place. Then there is no cell. That is a system sentence: a part (the membrane), an interaction (selective traffic), a consequence (the difference collapses).

You do not need a catalog of every protein in the membrane. You need to stop saying “the cell lets things in” as if the cell were a person with keys. The membrane and its proteins do the letting. “The cell wants nutrients” is Unit 1 purpose-talk wearing a smaller costume.

Jobs that depend on jobs

Textbooks love organelle posters. This unit keeps three jobs, named conservatively, because a first-year can hold them:

  1. Information. In cells with a nucleus, most of the DNA sits there. DNA is a long molecule that can be copied and that can be used as a template for messages. The messages leave. The DNA, in the ordinary life of that cell, stays. Unit 3 will say what the molecule does. Here, notice the system fact: information is stored in one compartment and used in another. That is traffic, not a slogan about “the brain of the cell.”
  2. Energy capture. Mitochondria (in the cells that have them) are where a great deal of the energy in food molecules is captured in a form the cell can spend. “Powerhouse” is still a plaque. The system fact is dependence: the reactions that capture that energy need a membrane of their own and a supply of fuel that came from somewhere else. If the fuel does not arrive, the capture slows. Unit 4 will trace food to work.
  3. Protein-making. Ribosomes are the workbenches where amino acids are joined in an order set by a message. Proteins then do much of the cell’s actual work — catalysts, structures, pumps in the membrane. If ribosomes stop, the pumps are not replaced, and traffic fails. Information that is never used to make a product is a file in a locked drawer.

A system is not a list. A list is “nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, Golgi…” A system sentence says that one job fails when another job fails. Protein pumps in the membrane depend on protein-making. Protein-making depends on messages and on energy. Energy capture depends on fuel and on membranes. You can enter that loop at any door. You cannot skip the doors and call the poster a cell.

Not every living thing has the same floor plan

Many bacteria have no nucleus. Their DNA is still there; it is not wrapped in that inner room. Plant cells have chloroplasts as well as mitochondria: they can capture light energy and still run the later food-to-work steps. Animal cells do not. Those differences matter, and they are still system differences — different jobs present, different traffic — not a ranking of which cell is “more advanced.” This course will not tell a progress story about cells. It will tell a dependence story.

A first-year paper often treats “prokaryote” and “eukaryote” as a quiz. Keep the words if you want. Use them as floor plans: with or without the inner room for DNA. If you cannot say what traffic changes when the inner room is absent, you have two souvenirs.

What we will not do with cells

We will not culture cells. We will not give a protocol for growing anything. We will not talk through how to get a gene into a cell. Those are lab and engineering moves. They are outside this sample on purpose. If you have seen a video of cells dividing, you may remember the pictures. The habit here is still Unit 1: what part is doing what to what, and what would fail if it stopped.

A city or factory analogy helps for a minute and becomes a just-so story when the city “wants.” Prefer: the membrane holds a difference; messages move; proteins get made; fuel is spent. If a sentence only works because you gave the cell a mind, strip the mind. If nothing is left, you had a story, not a system.

Practice will ask you to pick the system sentence and to name a dependence. Homework asks you to take one job and write it as a system: what it needs from another job, and what fails if that supply stops. Stay conservative. You do not need twenty organelles. You need one honest loop.

Practice

Which sentence treats the cell as a system?

Pick the sentence that names a boundary or a dependence, not a poster or a mind.

Practice

Name the dependence

If ribosomes stop making proteins, which job fails next in a way this lesson actually claimed — and why?

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 150–250 words.

Pick one cell job from this unit: membrane traffic, information, energy capture, or protein-making. Write it as a system, not a definition. Name the part. Name what it needs from another job. Name what would fail if that supply stopped. Do not list organelles. Do not give the cell a mind. Do not write a lab step. No medical advice.
