Unit 5 · A tested claim · ~40 minutes

Evolution as a tested claim

Variation. Selection. Time. Not “the strongest survive.” Not a just-so origin story.

3 objectives Sort + rewrite 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. State evolution as three pieces: variation in a population, selection in a place, and time for the makeup of the population to change.
  2. Tell a selection claim from a just-so purpose story and from “survival of the strongest.”
  3. Name a kind of evidence (fossils, comparison, or observed change) without inventing a study.

Lesson

Not a belief. A claim with pieces.

Evolution, in the modest sense this gen-ed unit will use, is this claim: the makeup of a population of living things can change across generations because individuals differ, some of those differences can be inherited, and some variants leave more offspring in a given environment than others. That is not a church. It is not a vibe about progress. It is a mechanism with three named pieces: variation, selection, time.

If you skip a piece, you get a cartoon. Variation without selection is a mixed crowd that stays mixed unless something else is going on. Selection without heritable variation is a one-generation story: the winners win, and their children do not keep the trait. Time without the first two is a calendar. “The strongest survive” skips almost everything. Strength is not always what leaves more offspring. A quieter plant that sets more seed in a dry year is not “stronger” in a gym sense. Fitness, in this room, means contribution to the next generation in that place — not a feeling, not a brand.

Darwin’s name will show up in every other textbook. You may use it. You do not need a biography. The public habit is the three pieces, plus the refusal of purpose-talk. “Finches have different beaks so they can eat different seeds” is Unit 1 again. “In a population of finches, beak depth varies and is heritable; in years when only hard seeds are left, birds with deeper beaks leave more offspring; over generations the average depth can shift.” That second sentence can be checked against records. The first cannot.

What has to be true

Variation has to exist. Look at any street of people, any lawn of weeds, any litter of puppies. Individuals are not copies. Some of that difference is environmental (food, weather, accident). Some of it can be passed on because it sits in sequence and in how sequence is used (Unit 3, kept conservative). Selection does not invent the variation. It sorts what is already there, in a place, this generation.

Selection is not a judge with a plan. It is a rate difference. Some variants are eaten less, dry out less, or find mates more often in these conditions. The environment is not a moral. A drought does not “want” deep roots. Deep-rooted plants may leave more seed in that drought. Next year the field can look different. If the rains return, the advantage can shrink. That is why “nature wants” is a lie twice: nature is not a person, and the advantage is local.

Time is not “millions of years” as a magic word. Some changes are slow. Some are fast enough to see in a human life: insects and the chemicals meant to kill them, bacteria and drugs (we will not turn that into a pathogen lab), weeds and a mowed lawn. Deep time matters for the big pattern of descent. This course will not reconstruct the tree of life on one page. It will say: if the three pieces run long enough, populations can become unlike their ancestors. That claim has been tested. It is not a vibe you vote on.

What counts as a test, here

Keep evidence kinds modest and public. Fossils show forms in order through rock that took time to lay down — not a complete film, a set of stills with gaps. Comparative anatomy and sequence similarity are the claim that living things share parts because they share ancestors, not because a designer reused a template in a workshop story. Direct observation is the beak record, the weed that survives the mower, the pest that is no longer stopped by last decade’s spray. You do not need to invent a paper. You need to say which kind of look would pressure the claim.

A just-so story often steals the ending. “Cheetahs are fast because they need to catch gazelles.” Needs is not variation. It is not a count of offspring. It is a purpose taped to a documentary shot. A tested claim would have to say: speed varies; some of that variation is inherited; in this place, faster cats left more surviving cubs; the average moved. If the sentence cannot take those blanks, it is a poster.

This unit will not do human ranking. It will not tell a progress story from “lower” to “higher.” It will not use evolution to excuse a social arrangement. Those moves are just-so stories with a beard. Descent with modification is a biological claim about populations. It is not a permission slip.

Subject facts stay conservative. We will not walk through experimental evolution protocols. We will not tell you how to select microbes in a dish. We will not give a how-to for breeding. If you write a homework that sounds like a lab or a eugenics pamphlet, you have left the course. Stay with a population you can name — weeds in a crack, birds at a feeder, dogs as a historical example of people choosing who breeds — and say variation, selection, time.

Practice will catch “the strongest,” “so that,” and “nature wants.” Homework asks for one example with all three pieces and one sentence of what would count against it. If you cannot name what would count against it, you wrote a belief. This unit asked for a tested claim.

Practice

Which sentence is the tested claim?

Same neighborhood: weeds in a cracked sidewalk. Pick variation + selection + time.

Practice

Rewrite “the strongest survive”

Cartoon: “Evolution means the strongest survive, so cheetahs got fast so they could catch gazelles.”

Rewrite with the three pieces. No strongest, no so-that, no nature-wants, no human ranking.

Homework

Copy this out. The page won't keep it. 200–300 words.

Pick one population you can actually point to or that is a public, ordinary example (weeds in a crack, birds at a feeder, a historical dog breed — not a made-up study). Write variation, selection, and time in three short paragraphs. Then write one sentence: what observation would count against your selection claim? Do not write “the strongest survive.” Do not write a breeding or microbe protocol. Do not rank people.
