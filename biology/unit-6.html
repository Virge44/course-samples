Unit 6 · A place is not a backdrop · ~35 minutes

Ecology: organisms in a place

A lawn is constraints and relationships. Not a poster about “the balance of nature.”

3 objectives Sort + look 1 homework

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Describe a real place as living things plus nonliving conditions (light, water, surface, disturbance) — not scenery.
  2. Name one relationship (eating, competing, sheltering) and one constraint that would change who can stay.
  3. Refuse “the balance of nature,” “the ecosystem wants,” and a food-web poster with no place attached.

Lesson

A place is a set of conditions

Ecology, in this compressed 101, is the study of organisms in a place — who is there, what they take and give, what limits them. A place is not a backdrop for a nature documentary. It is light, water, temperature, a surface, and a history of disturbance (a mower, a flood, a parking lot). The same plant is a different problem on a riverbank and in a sidewalk crack. If you cannot name the conditions, you are still looking at a poster labeled “habitat.”

Start smaller than “the environment.” The environment is a cloud. A campus lawn, a gutter, a feeder by a window, a vacant lot — those you can stand in. Who is there? Grass, a dandelion, a sparrow, an ant, a person who decides when to mow. What is nonliving and still doing work? Sun angle. Last night’s rain. Compacted soil. A brick that holds heat. That list is already an ecological description. It does not need a biome name to be honest.

Scale is a choice, not a virtue. A puddle is a place. A forest is a place. This course prefers the scale you can check this week. If you write “the Amazon” and you are sitting in Greensboro, you are probably writing a civic slogan. You may care about the Amazon. Care is not a substitute for a look.

Relationships, not a peace treaty

“The balance of nature” is the tourist plaque of this unit. Places are not still. Populations rise and fall. A wet year makes a pulse of insects; birds have an easier week; a dry year reverses it. A mower is a regular catastrophe for anything that flowers tall. That is not balance. That is a constraint with a schedule.

Three relationships are enough for a first-year paragraph:

  1. Eating. Energy moves (Unit 4) when one organism takes another’s molecules. A sparrow at a feeder is eating seed someone poured. A sparrow on the lawn may be eating insects. Name who takes what. “The food chain” without names is a diagram from a binder.
  2. Competing. Two plants using the same patch of light and water cannot both have all of it. Competition is not a personality. It is a shared limit. The dandelion and the grass are in a relationship even if nobody is biting anybody.
  3. Sheltering / using structure. A bird uses a hedge. An insect uses the underside of a leaf. A person uses the lawn as a path. Structure is a condition other organisms make.

You will meet the word niche. Keep it modest: the range of conditions a species can persist in, and the ways it uses resources, not a job title assigned by nature. “The bee’s niche is to pollinate so flowers can thrive” is purpose-talk. Bees take nectar and pollen; some plants get pollen moved as a side effect of that taking. Both can be true. Only one is a mechanism of the bee’s living.

What “the ecosystem” is not

An ecosystem is a word for the living plus the nonliving in a bounded place, and the transfers among them. It is not a person. It does not want. It does not get angry. It does not “seek balance.” Those sentences show up in first-year papers and in ads for products. Strip them. Ask: who is taking what, and what happens if a condition changes?

People are not outside the place. A feeder is an ecological object. A mower is an ecological object. A pesticide on a lawn is a condition (this course will not tell you how to apply one). If you write ecology as “nature over there, humans over here,” you have made a museum diorama. The gen-ed question is closer: what is this patch doing, and who is in it, including the person who set the schedule?

We will not turn this into a field-lab protocol. Do not collect organisms for this course. Do not trap. Do not culture anything from soil or water. Look, count roughly if you can do it without taking, and leave the place as you found it. Unit 8 will let you use a bird at a feeder or this teaching figure. The same safety holds here.

Practice will ask you to reject the balance plaque. Homework is a real place you can see this week — even from a window. Name two organisms, one nonliving condition, one relationship, and one change that would push someone out. If you cannot walk far, a window box or a parking-lot tree is enough. The honesty of the look is the grade, not the scenery.

Practice

Which sentence is ecology, here?

Pick the sentence that names a place, a relationship, and a constraint — not a peace treaty.

Practice

Rewrite the balance plaque

Tourist version: “The ecosystem is in perfect balance and every species has a job so that nature can thrive.”

Rewrite as a place: who is there, what they take, what limits them. No balance, no job assigned by nature, no “thrive.”

Homework

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 200–300 words.

Describe a place you can actually see this week (window is allowed). Name two organisms, one nonliving condition, one relationship (eat / compete / use structure), and one change that would make it harder for someone to stay (a dry week, a mow, a feeder going empty). Do not collect, trap, or culture. Do not write “balance of nature.” Do not give the ecosystem a mind.
