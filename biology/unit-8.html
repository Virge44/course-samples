Unit 8 · The close · ~45 minutes

A short evidence writeup

A claim, a look, and what would count against it. A feeder, a teaching figure, or stairs you already climb. Not a lab.

3 objectives Sort + draft 1 writeup

Look. Do not culture. Do not diagnose.

Allowed: a bird at a feeder you already have; mold that already appeared on kitchen bread, seen through a closed bag if possible — do not sniff, do not open a science project, do not grow mold on purpose; pulse counted after stairs you already use, not as a health test; or the teaching figure on this page. Not allowed: growing anything, plating, incubating, opening moldy bags to inhale, trapping, collecting, genome talk, medical claims. If mold is a bad idea for you, skip it. The feeder and the figure are enough.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

  1. Write a claim a classmate could refuse (a mechanism or a relationship, not a slogan).
  2. Attach one honest look: household observation or the teaching figure — with time, place, and what you actually saw.
  3. Name what would count against the claim, and refuse a protocol dressed as a project.

Lesson

Evidence, at this scale

This course is not a research lab and not a journal. Evidence, here, means: you made a claim that can be wrong, you pointed at something a stranger could in principle see, and you said what would make you back down. That is the same public habit as Unit 1, now with a look attached. A just-so story cannot take a look. “Birds come to feeders because they need us” is a purpose. “Between 7 and 7:20 on Tuesday I counted four landings on the left perch and one on the right; if the seed on the left was not the reason, swapping the seed and seeing the counts hold would pressure my claim” is a writeup. It can be small. It cannot be a poster.

You have three honest sources. First, a household look you do not have to invent: a feeder, a window tree, mold that already happened on bread in a kitchen, stairs you already climb. Second, the original teaching figure on this page (a feeder sketch with two perches). Third, if an instructor later adds a clearly public-domain plate, they must label it and you must still write a claim, not a caption of awe. This sample does not send you hunting copyrighted textbook art.

What you saw is not what you wished. “I observed that nature is connected” is a mood. “I observed a sparrow take seed, then leave when a larger bird landed” is a look. Time and place make the look checkable. If you cannot say when and where, you have a memory of a documentary, not evidence for this page.

The three-part writeup

Keep the paper short. 250–400 words is enough if every sentence does a job.

  1. Claim. One sentence that could be false. Use the unit muscles: a mechanism (parts, interaction, consequence), a selection sentence with variation and a place, a trade (keep / spend), or an ecological relationship. Not “birds are important.” Not “mold means the kitchen is dirty” as a moral.
  2. Look. What you saw, counted, or pointed at in the figure. Include a time or a condition (after stairs, after rain, left perch vs right). If you use the figure, say which marks you are treating as data and remember it is a teaching sketch, not a field photograph.
  3. What would count against it. One honest reversal. If the counts did not flip when you swapped seed, your perch claim is weaker. If pulse did not rise after stairs, your “reallocation on this climb” claim is weaker — and that is not a diagnosis of health. If the figure’s arrows are the only reason you believe a taking happened, you have a diagram, not a look; say so.

Bread mold, if you use it: you are looking at spots that already appeared on bread someone meant to eat. You are not starting a culture. You do not open the bag to sniff. You do not write how to grow more. A conservative claim is about appearance and condition — “spots spread faster on the slice that stayed closed in a warm spot” — only if you already had two slices in different ordinary kitchen conditions, not because you designed an incubator. If you did not already have that, do not create it. Use the feeder or the figure.

Pulse after stairs, if you use it: count beats at the wrist or neck for 15 seconds and multiply by four, sitting, then after a climb you already do. Write it as Unit 7’s trade: more spending on movement and supply. Do not compare yourself to a chart. Do not tell a classmate they are unhealthy. Do not start a training plan for a grade.

What will fail this close

A theme paper (“this shows the web of life”). A protocol (“I will leave bread in a dark drawer for ten days”). A diagnosis. A copied abstract. A claim with no look. A look with no claim (“I watched birds. It was nice.”). An invented study (“researchers at a famous lab found…”). This sample would rather have a plain twenty-minute feeder count than a fake citation.

You are not being asked to prove evolution or photosynthesis from a windowsill. You are being asked to practice the only scientific habit a gen-ed 101 can actually grade without a lab: say something that could be wrong, point at a look, and name the refusal. That is enough to close the course. It is also enough to show, on the design side, that a 101 can end in a turn-in instead of a slogan.

This folder remains an instructional-design sample. The author did not teach biology as a content expert. Subject facts stayed conservative on purpose. If you take one sentence out of here into the world, take this: a because a classmate can check is worth more than a Latin name you cannot cash.

Practice will ask you to pick the writeup that has all three parts. Homework is the writeup itself. Copy it out. The page will not store it.

Practice

Check a draft for costumes

Paste a few sentences of your writeup. This page can only catch slogans, protocols, and clinic talk. It cannot grade whether you actually looked.

Homework — the evidence writeup

Turn this in if an instructor is running the course. If you are working alone, write it anyway. 250–400 words.

Write a short evidence note. (1) One claim that could be false. (2) One look: bird at a feeder, mold that already appeared on bread (through a closed bag; do not sniff; do not grow it), pulse after stairs you already climb (not a diagnosis), or the teaching figure on this page. Include time or condition. (3) What would count against the claim. Do not write a protocol. Do not invent a study. Do not give medical advice. Do not collect or culture.
